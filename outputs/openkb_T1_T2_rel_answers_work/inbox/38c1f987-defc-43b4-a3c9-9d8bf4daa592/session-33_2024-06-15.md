**Assistant:** You can definitely get better at answering those items without drifting into “test‑gaming,” but the key is to stay in the territory of understanding rather than strategizing.

Most people run into trouble when they react to the *idea* behind a question instead of the exact wording. Forced‑choice items are usually trying to capture how you tend to behave **most of the time**, not what you’re capable of doing or what you think you *should* do. The safest way to answer them is to pause just long enough to picture what you typically do in a normal week, then choose the option that matches that picture, even if neither option feels perfect.

When scales “flip,” it usually just means you had been interpreting the prompts through your own personal definitions. Once the psychologist unpacked what the test meant by certain words, you stopped substituting your own meanings and your answers lined up differently. That doesn’t imply anything pathological. It can mean you’re more situational than you realized, or simply that the test’s language didn’t match how you normally think about yourself.

A simple guiding thought is to read each item literally, think about how you behave in everyday life rather than in exceptions, and then go with your first accurate impression rather than your first emotional reaction. If you do that, you’ll stay honest and consistent without overthinking it.

Here are a few made‑up examples to show how to read the wording literally, not strategically:

If an item says “I usually start tasks right away” versus “I usually wait until I’m ready,” the key word is “usually.” It isn’t asking whether you *can* start right away or whether you *should* wait; it’s asking what tends to happen on an average Tuesday.
