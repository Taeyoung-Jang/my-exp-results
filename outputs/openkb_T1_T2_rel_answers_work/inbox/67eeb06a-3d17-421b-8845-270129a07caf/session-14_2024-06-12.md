# Conversation session 14

Date: 2024-06-12 10:00

**User:** I’ve been getting a lot of invites to professional networking mixers and “community meet-and-greet” events lately, and I’m torn about whether they’re actually worth the time. How do you decide which ones to go to, and what do you do to make them feel less awkward or more meaningful?

**Assistant:** I usually think about two things: who’s likely to be there and what state of mind I’m in. If the event attracts people I genuinely want to learn from or collaborate with, it’s more likely to be worth the time. If it feels like something I’m only considering out of obligation, I skip it. Your energy matters too; when you’re already stretched thin, even a good event won’t feel productive.

Once you’re there, it helps to give yourself a simple purpose, like meeting one interesting person or having one real conversation instead of trying to “work the room.” Asking people what brought them to the event or what they’re working on now tends to open things up without feeling forced. And it’s fine to leave once you’ve hit your personal goal—there’s no prize for staying the whole time.

If you walk away with one useful connection or even just a conversation that felt genuine, that’s usually enough to call it a win.

**User:** Honestly I’m leaning toward skipping most of them unless there’s a clear mission or cause behind it. I’d rather spend my limited free time with groups doing tangible work that aligns with my values than doing small talk for its own sake. 

How would you screen invites quickly—like what signals tell you it’s more substance than “business card swapping”? And if I do go, what’s a graceful way to steer conversations toward something real (or exit without feeling rude)?

**Assistant:** I think you can screen them pretty fast by looking for signs that the event has an actual purpose beyond mingling. If the invite clearly describes a topic, a project, or a shared goal instead of vague phrases like “networking opportunities,” that’s a good sign. Seeing a specific agenda, named speakers, or organizations you respect involved usually means there will be substance. If all you see is a catchy title and “light refreshments,” it’s probably a business‑card shuffle.

If you do end up going, you can nudge conversations deeper just by asking questions that aren’t the usual “So what do you do?” Things like what they care about in their work, what they’re trying to solve, or what drew them to the event. Most people actually like being given permission to talk about something real.

For leaving, a simple “I’m going to say hi to a couple more people before I head out, but it was really nice talking with you” is usually enough. It signals you’re moving on without making it awkward, and no one takes it personally.

**User:** I typically skip those mixers; I prefer dedicating my free time to organizations that align with my progressive values. Given that, what are a few quick “green flags” in an invite that suggest it’s actually mission-driven (not just networking), and how would you politely decline or bow out early without burning bridges?

**Assistant:** If an invite is genuinely mission‑driven, it usually shows its hand right away. You’ll see a clear description of what the group is trying to accomplish, not just who might show up. There’s often a stated purpose, a project update, a speaker tied to a real issue, or a partner organization that already does work you respect. When an invite leads with the cause rather than “come network,” that’s usually the signal you’re looking for.

If you want to decline, something simple like “Thanks so much for the invitation; the timing doesn’t work for me, but I appreciate being included” keeps things warm without committing you to future events. If you’re at an event and want to slip out early, you can say, “I’m going to head out, but I’m glad I could stop by for a bit.” It’s polite, it doesn’t invite follow‑up questions, and it keeps the connection intact.

**User:** Can you give me a few concrete decline templates for different situations—like (1) a generic corporate mixer, (2) a “community” event that’s vague on purpose, and (3) something mission-driven but not aligned with my priorities right now? Also, if I do attend and want to leave early, what’s a good one-liner plus a follow-up text/email to send afterward to keep the relationship positive?

**Assistant:** Here are some short templates you can drop into emails or messages, written as separate examples rather than a list.

For a generic corporate mixer, you could say something like: “Thanks for thinking of me. I’m focusing my time elsewhere right now, so I won’t be able to attend, but I appreciate the invite.”

For a “community” event that doesn’t spell out its purpose, something like: “I’m trying to be intentional about the events I commit to, and I’m not able to make this one. Please keep me posted on future projects or gatherings with a clearer mission.”

For something mission‑driven but not aligned with your priorities at the moment, you might say: “I’m glad you reached out. The work you’re doing matters, but I’m concentrating my volunteer time on a specific set of issues right now, so I won’t be able to join. Wishing you a great event.”

If you attend and want to leave early, a simple line works fine in person: “I’m going to slip out, but it was really nice talking with you.”

A quick follow‑up afterward can smooth everything over: “Great meeting you last night. Thanks again for the invitation—I’m glad I could stop by, even briefly.”
