# Conversation session 39

**User:** I’ve been trying to get more adventurous with food lately, but I keep falling back on the same safe choices. If I wanted to start exploring unfamiliar cuisines without wasting money or getting overwhelmed, how would you recommend I go about it—any strategies for picking dishes, asking questions at restaurants, or finding good spots?

**Assistant:** You can ease into new cuisines without making it feel risky by approaching it a bit like sampling rather than committing. If you’re at a restaurant, start with one unfamiliar dish alongside something you already like, and ask the server what the most “approachable” option is for someone new to that cuisine. Staff are usually great at steering you toward dishes with familiar flavors or letting you know if something is especially spicy or unusual.

Trying places that offer small plates, lunch specials, or tasting portions makes it easier to explore without spending much. Markets, food halls, and food trucks are also good because you can try one item without a full sit‑down meal. When choosing restaurants, look for spots with a steady crowd and a menu that explains dishes clearly or includes photos—both help you avoid surprises.

If you want to dip a toe in at home, pick one ingredient or spice from a cuisine and build a simple dish around it. That way you get the flavor without investing in a complicated recipe.

**User:** Ever since I started that little “try-one-new-thing-a-week” challenge, I’ve been making myself pick whatever I can’t pronounce without laughing—last night it was some fermented bean stew from a corner spot, and I’m still thinking about it. If I want to keep that momentum but avoid ordering something way too intense by accident, what are the best quick questions to ask (about flavor, funkiness, spice, texture), and are there certain types of restaurants/menus where this approach works especially well?

**Assistant:** You can keep the fun of the “mystery pick” without accidentally jumping into the deep end just by asking a couple of simple, non‑awkward questions. Something like, “Is this mild or strong in flavor?” or “How spicy is it compared to what most people expect?” gives the server an easy way to gauge your comfort level. Asking, “What’s the texture like?” or “Is it more savory or more funky?” also helps because those are the things that usually catch people off guard.

This approach tends to work best in places where staff are used to guiding newcomers—family‑run spots with regulars, casual restaurants with friendly service, or anywhere with a menu that assumes some dishes will be unfamiliar. Food halls, small-plate restaurants, and places with tasting menus can be great too, since portions are smaller and it’s cheaper to experiment. As long as you show genuine curiosity, most servers love helping you find something adventurous without it feeling like a dare.

**User:** Could you give me a short “script” I can use when I’m at the counter or table—like 3–5 quick questions in order—that helps me land on something adventurous-but-not-punishing? Also, if the menu’s not translated well (or there are no descriptions), what are the best cues to look for so I’m not blindly rolling the dice?

**Assistant:** You can keep it simple and conversational, almost like a little routine. You might start with, “If I’m new to this dish, is the flavor on the mild side or pretty bold?” Follow it with, “How spicy is it compared to what most people expect here?” Then ask, “What’s the texture like?” and, if you want one more, “Is there anything about it that surprises people trying it for the first time?” That usually gives you a clear sense of where it lands on the adventurous scale.

If the menu isn’t translated well, the safest cues are how the staff react when you point to something—if they pause or ask whether you like strong flavors, that’s a hint it might be intense. Looking for dishes listed as house specials, signature items, or anything you see going to multiple tables is also a good compass; those tend to be flavorful but not extreme.

**User:** That script is perfect—thank you. One more thing: when you say “bold” vs “funky,” can you help me calibrate that? Like, what words should I listen for (or avoid) if I want “interesting and fermented” but not “hits you in the face”? And if I’m pointing at a dish and they answer with something vague, what’s a good follow-up that gets a real answer without sounding picky?

**Assistant:** When people say “bold,” they usually mean strong seasoning, big spices, or a rich flavor that’s still pretty friendly. “Funky” usually points to fermented, earthy, or slightly sour notes. If you want the pleasant, interesting kind of funk but not the intensity, listen for words like “light,” “balanced,” “mildly fermented,” “a little tangy,” or “not too strong.” Words that signal you might be stepping into face‑punch territory are “pungent,” “very fermented,” “sharp,” “strong aroma,” or “acquired taste.”

If the answer you get is vague, something gentle like, “If you were recommending this to someone trying this cuisine for the first time, would you say it’s an easy one or more of a deeper cut?” usually gets an honest, practical response without sounding fussy.

**User:** If they say “acquired taste” or “strong aroma,” what are a couple of safer pivots I can make on the spot—like, “Okay, then what’s the closest thing to this but gentler?” Also, are there a few fermented/aged flavors that tend to be *beginner-friendly* across cuisines (like miso vs. fish sauce vs. stinky tofu), so I can recognize the “good funk” lane?

**Assistant:** If they drop phrases like “acquired taste” or “strong aroma,” you can pivot smoothly by saying something like, “Got it—what’s the gentler cousin of that?” or “Is there something similar that still has that nice depth but isn’t quite as intense?” That keeps the vibe curious rather than picky.

Beginner‑friendly fermented flavors tend to be the ones that add warmth or savoriness without hitting you with a sharp smell. Miso is usually an easy entry point, as is mild kimchi, yogurt‑based tang, or dishes that use fish sauce in the background rather than front and center. Fermented bean pastes in general often sit in that friendly middle zone, too. When you hear descriptions like “smooth,” “savory,” “round,” or “subtle,” you’re usually in the good‑funk lane rather than the stinky‑sock lane.

**User:** Could you give me 5–6 “gentler cousin” examples I can use across different cuisines—like, if a menu item is centered on stinky tofu/fermented shrimp paste/natto/very aged cheese, what are the nearby dishes or ingredients that still scratch the fermented itch but are less intense? And if you can, include what to say in the moment (a one-liner pivot) for each.

**Assistant:** You can think of them as little pairs you keep in your back pocket. If someone warns you about stinky tofu, you can pivot to something like a mild fermented bean curd or a braised tofu dish that uses the same seasonings without the punch, and say, “Is there a version of this with the same flavors but a softer aroma?”

If it’s fermented shrimp paste that’s the culprit, dishes where the paste is blended into a curry or sauce are usually gentler. You can ask, “Is there a dish where that flavor is more in the background?”  

If natto feels like too much, miso‑based soups or stews give you the savory funk without the sticky texture. A good line is, “What’s the miso‑leaning option that gives me the depth without the intensity?”

If very aged cheese is too strong, fresher varieties or melted versions often hit the same notes more softly. You can say, “Is there something with similar richness but milder on the nose?”

If strong fish sauce is front and center, look for dishes where it’s part of a balanced dressing or broth instead of the star. The quick pivot is, “Is there a dish where the fish sauce is more of a seasoning than a main flavor?”

If the menu leans on hardcore fermented beans (like very aged doubanjiang), gentler soybean pastes or stews with a lighter fermentation profile keep the funk without the bite. You can ask, “What’s the smoother, less sharp sibling of this flavor?”

Those cues usually get you to the “interesting funk” zone without slipping into the deep end.
