# Conversation session 11

**User:** I’m trying to get better at choosing what to order at restaurants—especially when the menu has a mix of “safe bets” and rotating specials. How do you decide what’s worth trying, and do you have any go-to strategies for ordering something you’ll actually enjoy?

**Assistant:** I usually think about a few things, and it becomes a quick mental check rather than a long process. If the place is known for something specific—like a signature dish, a style of cuisine, or something you see coming out of the kitchen a lot—that’s almost always the safest choice. Specials can be great, but they’re at their best when they highlight seasonal ingredients or something the chef seems excited about, not when they feel like a way to use up odds and ends.

I also pay attention to the vibe of the restaurant. A busy, confident‑looking kitchen usually means you can take a risk. If things look a little chaotic or slow, sticking to simpler dishes is usually smarter.

When in doubt, I ask the server what they personally like. Not “what’s good,” but “what do you actually order here?” You can learn a lot from the hesitation or enthusiasm in their voice.

And finally, think about what you’re genuinely in the mood for. Even a fantastic dish can fall flat if it’s not what you want that day. Mixing what the place does well with what you’re craving is usually the sweet spot.

**User:** That makes sense. If I’m at a place where I already have a “default” order I love, how do you decide when it’s actually worth deviating from it—like trying a special—without risking disappointment?

**Assistant:** I’d treat your default order like a home base rather than a rule. If a special sounds interesting but you’re nervous about skipping your favorite, I’d look for a clear reason to switch—something like a seasonal ingredient you love, a flavor combo that really matches your taste, or a dish that seems aligned with what the restaurant is already good at.

It also helps to check how bold the special is. If it’s just a twist on something you already like, it’s a smaller gamble than a totally unfamiliar dish. And if the server lights up when you ask about it, that’s usually a good sign.

If none of that happens, stick with your default. You’re not missing out if the special doesn’t give you a good reason to stray.

**User:** Got it. One more thing: when you ask the server about a special, what specific questions do you use to figure out if it’s genuinely good vs. just being pushed?

**Assistant:** I’d keep it casual and ask things that nudge the server into giving a real opinion rather than a sales pitch. Something like, “Have you tried it yourself?” is great, because you can tell a lot from their reaction. If they haven’t, you can follow with, “How are people liking it so far?” which gives you a sense of whether it’s actually landing well.

You can also ask, “What’s the story behind this one?” If they can explain why the chef put it on the menu—seasonal ingredients, something they’ve been working on, a dish they’re proud of—that’s a good sign. If the explanation feels vague or rushed, that usually means it’s not a standout.

Most of the time, the tone of their answer tells you more than the words. Enthusiasm is hard to fake.

**User:** I always order the grilled salmon at this place, even when the daily specials look tempting. Given that, what would you ask the server specifically to judge whether today’s special is worth skipping my usual, and are there any “red flag” answers that would make you stick with the salmon?

**Assistant:** I’d keep it simple and focused on what you already know you like. I’d ask the server, “Have you tried the special, and how does it compare to the dishes the kitchen is best at?” That gets you a real comparison instead of hype. I’d also ask, “What makes this special something the chef wanted to run today?” because a good special usually has a clear reason to exist.

If their answers get vague—like they haven’t tasted it, they don’t know much about it, or they say something generic like “people seem to like it”—that’s usually when I’d stick with the salmon. If they hesitate, look unsure, or can’t describe the dish in a way that sounds intentional, that’s another sign your default is probably the better choice. Enthusiasm and specifics are what you’re listening for; anything less and the salmon wins.
