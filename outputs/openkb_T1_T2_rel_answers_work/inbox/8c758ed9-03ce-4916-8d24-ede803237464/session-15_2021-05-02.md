**User:** I’ve been thinking about Easter traditions lately—how different families and cultures celebrate it in their own ways. What are some interesting Easter customs around the world, and do you have any ideas for meaningful (but not overly religious) ways to mark the holiday at home?

**Assistant:** Easter ends up looking very different depending on where you are. In Sweden and Finland, kids dress up as little witches and go door‑to‑door with drawings in exchange for sweets, almost like a springtime Halloween. In parts of Greece, people dye eggs a deep red and crack them against each other for good luck. In Bermuda, it’s all about making and flying homemade kites. And in Australia, the Easter Bunny is sometimes replaced by the Easter Bilby, a native marsupial, to raise awareness for wildlife conservation.

If you want something meaningful at home without leaning too hard into religious themes, you could focus on the holiday’s general themes of renewal and connection. You might spend time outdoors and make it a small tradition to plant something new each spring. You could cook a special meal using seasonal ingredients, or try a family recipe that only comes out this time of year. Some people like sharing small notes of appreciation or hopes for the season ahead, almost like a gentle reset for the coming months. Even a simple egg‑decorating session, especially if you personalize the designs with family memories or aspirations, can feel warm and intentional without being overtly religious.

In the days leading up, you could set aside a little time each evening for something simple that marks the shift in season: a short walk at dusk, refreshing a corner of your home with flowers or greenery, or doing a light “spring reset” like clearing out one drawer or closet. Some people enjoy natural egg‑dyeing with onion skins, beets, or red cabbage during this week—it nods to tradition without feeling strictly religious, and the process itself is calming.

On the day itself, you might make a special brunch or dinner that uses early‑spring produce, or prepare one dish that connects to your family background. If you enjoy exchanging small gifts or notes, you could write a blessing or wish for the season that focuses on renewal, health, or connection rather than faith. And since Orthodox Easter often lands when the weather is finally starting to turn, spending part of the day outside—planting something, sharing a picnic, or taking a slow walk—can make the holiday feel grounded and meaningful without leaning heavily on religious ritual.
