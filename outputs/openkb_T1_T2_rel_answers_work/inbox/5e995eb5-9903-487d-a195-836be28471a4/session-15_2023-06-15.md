**User:** I’m trying to figure out a lightweight setup for video editing and maybe some basic 3D stuff without my computer melting down — I’m working with a machine that’s a few generations old, so it lacks the computing power needed for high-intensity applications. Can you help me pick software and settings that’ll still run smoothly, and maybe suggest a workflow (like proxies, render settings, etc.) that makes the most of limited hardware?

**Assistant:** You can absolutely get workable performance on older hardware if you choose the right software and keep your workflow “light.” I’ll keep this straightforward and practical.

For video editing, go with something that’s efficient rather than feature‑heavy. DaVinci Resolve runs surprisingly well on older systems as long as you rely on proxy media, but if your GPU is very weak, Kdenlive or Shotcut usually feel smoother because they’re less demanding. Resolve is still the best option if you can run it, though, because its proxy workflow is excellent.

For 3D work, stick to simple modeling or animation in Blender and avoid heavy viewport effects. Eevee is usually fine; Cycles will choke unless you keep samples extremely low.

The biggest performance win is building your workflow around proxies. Convert all footage to low‑resolution, low‑bitrate proxies before you start editing. Something like 720p or even 540p ProRes Proxy or DNxHR LB works well because they’re easy for the CPU to decode. Edit using those, then switch back to full media only for final export.

In the timeline, keep playback resolution low, disable unnecessary effects until the final pass, and consider pre‑rendering only the heavy sections. For exports, choose a sane bitrate and avoid max‑quality settings unless absolutely needed, because the last few percent of quality cost most of the render time.

If you’re doing any 3D elements for the video, render them at a modest resolution and upscale later if necessary. Keeping samples low with denoising turned on helps a lot on weak hardware.

**User:** Can you give me step-by-step proxy setup instructions for Shotcut (and Kdenlive as a backup), including exactly what proxy codec/resolution to pick for 1080p60 phone footage, and what timeline FPS I should use so I don’t get stutters or audio drift? Also, any Windows 10 tweaks (cache location, drive choice, background apps) that noticeably help on 8GB RAM?

**Assistant:** Here’s the clearest way to walk you through it without formal lists, but still in an easy step‑by‑step flow.

For Shotcut, start by opening the Settings menu and enabling the Proxy feature. Once it’s on, open the Proxy Settings panel and choose ProRes Proxy as the format because it’s very light on the CPU. Set the proxy resolution to about 720p while keeping the frame rate exactly the same as your source, so 59.94 or 60fps. After that, import your clips normally; Shotcut will generate proxies automatically, and your timeline should feel far smoother.

Keep your project set to 1080p at the same frame rate as your footage. Matching the frame rate is what prevents audio drift and stutters, especially on phone footage that’s often variable‑frame‑rate under the hood. When editing, set the preview scaling to half or quarter quality if the timeline still feels heavy.

For Kdenlive, the idea is almost identical. Turn on Proxy Clips in the Project Settings area. Choose a proxy profile using either DNxHR LB or ProRes Proxy and change the proxy resolution to 720p. Again, match frame rate to the source footage. Kdenlive will make the proxy files the moment you add the clips, and playback should smooth out right away.

On Windows 10 with 8GB of RAM, you can get a bit more breathing room by placing your project files, proxies, and cache on your fastest drive, ideally an SSD. In both editors you can point the cache or proxy folder to that SSD in the preferences. Closing apps like browsers, game launchers, and chat clients helps more than people expect, because even a small background RAM load puts pressure on the system when you’re already at 8GB. Turning off Windows’ real‑time preview enhancements like transparency effects in the system personalization settings can also give a small but noticeable reduction in GPU overhead.

**User:** In Shotcut, where exactly do I enable proxies and choose ProRes Proxy + 720p? I’m not seeing a clear “Proxy Settings” screen. Also, my phone clips are probably variable frame rate—what’s the best way to convert them to constant 60fps first (tool + exact settings), and should I set the project to 59.94 or 60?

**Assistant:** In Shotcut, the proxy controls are a little tucked away. Open the Settings menu at the top of the window and look for the “Proxy” item. Turn on “Use Proxy.” Right below it in the same Settings menu, you’ll see “Proxy > Storage” and “Proxy > Mode.” To set the format and resolution, go to Settings, then choose “Proxy > Proxy Format.” In that submenu you’ll see choices like ProRes and DNxHR; pick ProRes Proxy. Then set “Proxy > Proxy Resolution” to 720p. Once those two are set, Shotcut will automatically generate 720p ProRes Proxy files when you import clips.

Since your phone footage is probably variable frame rate, it’s best to convert it to constant frame rate before editing. HandBrake is a simple tool for that. Load a clip, switch Video to Constant Framerate, and choose either 59.94 or 60 as the target. Use H.264 with a medium preset and a high enough bitrate that you’re not adding visible compression; something like 20 to 25 Mbps is fine for 1080p phone footage. Export that, then bring the CFR version into Shotcut so the audio stays locked in.

If your source videos are truly 60.00fps, you can set your Shotcut project to 60. If they’re 59.94, use 59.94. If you’re unsure, 59.94 is the safer default because most phones silently use that instead of exact 60. You can check the exact rate by loading a clip into MediaInfo and looking at the frame rate it reports.

**Assistant:** Shotcut has changed over time, and the older instructions you’ll find online often don’t match current builds. Right now, Shotcut’s proxy system is very simple and doesn’t expose codec/resolution controls in the menus. You only get an on/off switch plus basic storage‑location options. Because you can’t directly pick ProRes or DNxHR inside Shotcut, and because the proxy behavior can be inconsistent on lower‑end Windows systems, switching to Kdenlive is the cleaner and more controllable choice for your setup.

In Kdenlive, open Project, then Project Settings, then the Proxies tab. Turn on “Use Proxies,” and then select the proxy profile from the dropdown. The safest and lightest option for your hardware is the DNxHR LB 720p profile, since it decodes more easily than H.264 proxies and doesn’t tax your GPU. If your system feels sluggish even with that, drop to a 540p proxy profile, but 720p is usually the sweet spot for 1080p footage. To place proxies on your SSD, open Settings, then Configure Kdenlive, then Environment, then Cache. Set both the cache directory and proxy directory to a folder on your SSD, apply, and restart Kdenlive so it uses the new location.

For converting your variable‑frame‑rate phone clips, open HandBrake and pick the “Fast 1080p30” preset as a starting point; you’ll change the frame rate shortly, but this preset keeps all the other defaults sane. Go to the Video tab and set the framerate dropdown to either 59.94 or 60. Most phones actually shoot at 59.94 even when they say “60,” so 59.94 is generally the better choice unless MediaInfo shows the source averaging near 60. Turn on the “Constant Framerate” radio button. Keep the encoder on H.264 at a reasonable bitrate by selecting RF 18–20 or setting an average bitrate around 20 to 25 Mbps. Leave the Tune and Profile settings at their defaults unless you have a reason to change them. Export the file, and that becomes your guaranteed‑CFR master for editing.

To verify it’s truly CFR, open the exported file in MediaInfo, switch to the Text or Tree view, and look for the frame rate line. A proper CFR file shows a single consistent number for “Frame rate,” with no mention of “variable,” no minimum/maximum rate, and no fluctuating values. If it lists only one fixed number—something like 59.940 fps—you’re good.

**User:** Can you double-check the exact Kdenlive menu paths? In my version I’m not seeing “Environment > Cache” under Configure Kdenlive. Where exactly do I set (1) proxy folder, (2) preview/render cache folder, and (3) the proxy profile itself?

**Assistant:** Kdenlive’s menus move around between versions, so here’s the layout that matches current Windows builds (22.x–24.x). I’ll phrase it conversationally so it’s easy to follow.

To pick the proxy profile itself, open Project and then Project Settings. Go to the Proxies tab. That’s where you turn proxies on and choose the codec/size preset, such as DNxHR LB 720p or ProRes Proxy 720p.

To set where proxies are stored, open Settings at the top, then pick Configure Kdenlive. In that window, open Project Defaults on the left and look for the Proxy section. That’s where you’ll find the Proxy folder setting. Point that to a folder on your SSD.

To set the preview and render cache folder, go back to Configure Kdenlive, but this time open the Miscellaneous section. Inside that, there’s a Cache data folder field. That’s your global cache location, and it’s where the timeline preview cache and related temp data will go. Point this to your SSD as well.

About codecs: on Windows, DNxHR LB inside an MXF container is usually the fastest to decode on weaker CPUs. It’s the most reliable choice when smooth playback is the priority. ProRes Proxy in a MOV container also works well and is a bit friendlier for cross‑platform projects, but Windows sometimes decodes DNxHR a little more efficiently. If your system feels borderline, DNxHR LB 720p is the safer bet; if you want maximum compatibility, go with ProRes Proxy 720p instead.
