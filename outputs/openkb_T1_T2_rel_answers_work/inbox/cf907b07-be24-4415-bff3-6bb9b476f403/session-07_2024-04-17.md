# Conversation session 7

Date: 2024-04-17 10:30

**User:** Lately I’ve been thinking about how people decide what’s worth buying versus what’s just impulse—especially with all the ads and “limited-time” deals everywhere. Do you have a framework or a few questions you ask yourself before purchasing something?

**Assistant:** I usually slow things down by running through a couple of quick gut‑checks in my head. I ask myself how often I’ll realistically use the thing, and whether I’d still want it just as much a week from now when the hype wears off. I also pay attention to the feeling driving the purchase—if it’s boredom, stress, or fear of missing out, that’s usually a red flag. And if the price makes me hesitate, I try imagining the money in my hand and whether I’d still trade it for the item. If all of that still feels solid, it’s probably a genuinely worthwhile buy rather than an impulse.

**User:** Could you turn that into a simple checklist I can keep on my phone—maybe 8–10 questions—and include a “wait period” rule for anything over a certain price?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple checklist you can save, kept as clean and short as possible:

1. Will I realistically use this often?
2. Would I still want it just as much a week from now?
3. Am I wanting this because of stress, boredom, or FOMO?
4. Does this solve a real problem I have?
5. Do I already own something that does the job?
6. If I had the money in my hand right now, would I still trade it for this?
7. Would I feel okay telling someone I trust why I’m buying it?
8. Is the “limited-time” pressure affecting my decision?
9. Will this add more clutter or maintenance to my life?
10. Is the cost worth the amount of value or joy I expect to get from it?

And a simple rule: for anything over whatever you consider a significant amount, set a 48‑hour wait period before buying.

**User:** I like this—especially the clutter/maintenance question. I’ve gotten into the habit of turning down unnecessary purchases since I prefer keeping my life free of clutter. Could you help me pick a good threshold for the wait-period rule (like based on income or monthly budget), and maybe suggest a longer wait time for bigger purchases?

**Assistant:** A simple way to set the threshold is to tie it to an amount that would noticeably affect your monthly budget but wouldn’t strain you if you waited. For most people, that ends up being somewhere around 1–3 percent of their monthly take‑home pay. So if you bring in, say, 4,000 a month, anything around 40–120 could trigger the short wait period.

For bigger purchases, you can step it up. Many people use a second threshold—something like 5–10 percent of monthly take‑home—and give those items a longer pause, maybe a full week. The idea is just to create enough space that the emotional “want” fades, and the practical “do I really need this?” becomes clearer.

If you prefer to keep life uncluttered, choose the lower end of those ranges. It makes the wait rule activate more often, which works in your favor.

**User:** That makes sense. Could you help me turn this into a simple 3-tier rule (small/medium/big purchases) with specific percentages and wait times, and maybe a quick example using a $3,500/month take-home budget?

**Assistant:** You can think of it as three spending tiers, each tied to a percentage of your monthly take‑home pay, with a built‑in pause that gets longer as the amount goes up.

For small purchases, set the line around one percent of your monthly income. With a 3,500‑dollar take‑home, that’s anything up to about 35 dollars. These are things you can buy without a wait, as long as they pass your basic checklist.

For medium purchases, use roughly three percent, which lands around 100 dollars. Give those a 48‑hour wait. That’s enough time for the impulse to fade and for you to see whether you still care about the item.

For big purchases, set the threshold at around seven to ten percent—about 250 to 350 dollars in this example. Put a one‑week pause on anything in that range or higher so the decision has room to settle.

That’s all you need: a quick no‑wait zone for small stuff, a two‑day buffer for the mid‑range, and a week‑long breather for anything that could noticeably impact your budget or add real weight to your life.
