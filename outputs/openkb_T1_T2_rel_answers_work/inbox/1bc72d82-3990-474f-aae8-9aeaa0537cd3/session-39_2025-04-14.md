# Conversation session 39

Date: 2025-04-14 10:00

**User:** I’m trying to get my days a little more structured while I job hunt. Nowadays, my main routine consists of sitting at the kitchen table with my laptop, firing off cover letters and hoping to get called in for an interview. Can you help me come up with a simple daily schedule that keeps me productive without burning out, plus a checklist for tailoring cover letters faster?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple structure you can follow without making your day feel rigid. Try easing into the morning with something that wakes you up gently, like a short walk or a quiet breakfast, then give yourself a focused block of job‑search time right after. Keep that block limited so it feels purposeful, not endless. Midday, step away from the computer for a real break, then come back for a lighter round of tasks such as checking messages, doing small admin chores, or researching companies. Wrap up the afternoon with something non‑work that signals you’re done for the day.

For your cover letters, think of the “checklist” as a quick mental run‑through rather than a formal list. Make sure you’ve pulled out one or two requirements from the job post that you directly address, confirmed your opening sentence mentions the role and company by name, swapped in one relevant example that shows you can do the work, and included a closing sentence that feels human and confident. Before sending, double‑check names, dates, and links, then move on without overthinking it.

If you'd like, I can help turn this into a template you can reuse each day.

**User:** Can you make that schedule more specific with actual time blocks (like 9–5-ish), and build the cover letter checklist into a reusable template I can copy/paste for each job? Also, how many applications per day is realistic without quality dropping?

**Assistant:** You could treat your days a bit like a light 9–5 without packing every minute. Something like: start around 9:00 with a calm wake‑up routine and a quick review of your plan. From about 9:30 to 12:00, focus on your main job‑search work: picking one or two roles, researching the companies, and drafting applications. Take a real break for lunch around noon and stay off the laptop until about 1:00. From roughly 1:00 to 3:00, do lighter tasks such as polishing a cover letter, updating your tracking spreadsheet, or sending follow‑ups. From 3:00 to 4:00, step away again for a walk or something that clears your head. If you want a final pass at job‑hunt tasks, keep it short, maybe 4:00 to 5:00, then call the day done.

For your reusable cover‑letter template, you can copy this and fill in the blanks each time:

“Hi [Name], I’m applying for the [Role Title] position at [Company] because [brief reason specific to the company]. Your job post mentions [Key Requirement 1] and [Key Requirement 2], and my background in [Your Experience] includes [short example proving you can do it]. I’m excited about the chance to contribute to [Something the team or company is doing]. Thanks for your time, and I’d love to talk more about how I can help.”

Before sending, just confirm the hiring manager or recruiter’s name, the role title, any numbers or links you mention, and that the example you used directly supports what the job ad asks for.

As for volume, most people can handle about two or three tailored applications a day without the quality slipping. If you’re staying consistent, that’s plenty.

**User:** That helps—can you also suggest a simple way to track applications and follow-ups (like what columns to use in a spreadsheet), and how often I should follow up after applying?

**Assistant:** A simple spreadsheet works well if you keep it lean. You can set it up with columns for the company name, the role title, the date you applied, the link to the job post, the version of your resume or cover letter you used, the contact person if you have one, the current status, and the date you plan to follow up. That’s enough to keep you organized without turning it into a second job.

For follow‑ups, a good rhythm is to check in once about a week after you apply, unless the posting specifically tells you not to. If you still haven’t heard back, you can send one more polite nudge about a week and a half to two weeks later. After that, it’s usually best to move on and keep applying while you wait.

**User:** Could you draft two short follow-up email templates for me—one for about a week after applying, and another “final check-in” 2 weeks later—so they sound polite but not desperate? Also, should I follow up differently if I applied through a company portal vs emailing a recruiter directly?

**Assistant:** Here are two short, simple emails you can reuse. They’re intentionally low‑pressure so they never sound needy.

For the first follow‑up about a week after applying:

“Hi [Name], I hope you’re doing well. I wanted to check in on my application for the [Role Title] position. I’m still very interested and would be glad to answer any questions or provide anything else you need. Thanks for your time.”

For the final check‑in a couple of weeks later:

“Hi [Name], I just wanted to send a quick note to see if there have been any updates on the [Role Title] role. I remain interested and would appreciate any timeline you’re able to share. Thanks again for considering my application.”

If you applied through a company portal and don’t have a person’s name, it’s fine to send the same message to a general hiring inbox or the recruiting email listed on their site. The tone doesn’t need to change; the only real difference is that those channels may be slower or less personal. If you applied directly to a recruiter or hiring manager, keep using their name and send the follow‑ups to them since it feels more direct and is likelier to get a response.
