**Assistant:** You can make this work by setting up tiny cues that nudge you forward without feeling strict. One simple trick is to give yourself a “no sitting down” rule on salsa days. As soon as you get home, you drop your work stuff, grab the gym bag that’s already waiting, and head right back out. Think of it like a quick pit stop rather than arriving for the night.

Another idea is to create a short transition ritual that feels good enough to look forward to. Maybe you change into your salsa clothes the moment you walk in, drink a glass of water, and put on one song that gets you in the mood. If you start the song, you go to class. If you really don’t want to go, you can skip—just not until after the song and the outfit change. That tiny sequence makes following through easier than backing out.

You can also set an “if-then” cue earlier in the day. If it’s a salsa night, then you send yourself a quick message before leaving work, like “Heading out = salsa.” It keeps the plan active in your mind so it feels like the next step, not a whole new decision.

These little commitments work because they bridge the dangerous gap between walking in the door and collapsing on the couch. The moment you keep that gap small, you usually end up out the door without fighting yourself.

**Assistant:** Here are a few you can follow without turning them into rigid lists.

One option is the “continuous motion” script. As you’re leaving work, you tell yourself you’re already on the way to salsa. When you get home, you unlock the door, drop your bag, grab your salsa bag without pausing, and head right back out. If you feel tired, you let yourself acknowledge it but keep your feet moving while you say, “I’ll decide after I’m outside.” Usually, by the time you’re on the sidewalk again, the decision is made.

Another option is the “quick reset” script. On your commute, you mentally switch from work to salsa mode. When you walk in the door, you change into your class clothes immediately, drink a glass of water, and put on a song you love. Your rule is that you don’t get to judge whether you’re too tired until the song ends. Once you’re dressed and the music is going, it’s easier to walk back out the door.

A gentler version is the “two-minute promise.” Before leaving work, you remind yourself that all you have to do is start. When you get home, you set a two-minute timer and use it to get ready just enough to leave—shoes, clothes, bag in hand. When the timer ends, you step outside and take stock. If you’re still tempted to bail, you promise you’ll walk for just one minute toward class. That minute usually does the trick.

For phone reminders, something simple works best. You could use “You’re already on your way,” or “Just start the routine,” or “Change clothes first, decide after.” These feel like nudges rather than pep talks.
