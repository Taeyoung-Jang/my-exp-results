**User:** I’ve been trying to level up my everyday cooking lately—especially the part where you build flavor right at the start. What are your go-to “foundation” aromatics and techniques for making a dish taste rich and savory without getting complicated?

**Assistant:** For everyday cooking, I almost always start with the same simple building blocks: onions (or shallots), garlic, and something aromatic like celery, carrots, or ginger depending on the cuisine. Cooking them slowly in a bit of oil or butter until they soften and get lightly golden does most of the heavy lifting for flavor.

A couple tricks make a big difference. Let the onions cook longer than you think—they don’t have to be caramelized, just gently sweetened. Add salt early so they release moisture and soften. And if you’re using spices, bloom them in the fat once the onions are soft; it wakes everything up and makes the dish taste deeper. A quick splash of something acidic at the end—lemon, vinegar, even a little tomato—tightens the whole thing together.

None of this is fancy, but it makes almost anything taste richer with almost no extra effort.

**User:** That helps a lot. When you say “bloom spices,” how long are we talking—and how do you keep garlic from burning if it’s in the pan too? Also, what’s your favorite quick deglaze to grab the browned bits without turning the dish sour?

**Assistant:** Blooming spices is quick—usually 20 to 40 seconds. You just want them fragrant, not dark. If garlic is in the pan, you can keep it safe by adding it a little later than the onions, letting the onions act as a buffer, or by adding a splash of liquid as soon as the garlic starts turning pale gold.

For deglazing, my fastest, most forgiving option is a small pour of broth or even water. It loosens the browned bits without shifting the flavor too much. If I want a little extra brightness without tipping into sour, a mix of broth with just a tiny squeeze of lemon or a splash of wine works nicely.

**User:** I make sure there's always fresh garlic around; honestly, a meal just feels incomplete to me without that savory base. Given that, can you walk me through the timing you’d use in a typical sauté so I can get the garlic flavor I want without it going bitter—like when exactly you’d add it relative to onions, spices, and the deglaze?

**Assistant:** If you’re doing a simple sauté, I’d start the onions first and give them a head start until they’re soft and just beginning to turn translucent. That usually takes a few minutes. Once they’ve relaxed a bit, I’d add the garlic; it only needs a short moment—about a minute—before it becomes fragrant. As soon as you smell it blooming, add your spices so they can warm in the fat without the garlic sitting there too long on its own.

When everything smells good and the spices feel “awake,” that’s your cue to deglaze. The moment the liquid hits the pan, the garlic is safe because the temperature drops and the moisture keeps it from browning further. This gives you full garlic presence without the bitterness that comes from letting it sit in hot oil too long.

**User:** Could you give me a concrete example with approximate times and heat levels—like “medium heat for X minutes, then add garlic for Y seconds,” etc.—for a basic onion-garlic base, and maybe one variation where you add tomato paste or soy sauce before deglazing?

**Assistant:** Sure. Here’s a straightforward, everyday version you can use as a template.

For a basic onion‑garlic base, I’d heat a little oil over medium heat until it’s warm but not shimmering aggressively. Add the onions and let them cook on medium for about five to seven minutes, just long enough to soften and turn translucent with a hint of gold at the edges. When they look relaxed, stir in the garlic and give it roughly thirty to sixty seconds—just until you catch that first strong whiff of garlic aroma. Once it hits that point, add any spices you’re using and let them toast in the fat for another half-minute or so. After that, go ahead and deglaze; the added liquid immediately drops the temperature and keeps both the garlic and spices from going too far.

If you want to work in tomato paste, I’d let the onions soften as above, add the garlic for that brief fragrant moment, then stir in a small spoonful of tomato paste. Let it cook for one to two minutes on medium heat. It’ll darken slightly and smell a little sweeter—that’s your cue to deglaze.

For a soy sauce variation, follow the same basic timing but add the soy sauce right after the spices bloom. Let it bubble for maybe fifteen seconds so it coats everything, then deglaze. It deepens the base without much extra work and won’t burn as long as you don’t leave it sitting too long before adding liquid.
