**User:** I’m trying to plan an actually restful stretch away from work, and I want it to feel “unplugged” instead of just working from somewhere else. What are some practical steps to set boundaries and prep things in advance so I can truly disconnect without coming back to chaos?

**Assistant:** One of the easiest ways to make time off feel truly “off” is to do a little boundary‑setting before you step away. It helps to start by telling the people who rely on you exactly when you’ll be unavailable and who they should go to instead. If you’re able to designate even a temporary point person for common questions, it prevents those “quick check‑ins” from landing on you while you’re trying to rest.

Before you disconnect, gather any open tasks and either finish them or clearly hand them off. Even a short note that explains the status of each thing can save you from a messy pile when you return. It also helps to write a brief plan for your future self about what matters most the week you get back, so you’re not spinning up from zero.

Set an out‑of‑office message that makes your boundaries obvious and gives people a path forward without you. If you’re tempted to peek at messages, remove your work apps from your phone or sign out completely; friction is your friend here.

Finally, decide in advance what “unplugged” means to you—no email, no Slack, no calendar, whatever it is—and treat it like a real commitment. When you come back, ease in with a buffer day if you can, so you can catch up intentionally instead of being hit with everything at once.

**User:** That helps—can you give me a concrete checklist and a couple of example out-of-office messages (one for email, one for Slack) that clearly say I’m not checking anything and point people to a backup contact? Also, any tips for making sure I don’t get pulled back in by “just one quick question”?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple checklist you can follow:

• Tell your team and key partners the dates you’ll be fully offline.  
• Assign a clear backup contact and make sure that person knows what to expect.  
• Hand off or wrap up anything active, and leave brief status notes for each item.  
• Set your out‑of‑office messages with clear boundaries and the backup contact.  
• Sign out of work apps or remove them from your phone.  
• Leave a short “first day back” plan so you can re‑enter calmly.

Sample email out‑of‑office:  
“Thanks for your message. I’m fully offline and not checking email until I return on [date]. For anything time‑sensitive, please reach out to [name] at [email]. I’ll get back to you once I’m back.”

Sample Slack out‑of‑office:  
“I’m unplugged and not checking Slack until [date]. If you need help before then, please contact [name]. I’ll respond when I’m back.”

To avoid getting pulled in by “quick questions,” make it hard to access work by logging out of everything, turning off notifications, and letting your team know ahead of time that you won’t see messages. The more you reduce your own ability to peek, the easier it is to stay truly offline.

**User:** I finally got the green light to take three weeks of uninterrupted PTO starting Monday—no laptop, no Slack—so I’m spending this weekend figuring out who gets the house keys and where the dog’s staying. Can you tailor that checklist into a “Friday end-of-day + weekend” plan (what to do in what order), and also give me slightly firmer email/Slack OOO templates that make it crystal clear I won’t respond and that the backup contact is the only route for anything urgent?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple Friday‑through‑weekend plan you can follow without turning it into a big project.

Start Friday afternoon by telling your immediate team, your manager, and anyone who regularly depends on you that you’ll be fully offline for three weeks. Give them your backup person’s name and make it clear that once you log off, all questions should go to that person. Right after that, hand off any active tasks. Keep it very short: a quick note on what’s done, what’s pending, and anything the backup needs to watch for. Then write a brief “first day back” note to your future self and leave it somewhere obvious. End the day by setting your out‑of‑office messages, signing out of email and Slack on every device, and shutting your laptop for good.

On Saturday, take care of logistical life things: confirm who has your house keys, double‑check pet care, and handle anything that might tug you back during PTO (bills, deliveries, appointments). If you have any lingering work apps on your phone, delete or disable them. If you’re worried about muscle memory opening email, sign out of the account entirely.

On Sunday, keep it light. Do one last sweep to make sure your OOO messages look exactly the way you want, confirm your backup contact is comfortable, and then mentally shift into vacation mode. Treat Sunday as your “off ramp” so you’re not carrying work energy into Monday.

Here are firmer OOO templates.

Email:  
“I’m out on extended PTO and completely offline until [date]. I will not be checking or responding to email during this time. For anything urgent or time‑sensitive, contact [name] at [email]. Messages sent to me before I return will be read after I’m back.”

Slack:  
“I’m on PTO and fully offline until [date]. I won’t see or respond to Slack messages while I’m away. For anything that can’t wait, please reach out to [name].”

These make it clear you’re unreachable and give people a single path forward, which is the best way to keep yourself truly out of the loop.
