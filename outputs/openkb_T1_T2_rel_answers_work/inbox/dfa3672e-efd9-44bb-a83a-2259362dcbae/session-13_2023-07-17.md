**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about commitment and reliability lately—like, when you say you’ll do something, how much should that promise matter if circumstances change? How do you decide what “keeping your word” looks like in real life without being rigid or unfair to yourself?

**Assistant:** I think “keeping your word” matters most as an expression of respect for yourself and the people you make promises to, but it doesn’t have to mean sticking to something no matter what. Real life is full of shifting priorities, new information, and legitimate limits.

A good way to think about it is that a promise is a commitment to act in good faith. If something changes, the responsible thing isn’t to blindly power through—it’s to communicate early, explain honestly, and take ownership of whatever impact the change has. That still counts as keeping your word, because you’re treating the promise seriously rather than disappearing or pretending nothing shifted.

So your word should matter, but not at the cost of your well‑being or reality. It’s more about showing up with integrity than being perfectly consistent.

**User:** Okay, but what if it’s not a crisis—just a better opportunity or something I’d honestly rather do? Is it still “integrity” to back out if I give notice, or is that basically making my promises meaningless?

**Assistant:** It depends less on the fact that you’re changing plans and more on the spirit behind the decision.

If you bail the moment something shinier comes along, people eventually learn that your yes doesn’t really mean yes. That does chip away at trust, even if you give notice. But you’re also allowed to change your mind when something genuinely matters more to you. Integrity isn’t about never choosing differently—it’s about being honest with yourself about *why* you’re doing it and being respectful of anyone affected.

If you can look at the situation and say, “I’m not doing this just because I’m bored or impulsive; this new thing meaningfully aligns with my priorities,” then giving clear, early communication is still acting with integrity. If the real reason is simply that something more fun came up, it’s worth acknowledging that and deciding whether the short‑term gain is worth the long‑term cost to reliability.

So your promises don’t have to be unbreakable. They just shouldn’t be casual.

**User:** That makes sense. I’m stuck on the “casual vs serious” part—how do you signal that up front? Like, are there phrases or boundaries you use so people know when you’re making a real commitment versus a tentative plan, without sounding flaky?

**Assistant:** You can signal it pretty naturally just by being clear about your level of certainty when you agree to something. People don’t usually need formal labels; they just need you to match your tone with your intent.

If you’re still unsure, saying something like “I’m likely in, but I need to confirm later” sets a different expectation than “Count on me.” If you know your plans depend on how the week goes, you can say so up front. And when you really mean it, using simple, confident language—“Yes, I’m there”—tells people they can rely on you.

It’s less about special phrases and more about not pretending to be more committed than you actually are. Being honest at the beginning feels a little vulnerable, but people usually appreciate it because it helps them plan and it protects you from feeling boxed in later.

If the original commitment was meaningful to your friend, or you know they’re counting on you in a way that affects their feelings or plans, that usually tips the scale toward honoring it. If the original plan was lower‑stakes and the new opportunity genuinely matters more to you, then backing out can be okay as long as you’re upfront and considerate.

What you’d say doesn’t need to be dramatic. Something like, “I know I said I’d be there, and I’m sorry to change plans. Something came up that I want to take, but I didn’t want to leave you hanging, so I’m telling you as soon as I can. If this puts you in a bind, let me know and I’ll figure it out.” That shows respect, takes responsibility, and leaves room to adjust if they really need you.

The key is that you’re not treating your promise as disposable—you’re weighing both sides and handling the change with care. That’s usually enough to keep your word meaningful without locking yourself into every plan forever.
