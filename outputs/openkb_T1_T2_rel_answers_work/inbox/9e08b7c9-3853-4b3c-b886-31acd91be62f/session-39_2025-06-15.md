**User:** I’m thinking about ways to showcase my software work to recruiters beyond just listing job titles—what are some strong, concrete ways to demonstrate impact and skill (especially for engineering roles) without it feeling like fluff?

**Assistant:** You can make your work feel real and credible by pointing to things that are *observable* rather than just descriptive. Recruiters latch onto anything they can mentally verify, even if they never actually check it.

One of the strongest approaches is to surface artifacts of your work. That could be links to PRs that show how you think, small code samples you’re proud of, a short write‑up explaining a tricky engineering problem you solved, or a before‑and‑after explanation of a system you improved. These don’t need to be long—two paragraphs and a diagram can be more persuasive than a whole portfolio site.

You can also share measurable changes you drove, even if they’re small. Improvements like reducing build times, cutting API latency, shrinking cloud costs, or eliminating a recurring on‑call incident show clear engineering impact and don’t feel like fluff because they’re grounded in numbers.

If you’ve built tools or automations that teammates relied on, call those out and explain the pain point they solved. Recruiters love evidence that engineers notice problems and quietly make things better.

Finally, any public footprint helps: a tiny open‑source contribution, a gist explaining a pattern you use often, or a short blog post on something technical you learned. These act as “proof of work” beyond your résumé and give a sense of your thinking style, which is often what companies are trying to gauge.

**User:** I was updating my résumé this week and realized I can’t really point people to any recent commits under my account anymore, since everything I’ve been working on lately lives behind private access—what are some good ways to provide “proof of work” anyway (e.g., portfolio pieces, sanitized write-ups, redacted screenshots), and how would you recommend I present that on my résumé or LinkedIn without raising red flags?

**Assistant:** You can still offer credible proof of work without exposing anything proprietary, and it won’t raise red flags if you frame it as standard industry practice.

One approach is to create short, sanitized write‑ups about problems you solved. Keep them high‑level: describe the engineering challenge, the constraints, the approach you took, and the measurable outcome, but don’t include code, logs, architecture diagrams, or anything that would reveal internal systems. These read like mini case studies and are completely normal for engineers who work in private repos.

Another option is to show process rather than product. You can talk through your reasoning, tradeoffs you evaluated, or how you approached debugging a tricky issue. This gives people a sense of how you think without exposing details.

If you have reusable side work—small tools, scripts, experiments, or learning projects—you can polish and publish those. Even something modest can serve as a public example of your coding style and technical instincts.

Redacted screenshots can work as long as they’re clearly conceptual. For example, you might show a high‑level dashboard layout or a diagram with the labels removed, just to illustrate the kind of systems you’ve worked with. Make sure it’s obvious that you intentionally redacted everything sensitive; that signals professionalism rather than secrecy.

On a résumé or LinkedIn, you can simply add a line under your summary or projects section saying that you maintain a small set of public case studies or technical notes. Something like “Selected technical write‑ups available on request” or “Public engineering notes and small open‑source samples available at <link>” feels normal and gives recruiters exactly what they need to feel confident without implying you’re trying to leak anything from a previous employer.
