# Conversation session 39

**User:** I’ve been thinking about how everything seems to be moving to subscriptions lately—apps, software, even some everyday services. How do you decide when a subscription is actually worth it versus buying something outright or using a free option?

**Assistant:** I usually think about it in a few simple terms: how often you use the thing, how quickly it changes or needs updates, and whether the subscription actually gives you real, ongoing value instead of just locking a feature behind a paywall.

If it’s something you use all the time—like cloud storage, music, or software that genuinely improves with updates—a subscription can make sense. It spreads out the cost and you’re paying for something that keeps evolving.

If the product doesn’t change much or you only need it occasionally, owning it outright tends to be better. Subscriptions can quietly pile up and cost far more than a one‑time purchase before you even notice.

And if there’s a free option that meets your needs without being frustrating or limited in a critical way, there’s nothing wrong with sticking to it. The best test is whether you’d feel the loss if you canceled. If the answer is no, the subscription probably isn’t worth keeping.

**User:** That “would I actually miss it if I canceled?” test really clicks for me. What are a few categories where you think subscriptions are almost never worth it, and a few where they usually are? Also, any tips for keeping track so they don’t quietly stack up?

**Assistant:** Some subscriptions almost always feel like bad deals, mostly because the product doesn’t really change or doesn’t need ongoing service. Things like basic utility apps, single‑purpose tools, or devices that lock essential features behind a monthly fee usually fall into that “not worth it” zone. Once you’ve bought the thing, you’re not getting much ongoing value, so the monthly charge is just padding.

On the flip side, subscriptions tied to something that genuinely keeps evolving tend to make more sense. Streaming services, cloud storage, or professional software that’s constantly updated can justify a monthly cost because you’re paying for continuous access or improvements, not just a one‑time product.

As for keeping them from stacking up, the easiest trick is to put all your renewals in a calendar with alerts. Another surprisingly helpful habit is checking your bank or card statements once a month with subscriptions in mind. And if you can, run everything through one payment method so nothing hides in the background.

**User:** Yeah, that matches how I’ve been feeling—especially with those “single-purpose” apps. Can you give me a short checklist I can run through before I start a new subscription, and maybe a simple way to do a monthly review without it turning into a whole project?

**Assistant:** You can think of the checklist as a quick mental run‑through rather than something written out. Before subscribing, ask yourself whether you’ll use it often enough to justify paying every month, whether the subscription actually adds ongoing value instead of just unlocking something that should be included, whether a one‑time purchase or free option would meet your needs just as well, and whether you’d genuinely miss it if it disappeared tomorrow.

For the monthly review, keep it as lightweight as possible. Pick one day a month, glance at the charges on whatever card you use for subscriptions, and ask yourself if each one still earns its place. If you treat it like a five‑minute habit instead of a budgeting project, it stays painless and you’re far less likely to let anything slip by unnoticed.

**User:** I’ve been enjoying how much simpler things feel lately—no more recurring charges showing up each month, and I’m fine sticking with the basic tools for what I need day to day. With that in mind, what’s a good “default rule” for when it’s still worth trying a subscription anyway, and how long should I give it before deciding to cancel?

**Assistant:** A simple rule you can lean on is this: only try a subscription if it solves a real problem you already feel, not a hypothetical one you might have someday. If something promises to save you time, replace several other tools, or genuinely upgrade a part of your daily routine, it can be worth a test run. If it’s more of a “maybe I’ll use it,” it’s probably not worth starting.

For the trial period, give it just long enough to see whether it naturally works its way into your life. Two to four weeks is usually enough. If you find yourself forgetting it exists or having to force yourself to use it, that’s your sign to cancel. If it becomes effortless and helpful without you having to justify it, it’s probably earning its keep.
