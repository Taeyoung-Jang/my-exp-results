# Conversation session 15

**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about career direction lately—how people decide whether to stay in a role they’re good at versus aiming for the “next level.” What do you think are the best ways to evaluate that choice without getting swept up in other people’s expectations?

**Assistant:** I think the clearest way to sort it out is to get very honest about what *you* actually experience day to day, rather than what you think you “should” want.

A good starting point is noticing how the work makes you feel. If your current role leaves you energized more often than drained, and you like the rhythm of it, that’s real data. The pull toward the “next level” should come from curiosity or excitement, not guilt or comparison.

It also helps to imagine the next level in concrete terms instead of as an abstract promotion. Picture the kinds of problems you’d be solving, the pace, the responsibility, the level of uncertainty. If that version of work feels like a natural extension of what you enjoy, it’s worth exploring. If it feels like a costume that doesn’t quite fit, that matters too.

And finally, check in with your values. Some people value mastery and depth, some value growth and challenge, some value stability or autonomy. If the choice aligns with your values, you’re far less likely to get derailed by other people’s expectations.

In the end, you’re not choosing between “ambition” and “staying put.” You’re choosing between two versions of a life you want to live, and the right answer is the one that feels like you can breathe more easily inside it.

**User:** That framing helps. How would you suggest I test-drive what a “next level” role would actually feel like without fully committing—especially if I’m not sure I want more ambiguity and people-management?

**Assistant:** You can usually get a pretty good feel for the “next level” without officially moving into it by taking on small slices of what that role actually involves. The trick is to do it in low‑risk, time‑bounded ways.

One option is to volunteer for a short project that has a bit more uncertainty or cross‑team coordination than your usual work. You don’t have to run the whole thing—just take on a piece where you’d need to navigate ambiguity or influence others. Pay attention to how it feels in your body and your mind: energizing, tolerable, or draining.

Another approach is to shadow someone who’s already operating at that level. Sitting in on a few of their meetings or asking them to walk you through how they make decisions will give you a surprisingly vivid picture of the day‑to‑day realities.

You can also try a temporary leadership moment, like mentoring a newer teammate or guiding a small group through a decision. It’s a safe way to sense whether people‑management feels natural or like something you’d rather avoid.

If you treat these as experiments rather than trials, you’ll learn quickly what resonates with you and what doesn’t—without locking yourself into a path before you’re ready.

**User:** If I do a short “leadership” experiment—like mentoring or coordinating a cross-team project—what are a few specific signals I should watch for that tell me “this is a good stretch” versus “this is the kind of work I’ll eventually resent”?

**Assistant:** You can tell a lot by how you feel during and after the work. If it’s a good stretch, you’ll likely notice a kind of steady alertness—you might be challenged, but you don’t feel scrambled. You’ll catch yourself getting curious about how to do it better rather than counting the minutes until it’s over. Even if you’re tired afterward, it’s the satisfying kind of tired, like your brain did something meaningful.

If it’s the kind of work you’d end up resenting, the emotional tone is different. You may feel a tightness or dread before doing it, or a lingering irritability afterward. Instead of “I’m growing into this,” it feels more like “I’m performing a version of myself I don’t actually want to be.” You might also notice that the people‑focused parts drain you more than the actual problem‑solving does, or that you’re spending a lot of energy masking frustration rather than engaging.

Those sensations are usually clearer and more honest than any narrative you try to put on top. The role should stretch you, not distort you.

**User:** I should probably add a constraint here: I'm not really looking to climb the ladder anymore; I find much more satisfaction in maintaining the systems I know well than navigating the uncertainty of a new position. Given that, what would a “good stretch” look like that still builds depth and impact without pushing me toward people-management or a more ambiguous role?

**Assistant:** A “good stretch” for someone who prefers depth, stability, and system‑level mastery usually feels more like sharpening a tool than switching careers. It’s the kind of challenge that lets you stay close to the technical or operational core of what you already do, while still giving you room to grow.

In practice, that might look like taking on a knotty problem inside your current domain—something that requires investigation, pattern‑spotting, or redesigning a piece of the system you already understand better than anyone. You’re not stepping into ambiguity for its own sake; you’re using your expertise to make things cleaner, faster, or more reliable.

Another version is becoming the person others seek out for guidance on a specific topic, not as a manager but as a resident expert. Mentoring in that context feels different: you’re not running their careers, you’re helping them navigate a system you’ve mastered. If that kind of guidance leaves you feeling grounded rather than drained, it’s a good sign you’re stretching in the right direction.

You can also look for chances to influence how work gets done without taking on formal leadership. Improving a workflow, writing clearer documentation, or shaping technical decisions can give you more impact while keeping you anchored in the environment where you’re strongest.

If the stretch keeps you in the realm of depth, clarity, and craftsmanship—and doesn’t ask you to become a different kind of person just to do it—it’s probably aligned with the path you actually want.

**User:** Can you give me a few concrete examples of “depth and impact” projects that fit that—like things I could propose in a typical engineering/IT environment (reliability, tooling, documentation, observability, etc.)—and how I’d frame them so they’re seen as valuable even without a title change?

**Assistant:** You can make this work by describing each idea as a problem the team already feels, then positioning your project as a low‑friction way to remove that pain. That keeps it valuable without tying it to hierarchy.

For example, you could take on improving a flaky or noisy part of the system—maybe a service that alerts too often or a deployment step that regularly stalls. If you frame it as reducing interruptions, cutting downtime, or giving everyone back focus time, it’s immediately clear why it matters.

Another option is building or refining internal tooling that the team relies on every day. A smoother local dev setup, a cleaner set of scripts, or a more reliable test harness can save hours across the team. When you pitch it as “making our daily work faster and less error‑prone,” it lands as a force multiplier.

You might also focus on observability. If there’s an area where the team consistently ends up guessing—logs scattered across systems, unclear dashboards, poor tracing—you can propose creating a clearer, unified view. Framed as shortening diagnosis time or reducing on‑call stress, it reads as practical impact, not title ambition.

Even documentation can be positioned this way. Instead of “I want to overhaul docs,” you can frame it as “people keep losing time rediscovering the same knowledge; I want to capture and organize it so we stop repeating work.” That’s depth with measurable payoff.

These kinds of projects signal leadership through craft, not management. And because each one ties directly to team pain, they tend to be valued on outcomes, not job titles.

**User:** Could you help me turn one of these into a concrete proposal I can take to my manager—maybe an observability/alert-noise reduction project? I’d like a short outline with scope, success metrics, rough timeline, and how it benefits the team (especially on-call), without it sounding like I’m angling for a promotion.

**Assistant:** Here’s a version you can hand to your manager that stays grounded, practical, and clearly about improving the team’s quality of life rather than changing your role.

You could say something like:

“I’d like to take on a focused observability and alert‑noise cleanup project. Right now we’re getting a mix of low‑value or unclear alerts, and on‑call often has to dig around multiple dashboards before figuring out what’s actually wrong. My plan is to map out the current alert set, identify which ones are redundant, noisy, or missing context, and then tune or remove them. I’d also put together a clearer set of dashboards or run‑books so that when something fires, whoever is on‑call can move straight to diagnosis instead of guessing.

The scope would be limited to the most active services and the alerts that trigger most often, so it’s manageable. For success metrics, I’d track a drop in noisy alerts, a faster time‑to‑understanding when something fires, and fewer unnecessary escalations. I think I can get an initial pass done in about four to six weeks alongside my regular work, with small updates after that as we learn what’s helping.

The main benefit is giving the team a calmer, more predictable on‑call experience and making incidents easier to handle, especially for newer folks. This isn’t about changing my role—it’s just an area where I already have the context, and I think I can make the system smoother for everyone.”

That keeps it concrete, scoped, and clearly driven by practicality rather than ladder climbing.
