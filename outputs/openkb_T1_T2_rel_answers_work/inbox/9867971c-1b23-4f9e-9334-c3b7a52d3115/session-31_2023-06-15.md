**User:** I’ve been trying to simplify my finances and think more intentionally about where my money “lives,” but I’m torn between keeping things super liquid versus putting more into longer-term investments. If you were helping someone design a clean, low-maintenance strategy, how would you decide what belongs in cash, what belongs in real estate, and what belongs in private/alternative investments?

**Assistant:** I’d think about it in terms of time horizon, stability, and how often you want to touch the money.

Cash is basically for anything you might need in the near term: your emergency buffer, surprises, and upcoming goals you don’t want exposed to market swings. If you know the money might be used within a couple of years, cash or cash‑like accounts usually make the most sense.

Real estate fits best when you’re looking for something relatively stable that you plan to hold for a long time and don’t mind being illiquid. It works well if you want slow, steady growth or income and you’re comfortable with the fact that it’s harder and slower to access your money once it’s in a property.

Private or alternative investments really only make sense for money you won’t need for a long time and are comfortable taking more risk with. They can be great diversifiers, but they’re usually the least liquid and the least predictable, so they belong in the “patient capital” bucket.

So the way I’d help someone decide is simply by matching each dollar to when they’ll need it and how much uncertainty they can tolerate. Short-term needs stay in cash. Long-term, stable goals can go into real estate. Long-term, higher-risk, higher‑reward goals fit better in private or alternative investments.

If the high‑yield savings account could cover several months of expenses plus any realistic “oh no” scenarios—job changes, vacancies in the rentals, capital calls from the PE deals—then your liquidity is probably fine. Most issues people run into come from underestimating how often real estate and private equity tie up cash, either through repairs, turnover, or follow‑on funding.

On the concentration side, I’d just make sure you aren’t relying too heavily on a single geography, a single type of tenant, or a single private equity firm or fund strategy. You don’t need to be perfectly diversified, but you want to avoid a situation where one local downturn or one manager’s mistake hits everything at once.

If the savings balance feels a little too small for your comfort, or if you’d be forced to sell a property or exit an investment early to handle a surprise, that’s the biggest red flag. Otherwise, having liquidity in cash and long‑term growth in rentals and PE is a very normal, workable mix—as long as you stay honest about how illiquid those long‑term buckets really are.

**User:** How would you actually quantify “enough” cash in my situation—do you think in terms of months of personal expenses, a percentage of net worth, or something like “6 months + X% of property value for reserves”? And for the PE deals, what’s your rule of thumb for planning around capital calls so I’m not forced to sell something at a bad time?

**Assistant:** I’d anchor it to real life cash needs instead of a percentage of net worth, because net‑worth‑based rules tend to be too abstract when you hold illiquid assets like rentals and private equity.

A simple way to think about “enough” cash is to start with whatever number of months of personal expenses helps you sleep at night—most people land somewhere between three and twelve depending on stability of income—then layer in very practical real‑estate reserves.

For rentals, I’d look at the actual carrying cost of your properties and make sure you could comfortably handle a few ugly but realistic situations: a vacancy or two, a major repair, or a tenant who stops paying. People often use the idea of holding a few months of expenses per property or keeping a cushion based on expected repairs and turnover, but the point is just to set aside enough that a bad month doesn’t force you to scramble.

For private equity, the cleaner way to plan is to assume that anything committed but not yet called is basically unavailable. In other words, you don’t treat uncalled capital as part of your spendable cash. If the fund has a history or pattern of calling capital in waves, you can keep a small buffer dedicated to that, but in general the safest approach is simply to reserve the full commitment mentally (and functionally) so you’re never caught off guard.

If you can comfortably cover your personal life, your properties’ occasional bursts of expenses, and any remaining capital commitments without touching long‑term investments, that’s usually the right level of liquidity.

The idea isn’t precision; it’s making sure a vacancy or a sudden expense doesn’t force you to raid long‑term investments.

Finally, I’d ask for your total uncalled capital across the private equity deals. The simplest rule is to assume the entire remaining commitment is spoken for. Treat it as money you cannot spend, because capital calls are rarely timed around your convenience.

Once you have those three dollar amounts, you add them together. If your HYSA balance can cover that combined number with a little breathing room, you’re probably in a healthy range. If your cash is noticeably below that total, you’re running thin and could be forced to sell something in a downturn. If your cash is far above it—enough that a meaningful chunk has no job and is just sitting there—you might be over‑liquid and leaving long‑term returns on the table.

It ends up feeling like a simple question: after filling the three buckets, do you still have idle cash with no purpose, or are you barely scraping by? That’s the check I’d use.
