# Conversation session 35

Date: 2025-08-15 14:30

**User:** I’ve noticed I tend to jump straight into “but here’s why that’s wrong” whenever I hear an argument I disagree with. Do you have any practical exercises or mental habits for getting better at steelmanning the other side first—like genuinely articulating their goals and values—before I start critiquing?

**Assistant:** A simple way to build the habit is to treat it like a short ritual you do before responding. When someone makes a point, pause and try to restate their view in a way that they would endorse. You’re not trying to agree with it, just to capture what a reasonable person on that side might mean.

A good exercise is to force yourself to write or say one or two sentences beginning with something like “The underlying concern here is…” or “If I put myself in their shoes, the value they’re protecting is…”. Don’t move on until you can phrase it without a sarcastic edge.

Another helpful habit is to imagine you’ve been temporarily drafted to argue *for* their position in front of an audience that already supports it. You don’t have to win the debate; you just need to avoid embarrassing your “team.” That mindset naturally pushes you to look for the strongest, most coherent version of the argument.

If you practice these small steps in low‑stakes conversations—podcasts, articles, imaginary debates—you’ll find the muscle shows up more easily when you’re in real discussions.

**User:** I’ve been helping my niece with her debate prep, and the coach has us do this weird drill: we can’t bring up counterarguments at all until we’ve written a full page of what the other side is trying to accomplish in the best possible light. It’s uncomfortable, but it’s making me notice how quickly I reach for the ugliest interpretation by default. How would you adapt something like that for everyday conversations where I don’t have time to “write a page”—like a quick mental checklist or a 30-second version I can run through before I respond?

**Assistant:** You can shrink that drill into a quick pause where you mentally answer just two questions before you open your mouth: what is this person *trying* to protect or achieve, and what’s the most charitable version of their worry?

You don’t need a full paragraph—one or two clear sentences in your head is enough. Something like “If I assume good faith, their goal is probably X,” or “The fear underneath this is likely Y.” Once you can state that without an internal eye‑roll, you’ve done the 30‑second version of the page‑long exercise.

It’s really about creating a tiny gap between hearing the argument and reacting to it. If you can build the reflex to pause and name their value or goal—even briefly—you’ll find the tone of your response shifts automatically, and the conversation stays much more constructive.

**User:** Can you give me an actual “30-second script” I could use in the moment—like 2–3 sentences I can say out loud to show I’m steelmanning before I critique—plus a few example versions for common hot-button topics (politics at Thanksgiving, school policy, workplace change)?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple out‑loud script you can use: “Let me make sure I’m capturing your point. As I understand it, you’re focused on [their underlying goal/value], and you’re worried that [their key concern]. Tell me if I’m getting that right before I respond.” It’s short, neutral, and signals respect without locking you into agreement.

For Thanksgiving‑style politics, it might sound like: “So you’re emphasizing stability and wanting policies that don’t disrupt people’s lives too quickly, and you’re worried the other approach could backfire. I want to check that I’m hearing you correctly before I add my take.”

For school policy: “It sounds like your priority is giving students a predictable environment, and you’re concerned that changing this rule might create confusion for teachers and families. Let me know if that matches what you meant.”

For workplace changes: “It seems like what you’re aiming for is smoother teamwork, and you’re concerned that the proposed shift might actually slow people down. I want to be sure I’ve got your reasoning right before I weigh in.”

Each version plants you briefly in their perspective, shows good faith, and buys you the space to pivot into your own view without escalating tension.

**User:** Could you also give me a “silent” version for when I can’t or shouldn’t mirror back out loud (like in a tense meeting), and maybe a quick way to tell if I’ve actually steelmanned vs. just paraphrased?

**Assistant:** A quiet version can be just a two‑step pause in your head: first name their goal, then name their fear. Something like, “Their aim is probably X. Their worry is likely Y.” If you can do that clearly and without slipping into mockery, you’ve done a real steelman.

A good check is whether, in your gut, you feel a moment of “I get why a reasonable person could hold this.” If you can imagine them nodding at your internal summary, you’ve steelmanned. If your summary makes them sound small, irrational, or petty, you’ve only paraphrased.
