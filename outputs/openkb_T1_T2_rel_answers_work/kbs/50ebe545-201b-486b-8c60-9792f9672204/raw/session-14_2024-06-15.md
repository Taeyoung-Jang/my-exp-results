# Conversation session 14

Date: 2024-06-15 14:30

**User:** I keep getting nudged to join store “rewards” programs every time I check out—are they actually worth it, or are there smarter ways to save money without signing up for a bunch of accounts?

**Assistant:** They’re only “worth it” in a few situations, and most people don’t need to sign up for every program they’re offered.

A rewards program is usually good when you shop at that store often enough to actually earn something back, and when the program doesn’t require you to hand over a lot of personal data or download an app you’ll never use. Grocery stores, pharmacies, and places you visit weekly can make sense because the discounts are immediate and don’t require much effort.

For everything else, you can usually save money without signing up. Checking prices with a quick search, using a single cash‑back card you trust, or waiting for predictable sales tends to save just as much without maintaining a dozen accounts. Some stores will even scan a public or generic loyalty code that’s already floating around online if you just want the sale price without joining anything.

So the short version: sign up only where you shop regularly, and skip the rest unless there’s a truly meaningful discount tied to it.

**User:** I actually gave up on those loyalty programs a long time ago; I prefer paying full price over letting them log everything I buy. Given that, what are the best low-effort ways to save at checkout that don’t involve accounts or apps—especially for groceries and pharmacies?

**Assistant:** You can still shave a surprising amount off your bill without creating accounts.

The easiest win is timing. Most grocery and pharmacy chains run weekly sales, and just glancing at the shelf tags while you shop often gives you the same discounts loyalty members get, especially on staples. Buying the store-brand version of anything from painkillers to pasta is another reliable cut; the ingredients are usually identical, and the price difference is consistent.

Being conscious of unit prices helps more than most people expect. The cheapest option isn’t always the biggest package, so comparing by ounce or tablet keeps you from overpaying. Clearance sections in both pharmacies and supermarkets can be genuinely good too, especially for seasonal items and overstock.

If you’re flexible, choosing produce that’s in season and protein that’s discounted that week can lower the whole bill without feeling like a sacrifice. And for household basics, stocking up a little when the price dips—without turning it into a hobby—pays off over time.

Those few habits usually save more than most loyalty programs, and you don’t have to sign up for anything.

**User:** Can you give me a short, practical “checkout script” for when the cashier asks for a phone number/rewards, plus a simple weekly routine (like 5–10 minutes) to catch the best grocery/pharmacy deals without accounts?

**Assistant:** When they ask for a phone number or rewards info, something simple works well, like: “No thanks, I’m skipping the rewards program, but I’m good to go as is.” If they push, you can add: “I prefer not to use loyalty accounts, but I’ll take any public sale price.”

For a quick weekly routine, give yourself a few minutes before your usual shopping day to glance at the paper or digital version of the store’s weekly ad—most chains let you view it without logging in. Look for the handful of items you buy often, notice what’s actually on sale, and shape your list around those. When you’re in the store, check the endcaps and clearance shelves on your usual path, skim the shelf tags for any unadvertised discounts, and choose store‑brand versions when they make sense. That little bit of planning plus in‑store awareness usually captures the best deals with almost no extra work.

**User:** That helps—can you make the routine even more concrete, like a checklist I can follow (including what to look for in the weekly ad and what to check once I’m in the store), and include a couple alternate one-liners in case the cashier keeps pushing?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple, compact checklist you can literally run through in a couple of minutes each week, plus a few extra checkout lines.

Weekly ad check (no account needed):  
• Look at your usual stores’ ads and note only the things you buy often: produce, meat, dairy, pantry basics, and household items.  
• Check for “loss leaders” (the big eye‑catching cheap items on the front page) and decide if any fit your week’s meals.  
• Notice any buy‑one‑get‑one or multi‑buy deals on items you already use.  
• Add only the worthwhile sale items to your list and ignore the rest.

In‑store check:  
• Scan endcaps for unadvertised discounts.  
• Look at shelf tags for temporary price drops, especially on staples.  
• Compare unit prices quickly—pick the best per‑ounce or per‑tablet option.  
• Do a fast pass by the clearance shelf or markdown cart.  
• Grab store‑brand if it’s still cheaper than the sale price of name brands.

Alternate quick lines for cashiers:  
• “I’m skipping accounts today, but I’m good to check out.”  
• “No loyalty number for me, thanks—just rung up as is.”  
• “I don’t use rewards programs, but I’ll take any standard sale price.”

Short, repeatable, and no accounts needed.
