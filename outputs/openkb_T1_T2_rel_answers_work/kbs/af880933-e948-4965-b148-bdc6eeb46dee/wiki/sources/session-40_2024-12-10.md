# Conversation session 40

**User:** I’ve been thinking about switching up where and when I do my runs—do you think running on softer surfaces (like trails or sand) is actually better for your joints, or does it just trade one set of problems for another?

**Assistant:** Softer surfaces can feel nicer on your joints because they reduce impact a bit, but they’re not automatically “better”—they just shift the stress around.

Trails, for example, are easier on impact but ask more of your stabilizing muscles and ankles because the ground is uneven. Sand is even softer, but it can overload your calves and Achilles if you’re not used to it. Pavement is the opposite: higher impact, but predictable and stable.

Most runners do best with a mix. Soft surfaces reduce repetitive pounding, and firmer ones help you maintain good mechanics and strength. As long as you ease into any big change, you usually get the benefits without the problems.

**User:** I’ve started timing my morning runs with the low tide; the wet, packed sand is so much better for my joints, even if the ocean breeze leaves a constant film of salt on my glasses. Given that, how would you suggest I ease into more sand running without aggravating my calves/Achilles—should I alternate days with pavement or trails, and are there any specific strength or mobility drills you’d prioritize?

**Assistant:** If the packed sand already feels good on your joints, the main thing is just giving your calves and Achilles time to adapt. Alternating days with pavement or trails is a good idea, not because one is “better,” but because it keeps your lower legs from getting hit with the same kind of load every day. You could start by making only a small part of each run on sand and gradually lengthen that section week by week.

For strength work, calf raises done slowly through a full range help a lot, both straight‑leg and bent‑knee so you hit the Achilles from different angles. Light eccentric heel drops off a step are great too, as long as they’re pain‑free. Mobility‑wise, gentle ankle circles and a relaxed calf stretch after runs are usually enough. The key is small, steady increases and paying attention to any morning tightness—that’s often the first sign you need to back off a little.

**User:** Can you give me a concrete 4‑week progression (like % of each run on packed sand vs pavement/trails, plus how many strength sessions per week), and what “warning signs” would tell me to hold steady or cut back? Also—any practical tips for dealing with the salt film on my glasses mid‑run?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple four‑week structure that stays flexible rather than prescriptive. In week one, keep sand to about a quarter of each run and do two short strength sessions. In week two, bump the sand section to roughly one‑third and keep the same strength work. By week three, you can move to about half your run on sand with two or three strength sessions depending on how your legs feel. In week four, try sixty percent on sand if everything has been smooth so far, but don’t force it.

If you notice morning Achilles stiffness that lasts more than a few minutes, a feeling of tugging or pinching in the tendon during runs, or calves that never quite loosen after an easy day, treat that as a cue to stay at your current level or drop back a bit.

For strength, keep it simple: slow calf raises both straight‑leg and bent‑knee, plus very gentle eccentric heel drops as long as they’re painless. Ten to fifteen minutes is plenty.

As for the salt film, the easiest fixes are keeping a tiny bit of water in a soft flask for a quick rinse, or giving your lenses a swipe with a shirt hem that you’ve already dampened before the run so it doesn’t smear. Some runners also use an anti‑fog/anti‑smudge wipe before heading out; it won’t stop the salt entirely, but it makes mid‑run cleaning easier.

**User:** This is helpful—can you lay that 4‑week plan out as a weekly schedule (e.g., 4 runs/week with which days are sand-heavy vs firm), and include exact sets/reps for the calf/Achilles work? Also, if I’m mostly on wet packed sand at low tide, is it worth worrying about the slight slope/camber, and how would you manage that?

**Assistant:** You can structure the four weeks without turning it into a rigid chart. Picture a week with four runs: an early‑week easy run, a mid‑week steady run, a weekend longer run, and one shorter recovery run wherever it fits your life. In week one, let only the early‑week run touch the sand, maybe the last quarter of that outing, and keep the others on firmer surfaces. In week two, shift your mid‑week run so that roughly a third of it is on packed sand while the other runs stay firm. In week three, let both the early‑week and weekend runs include about half their distance on sand. By week four, make the weekend run the “sand‑heavy” one at around sixty percent, and let the other three hover at whatever level from week three felt easy.

For strength work, aim for two to three sessions each week. A simple sequence works well: three slow sets of ten straight‑leg calf raises, three slow sets of ten bent‑knee calf raises, and two or three sets of eight gentle eccentric heel drops on each side, all pain‑free. You don’t need more than ten to fifteen minutes total.

The shoreline camber is worth paying mild attention to, especially if you always run in the same direction. If the tide allows, switch directions halfway through or alternate which days you run closest to the water versus slightly higher on the firmer band. The small shifts in angle help keep loads even across your ankles and Achilles.
