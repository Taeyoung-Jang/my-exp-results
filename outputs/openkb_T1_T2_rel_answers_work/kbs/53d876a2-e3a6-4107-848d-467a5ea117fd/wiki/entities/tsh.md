## TSH (Thyroid Stimulating Hormone)

TSH is a pituitary‑derived hormone that regulates the activity of the [[entities/thyroid]]. It is the standard first‑line laboratory test used to screen for thyroid imbalance; the result is reported as a concentration (often in mIU/L).

### Key points from [[summaries/session-36_2025-08-15]]
- **Screening role**: In the discussed conversation, TSH is identified as the primary lab to order when thyroid symptoms are suspected.
- **Interpretation of borderline results**: A mildly elevated TSH with a normal [[entities/free-t4]] is described as *subclinical hypothyroidism* and may not require immediate medication. Treatment decisions hinge on symptom severity, trend of TSH over time, presence of thyroid antibodies, pregnancy intentions, or cardiac concerns – topics covered under [[concepts/subclinical-hypothyroidism]].
- **Follow‑up timing**: After adjusting a dose of [[entities/levothyroxine]], repeat TSH (and free T4) is typically ordered six to eight weeks later to allow levels to stabilize. Early re‑testing can be requested if red‑flag symptoms (e.g., new shortness of breath) arise.
- **Monitoring & tracking**: Patients are encouraged to log symptoms, weight changes, heart rate, and blood pressure as part of a broader [[concepts/health-tracking]] and [[concepts/weight-monitoring]] strategy, which helps the [[entities/endocrinologist]] interpret TSH trends.

### Clinical use
- **High TSH** suggests hypothyroidism (under‑active thyroid).
- **Low TSH** suggests hyperthyroidism (over‑active thyroid).
- Results are interpreted together with free T4, free T3, and sometimes antibody tests.

### Practical advice
- Keep a consistent log of medication timing (see [[concepts/levothyroxine-adherence]]) and symptom patterns to provide context for TSH results.
- Discuss any rapid changes in breathing, heart rhythm, or swelling with your [[entities/endocrinologist]] promptly, as these may warrant earlier lab assessment.
