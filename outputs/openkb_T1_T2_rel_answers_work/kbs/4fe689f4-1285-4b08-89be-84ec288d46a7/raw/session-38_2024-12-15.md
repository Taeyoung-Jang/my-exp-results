**User:** I’m trying to figure out if I should start wearing a watch again or just keep going without one. At the pool this morning, I realized I’ve gotten used to having nothing on my wrists anymore—it’s been months since I last strapped anything on there. Can you help me think through a few good options (like going back to a simple analog watch vs a fitness tracker vs nothing), and what pros/cons I might be missing for day-to-day life and workouts?

**Assistant:** It really comes down to what you want your wrist to *do* for you in daily life. Since you’ve already gotten comfortable with wearing nothing, it’s worth treating that as a real option rather than a default you need to “fix.”

If you went back to a simple analog watch, the main appeal is that it’s low‑maintenance and feels more like a small piece of style than a gadget. It tells the time, disappears into your routine, and doesn’t nag you. The downside is that it won’t give you anything for workouts beyond maybe being something you notice on your wrist again, which you might not love after going without one for months.

A fitness tracker gives you very different value. If you care about logging swims, runs, sleep, or general activity, it can be surprisingly motivating. It also gives you instant access to pace, heart rate, and timers if that matters for your pool sessions or other workouts. The tradeoff is that it’s another device to charge, it can feel a bit needy with notifications, and you have to be okay with having tech strapped to you again.

Staying with nothing is honestly underrated. If you like the feeling of unencumbered wrists and you’re not missing any data or time-checking convenience, then you’ve already discovered the biggest benefit: freedom. The only real drawback is that you might need to pull out your phone slightly more often, and you’ll have no easy way to track workouts unless you use some other method.

If you think about what you missed (or didn’t miss) during these months without a watch, that usually points you in the right direction. If you didn’t miss tracking data, you might not need a device. If you *did* sometimes wish you had a timer or swim metrics, then a lightweight tracker might justify itself. If all you want is to know the time without pulling out your phone, analog is the easiest, most unobtrusive solution.

You can also try wearing something again for a week to see if it still feels natural or if it just annoys you now. That tends to answer the question quickly.

**User:** If I went the fitness tracker route, what are the most useful features for swimming specifically (lap counting, heart rate, intervals, etc.), and what are the common annoyances? Also, is there a “minimal” tracker that won’t feel like I’m wearing a tiny phone on my wrist?

**Assistant:** For swimming, the features people actually end up using the most are automatic lap counting, distance and pace tracking, and a simple interval timer you can hit without fumbling. Optical heart‑rate in the water is hit‑or‑miss on most devices, so it’s nice when it works but not something to rely on. What usually matters more is how quickly the watch locks onto what stroke you’re doing, how well it handles push‑offs, and how accurate it is about turns.

The annoyances tend to be things like the screen not responding well with wet hands, the device miscounting laps when your stroke isn’t perfectly consistent, or having to scroll through menus while your hands are cold or wet. Charging is also a minor pain if you swim a lot, and some trackers feel bulky or drag a bit against the water.

If you want something minimal, there are trackers that behave more like quiet little data loggers than smartwatches. Models like the Fitbit Inspire series or the older Garmin Swim 2 style of devices stay very slim, don’t scream for attention, and mainly just record your workouts. They don’t feel like wearing a tiny phone the way an Apple Watch or full Garmin smartwatch does. They’re basically just a light band with swim metrics attached.

If you want the smallest, least intrusive option that still tracks laps and distance well, look for something narrow, with physical buttons instead of a touchscreen, and very few “smart” features. Those tend to disappear on your wrist much more easily in and out of the pool.

**User:** One thing: I really don’t want to buy something that turns into a distraction or guilt machine. How do I set up a swim-capable tracker so it stays “quiet” (no notifications, minimal prompts), but still gives me lap/distance and a basic interval/rest timer? **Assistant:** You can keep a tracker very quiet if you set it up right. On both Garmin and Fitbit, you can turn off all phone notifications, disable “move” or “steps” reminders, and shut down things like daily goals, bedtime nudges, and stress prompts. Once those are off, the device basically becomes a silent sensor that only lights up when you start a swim or glance at the screen. You’ll still get lap and distance tracking automatically, and you can manually start a simple swim workout that includes rest intervals without the watch trying to “coach” you.

Between the Garmin Swim 2 and a slim Fitbit Inspire, the Garmin is actually the less naggy option. It’s designed around workouts rather than lifestyle prompts, and it’s much easier to pare it down to just tracking swims. Fitbit devices tend to push goals, badges, and nudges by default, and although you can turn most of that off, they still have a bit more of a wellness‑reminder personality. The Swim 2 feels more like a quiet tool: it does its job and stays out of your way.

**User:** If I go with the Garmin Swim 2, can you give me a step-by-step “quiet setup” checklist (watch + app settings) to disable nags/notifications and keep it focused on pool swims and a simple interval/rest timer? Also, what’s the simplest way to set up an interval set like 10×100 with 20s rest on that watch?

**Assistant:** You can keep it quiet without using a formal bullet list. Here’s a clean walkthrough you can follow.

Start on the watch by going into the settings menu, opening the phone category, and turning off all smart notifications. While you’re there, switch off “Move Alerts” and any other activity reminders. Then go into the general system settings and set the watch to only use vibration for actual button presses or timers, not alerts. After that, open the activity profiles and trim the list down so the only one you really see is Pool Swim.

In the Garmin Connect app, open the device settings page and turn off call, text, and app notifications. Disable the daily goals, intensity alerts, and anything labeled as “reminders” or “insights.” You can also turn off things like “Body Battery” or stress tracking if you don’t want the watch to surface suggestions tied to those. Once all of that is off, the Swim 2 behaves like a quiet swim tool that only wakes up when you start a workout or check the time.

For a set like ten one‑hundreds on twenty seconds rest, the simplest method is to skip creating a structured workout and just use the watch’s built‑in interval button. Start a Pool Swim, swim your hundred, hit the lap button at the wall, and the watch automatically moves into a rest segment. When your twenty seconds are up, hit the lap button again to start the next repeat. It keeps track of reps, splits, and rest times without you having to pre‑program anything.
