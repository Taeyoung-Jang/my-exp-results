**User:** I’m trying to sanity-check a few things after a hardware change and could use your help figuring out what to test and what settings to tweak. I spent most of Sunday letting the IT shop finish the workstation refresh—turns out the bottleneck was the old integrated graphics, so they moved my work over to a proper graphics card and a beefier power supply. Now I want to make sure everything’s actually using the new GPU (Windows + apps like Photoshop/Premiere/Blender), and that I’m not missing any obvious driver/control panel settings. Can you give me a quick checklist of what to verify (Device Manager, BIOS, power settings, GPU selection per app, benchmarks) and any common gotchas after this kind of upgrade?

**Assistant:** You can do a quick pass through the usual spots without getting too deep into technical tweaking. Start in Windows by opening Device Manager and confirming that the new GPU shows up under Display adapters with no warning icons; if you still see the old integrated graphics listed as the active device, something’s wrong. Then open the GPU manufacturer’s control panel (NVIDIA Control Panel or AMD Adrenalin) and check that the drivers are current and that the system is defaulting to the discrete GPU rather than auto‑selecting the integrated one.

It’s also worth glancing at the BIOS just long enough to confirm that any “Primary Display” or “Integrated Graphics” option is set to favor the PCIe GPU or is set to auto with the integrated graphics disabled when a discrete card is present. You don’t need to change anything else there.

Once Windows is definitely using the new GPU, make sure your creative apps are pointed at it. In Windows Settings under System > Display > Graphics, you can assign each app (Photoshop, Premiere, Blender) to “High performance,” which forces them to use the discrete GPU. Inside each app, look for its own GPU acceleration toggle—Adobe apps show a renderer choice in their performance or playback settings, and Blender lets you pick the compute device in its preferences.

A quick sanity check with something lightweight like GPU‑Z, Task Manager’s Performance tab, or a simple benchmark can confirm that the GPU is actually doing work when you load a project. If the GPU sits at 0 percent while the CPU spikes, you know something’s still not routed correctly.

The most common hiccups after this kind of upgrade are missing or stale drivers, power settings stuck on a low‑power mode, displays still connected to the motherboard video port instead of the GPU’s ports, or apps clinging to “Auto” renderer settings that default back to the integrated graphics. Once those are squared away, you should be good to go.

**User:** Can you get more specific on the “displays connected to the motherboard vs GPU” part—what symptoms would I see if a monitor is plugged into the wrong port, and is there an easy way in Windows to confirm which GPU is driving each display? Also, if the integrated graphics still shows up in Device Manager, is it better to disable it there or in BIOS?

**Assistant:** If a monitor is plugged into the motherboard instead of the GPU, the most obvious sign is that the system will behave as if the new graphics card isn’t doing anything. You’ll usually notice that the desktop feels sluggish, GPU‑accelerated apps won’t show any speed‑up, and Task Manager will show almost no activity on the discrete GPU even when you open something that should tax it. Sometimes certain resolutions or refresh rates also won’t be available because the integrated graphics can’t drive them.

A quick way to confirm which GPU is driving a display is to open Task Manager, go to the Performance tab, and then click on each GPU listed. At the top of the window, Windows shows which displays are attached to that GPU. If your monitor shows up only under “GPU 0 Intel…” and not under “GPU 1 NVIDIA/AMD…,” then that monitor is plugged into the wrong place.

If the integrated graphics still appears in Device Manager, it’s generally cleaner to disable it in the BIOS rather than in Windows. Disabling it in BIOS prevents any chance of Windows re‑enabling it after an update, and it avoids weird resource conflicts. If the BIOS doesn’t offer a clear option to disable it, leaving it enabled is usually fine as long as your displays are connected to the graphics card.

**User:** One more thing: if I can’t (or don’t want to) disable the iGPU in BIOS, is there any downside to leaving it enabled but making sure all monitors are plugged into the discrete card? I’ve heard about “multi-monitor” iGPU settings and also about Quick Sync—could leaving the iGPU on actually be beneficial for Premiere/Media Encoder, or does it just introduce more chances for apps to pick the wrong GPU?

**Assistant:** There’s no real downside to leaving the iGPU enabled as long as all displays are hooked to the discrete card. In that setup, Windows will just treat the iGPU as an extra, unused device unless an app specifically looks for it.

For Adobe apps, keeping the iGPU active can actually be useful. Intel Quick Sync can speed up H.264/H.265 encoding and decoding, and Premiere/Media Encoder will happily use it alongside your discrete GPU. That doesn’t interfere with GPU acceleration for effects or playback—the dGPU still handles the heavy lifting as long as your monitors are plugged into it.

The only “gotcha” is that a few apps default to whatever GPU Windows labels as GPU 0. If your iGPU happens to be GPU 0, you might occasionally see an app auto‑select the wrong one. You can override this easily in the app’s settings or in Windows’ Graphics settings, and once you force it to the high‑performance GPU, it tends to stay that way.

So unless you’re troubleshooting or trying to simplify the system, leaving the iGPU enabled is perfectly fine and can even be a net win for video workflows.

**User:** Got it. If I leave the iGPU enabled specifically for Quick Sync, what’s the best way to confirm Premiere/Media Encoder is actually using it (and not falling back to software), and at the same time confirm effects/playback are still using the discrete GPU? Also, any recommended Windows/NVIDIA settings to reduce the chance that apps “stick” to GPU 0?

**Assistant:** In Premiere and Media Encoder, the easiest confirmation is during export. Start an H.264 or H.265 render, then open Task Manager and watch the Intel GPU. If Quick Sync is active, you’ll see its video‑encode or video‑decode engines jump noticeably. At the same time, the discrete GPU should show load on its 3D/compute engines when effects or GPU‑accelerated transitions are involved. If the Intel GPU stays flat and the CPU spikes, you’re not using Quick Sync.

For playback and effects, Premiere’s Project Settings panel will tell you which renderer it’s using. If it says CUDA or OpenCL for your NVIDIA card, then the discrete GPU is in charge. You’ll also see the NVIDIA GPU working in Task Manager whenever you scrub, play a timeline with GPU effects, or use the Lumetri panel.

To avoid apps sticking to GPU 0, the simplest approach is to go into Windows’ Graphics settings and manually assign Premiere, Media Encoder, and anything else you care about to “High performance,” which forces the discrete GPU. In the NVIDIA Control Panel, setting “Preferred graphics processor” to the high‑performance GPU under the global settings achieves the same thing. Once both of those are in place, apps almost never wander back to the iGPU unless they’re explicitly coded to.
