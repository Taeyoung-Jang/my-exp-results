**User:** I’m trying to put together a strength-training routine that feels athletic but also sustainable long-term. If you were designing a 3-day-per-week program for someone who wants stronger shoulders and upper body without living in the gym, what would you prioritize and why?

**Assistant:** I’d focus on making each day cover a few big movements that give you the most return for your time, then layer in just enough shoulder‑focused work to steadily build strength without beating you up.

The backbone would be pushing, pulling, and some form of overhead work. Horizontal presses and rows build a lot of upper‑body strength in a balanced way, and they support shoulder health by keeping the front‑to‑back strength ratio in check. Overhead presses or landmine variations give you that athletic shoulder strength without requiring huge training volume. I’d make sure you’re getting at least one solid overhead movement each session, even if the load varies.

From there, I’d add a couple of short “shoulder health” finishers—things like controlled raises, external rotations, or light stability work. These don’t take long, but they make your shoulders feel better under heavier training and help you stay consistent week to week.

The idea is that each workout feels like a full session without being long: one heavier push, one heavier pull, one overhead pattern, then a brief shoulder‑stability piece. That gives you the athletic feel, steady progress, and low wear‑and‑tear you’re aiming for.

**User:** Overhead work isn’t really an option for me. Can you redesign that 3‑day plan to build strong shoulders and upper body while avoiding overhead pressing entirely, and suggest good progressions?

**Assistant:** You can build strong, athletic shoulders without putting anything overhead if you lean into smart horizontal pushing and pulling, angled presses, and targeted shoulder‑health work.

I’d structure each day around one main press, one main pull, and one shoulder‑focused accessory. Instead of overhead pressing, you can use landmine presses kept low, high‑incline presses, dips, push‑ups, and fly or raise variations that don’t take your arm into a painful overhead position. For pulling, rows in different angles do most of the heavy lifting for shoulder strength and long‑term joint stability. Rotator‑cuff work and controlled lateral‑raise variations quietly build the strength you’d normally chase with overhead movements.

A simple weekly flow could look like this: one day built around a heavier horizontal press like a bench or weighted push‑up, paired with row variations and a lateral‑raise pattern. Another day built around an incline press or landmine press, paired with a verticalish pull like a pull‑down or assisted pull‑up and then some rear‑delt or external‑rotation work. The third day can center on dips or a close‑grip press, combined with a different row pattern and a slightly higher‑rep shoulder‑stability exercise.

For progressions, increase load slowly on your main press and main pull, or add reps before adding weight. On the shoulder accessories, progress by tightening control and adding small weight bumps rather than chasing big jumps. As long as those patterns stay pain‑free, you’ll get steady strength gains without ever going overhead.

**User:** Can you lay that out as an actual 3‑day template with specific exercises, sets/reps, and what to do week to week to progress—ideally with options if certain movements bother my shoulders?

**Assistant:** Here’s a clean three‑day template you can follow without using overhead work, written in a way that stays easy to read without formal lists.

For Day 1, build the session around a heavier horizontal press. Something like a flat dumbbell press for three to four sets of five to eight reps works well. Pair that with a chest‑supported row for the same sets and reps. Finish with a lateral‑raise pattern—either dumbbells or cables—for two or three sets of 12 to 15. If standard pressing bothers your shoulders, switch the press to a neutral‑grip dumbbell press or a decline press, both of which are usually friendlier on the joint.

For Day 2, use an incline or landmine‑style press as the main lift. Three or four sets of six to ten reps is perfect. Match it with a verticalish pull such as a pull‑down or assisted pull‑up for three or four sets of six to ten. Wrap with rear‑delt work, like reverse flys or face‑pull‑style motions, for two or three sets of 12 to 15. If incline angles irritate your shoulders, drop the bench angle or switch entirely to the landmine, which tends to be very forgiving.

For Day 3, center the workout on dips or a close‑grip press for three to four sets of five to eight. Pair that with a different rowing angle—single‑arm dumbbell rows or a cable row—for three or four sets of eight to ten. Finish with a controlled external‑rotation drill or very light Y‑raises for two or three sets of 12 to 15. If dips bother you, keep them shallow or swap in a close‑grip neutral press.

To progress week to week, try adding one rep to each main lift until you reach the top of the rep range, then increase weight slightly and drop back to the lower end of the range. On the shoulder‑accessory work, increase weight only when the sets feel genuinely easy and controlled; slow, steady jumps go a long way. If something feels sharp or sketchy, adjust the angle, grip, or load rather than pushing through it. This lets you accumulate strength without aggravating the joint.

**User:** I’ve got chronic tendonitis in my shoulders, so overhead lifting is completely out of the question for me. With that in mind, can you tweak the 3‑day template so it’s tendonitis‑friendly (exercise swaps, grips/angles, and any “do/don’t” pain rules), and also tell me how you’d handle progression if I have flare‑up weeks?

**Assistant:** You can keep the same overall structure but make it friendlier by choosing movements that keep your elbows a little closer to your body, avoiding long levers, and staying inside pain‑free ranges. The goal is to load the upper body without provoking the irritated tissue.

For Day 1, use a neutral‑grip dumbbell press on a flat or slight decline bench. Keep your elbows tucked about 30–45 degrees from your sides, and stop the range where your shoulder feels stable. Pair that with a chest‑supported row using a neutral or slightly underhand grip, since that usually feels smoother on irritated shoulders. For the shoulder finisher, use cable lateral raises with your arm slightly bent and raised only to the height that feels clean; most people do better stopping a little below parallel.

For Day 2, stick with a low‑incline press at a very gentle angle or go straight to a landmine press kept low and forward. Both let you train that “upward” pattern without true overhead movement. Match that with pull‑downs using a neutral‑grip attachment, keeping your elbows moving in front of your torso rather than flaring out. Finish with rear‑delt cable work, keeping the range small and controlled.

For Day 3, a close‑grip neutral bench press or a push‑up variation with handles is usually the calmest pressing option. Pair it with single‑arm cable or dumbbell rows where you can fine‑tune the path to avoid any pinch. Wrap up with external‑rotation work using a cable set low so you can keep your elbow tucked and avoid any stretch that triggers the tendon.

A simple pain rule makes this sustainable: mild discomfort is okay, but anything sharp or that lingers after the set means you shorten the range, reduce load, or change the angle. Nothing should feel worse five minutes after the set than during it.

For progression, keep things very gentle. Add reps first, and only when the movement feels consistently good for at least two weeks do you bump weight a little. When you hit a flare‑up week, don’t stop training—just cut the weights to about half, slow the tempo, and stay strictly pain‑free. Once the flare settles, pick up where you left off rather than chasing the numbers you used before. This lets you maintain momentum without irritating the tendon again.

**User:** Can you put that tendonitis-friendly plan into a clear weekly layout (Day 1/2/3) with exact exercise order, sets/reps, and rest times—and include 1–2 alternative swaps per exercise in case something flares up?

**Assistant:** Here’s a clear week‑to‑week layout written in plain sentences so it’s easy to follow without relying on lists. Each day flows in order from first exercise to last, with simple swaps you can jump to if your shoulder feels irritated.

For Day 1, start with a neutral‑grip dumbbell press on a flat or slight‑decline bench. Do three to four sets of five to eight reps, resting about two minutes between sets. If this bothers your shoulder, switch to a decline dumbbell press or a neutral‑grip machine chest press. After that, move to a chest‑supported row using a neutral or slightly underhand grip for three to four sets of six to ten reps, resting around ninety seconds. If that position feels off, use a single‑arm cable row or a landmine row with your torso supported on your free hand. Finish the session with cable lateral raises for two or three sets of twelve to fifteen reps, resting about a minute. If even that causes discomfort, keep the raise range smaller or use a light dumbbell raise in the scapular plane (arms about thirty degrees forward).

For Day 2, begin with a very low‑incline neutral‑grip dumbbell press or a low‑angle landmine press. Work three to four sets of six to ten reps with about ninety seconds of rest. If either version causes a flare, drop the incline even lower or switch entirely to a low‑angled machine press with a neutral handle. Follow it with a neutral‑grip pull‑down for three to four sets of six to ten reps, resting ninety seconds. If that irritates your shoulder at the bottom, use a high‑angle cable row or a band‑assisted pull‑up with a neutral grip. Finish with rear‑delt cable work, such as a cross‑body rear‑delt pull or reverse‑cable fly, for two or three sets of twelve to fifteen, resting a minute. If that feels sketchy, shorten the range or switch to a prone bench rear‑delt raise with very light dumbbells.

For Day 3, start with a close‑grip neutral bench press or push‑ups using neutral‑grip handles for three to four sets of five to eight reps, resting about two minutes. If pressing bothers you that day, use a machine chest press with the handles set narrow. Follow this with a single‑arm cable row or dumbbell row for three to four sets of eight to ten reps, with about ninety seconds of rest. If that row angle feels off, switch to a chest‑supported row with the bench set slightly higher or a low‑cable row where you can freely adjust your elbow path. Finish with external‑rotation cable work for two or three sets of twelve to fifteen per side, resting about a minute. If that movement catches, keep the elbow even closer to your body or switch to a light band where resistance is easier to control.

For pain rules, keep all movements inside a range that feels stable, controlled, and free of sharp sensations. Mild discomfort that fades immediately after a set is fine, but anything that stays or increases means you shorten the range or switch to one of the alternate options.

For progression, increase reps first. Once you hit the top of the rep range for two straight weeks with clean, pain‑free form, bump the weight slightly and drop back to the lower end of the range. On flare‑up weeks, don’t abandon the routine; cut the load to about half, slow the tempo, keep everything pain‑free, and resume normal loading only once the shoulder feels calm again. This keeps progress steady without aggravating the tendon.
