**User:** I just got put on an on-call rotation at work and I’m trying to figure out how people make it sustainable. Do you have any practical strategies for setting boundaries, managing sleep, and not burning out—especially when you’re the “first responder” for incidents?

**Assistant:** It’s definitely possible to make on‑call sustainable, but it takes some deliberate habits. What helps most people is treating on‑call like a predictable mode your life shifts into, rather than something chaotic that just happens to you.

One of the biggest things is negotiating clear expectations with your team. You should know exactly what counts as a page, what your response window is, and what you’re *not* responsible for. The clearer the rules, the less mental load you carry when you’re off‑call or asleep. If the expectations are vague, push for written guidelines or a runbook so you don’t feel like you have to scramble or overperform.

Sleep management tends to get easier once you build routines around being on‑call. Many people go to bed a little earlier during their rotation or plan low‑key evenings so they’re not exhausted if they get woken up. If you get paged in the middle of the night, try to return to sleep right away rather than staying up to “wind down”; your only real job in that moment is to get rested again. Short naps the next day can also make a huge difference.

Boundaries are mostly about deciding what changes when you’re on‑call. Some people avoid alcohol, long drives, or commitments where they can’t step away; others take their laptop everywhere but draw a line around not doing extra project work that week. Pick the version that protects your sanity and communicate it so you don’t feel guilty sticking to it.

To avoid burnout long‑term, pay attention to how often you’re getting paged and how often incidents repeat. If the same issue wakes you up two or three times, that’s a sign your team needs to prioritize reliability work or automate a workaround. On‑call shouldn’t be a life sentence of firefighting. The healthiest teams treat pages as signals that something needs to be fixed permanently, not as a normal weekly occurrence.

And finally, make sure there’s actual recovery time after your shift ends. Even one quiet day off from being “on alert” mode can make the rest of your week feel normal again.

**User:** Ever since they moved me onto the rotating on-call schedule, my phone starts lighting up the second I badge in—half the time I’m already triaging issues before I’ve even set my bag down. How do I set a boundary around that “immediate triage” expectation without looking like I’m not pulling my weight?

**Assistant:** You can set that boundary without looking unhelpful if you frame it as wanting to be effective, not less available.

A simple approach is to talk with your lead or whoever runs the rotation and say something like, “When I’m walking in the door, I don’t have the right context yet, so I end up responding half‑blind. Can we treat my shift as starting once I’m actually logged in?” That keeps the focus on quality of response, not on avoiding work.

If you want something softer, you can tell your team you need a few minutes after arriving to get your environment up and check the dashboard before jumping into alerts. Most people will accept that as reasonable—you’re still on‑call, just not required to literally respond while you’re still unzipping your backpack.

You can also shift the dynamic by consistently giving better, clearer responses once you’re at your desk. When people see that your first five minutes make your whole on‑call shift smoother, they tend to stop expecting “instant” triage the moment you walk in.

None of this reads as slacking; it reads as wanting to do the job properly. And you’ll feel a lot less like you’re being ambushed the moment you badge through the door.
