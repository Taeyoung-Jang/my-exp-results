# Knife Steel Selection

A concise guide to evaluating and picking the right steel for kitchen knives, balancing edge retention, ease of sharpening, and maintenance requirements.

## Overview
Choosing a knife steel is one of the most impactful decisions when building a reliable kitchen knife collection. The steel determines how long the edge holds, how easy the knife is to sharpen, and how much care it needs to stay rust‑free.

## Common Stainless Options
- **VG‑10** – High‑carbon stainless that offers excellent edge retention while remaining relatively easy to sharpen. Suitable for both Western and Japanese styles.
- **AUS‑8** – A mid‑range Japanese stainless steel that provides a good balance of toughness and sharpenability.
- **German stainless (56–58 HRC)** – Typical of many Western chef’s knives; offers durability and easy maintenance, though edge retention is modest compared to premium Japanese steels.

These steels are praised in the source discussion for being “mid‑range stainless steels that hold an edge well but aren’t so hard that they chip easily.” They are ideal for cooks who want a dependable knife without specialized sharpening tools.

## High‑Carbon Considerations
High‑carbon steels (e.g., plain carbon or carbon‑core alloys) deliver a razor‑sharp edge and superior cutting performance, but they demand more diligent care:
- Keep the blade **bone‑dry** after each use.
- Apply a thin coat of food‑safe mineral oil periodically to inhibit oxidation.
- Perform **regular light touch‑ups** on a fine sharpening stone (3000–6000 grit) to avoid corrosion and maintain a keen edge.

The source emphasizes that high‑carbon knives “reward frequent, gentle maintenance far more than infrequent heavy sharpening.”

## Choosing for a Starter Kit
When assembling a starter kit (see [[concepts/kitchen-knife-starter-kit]]), a stainless steel such as VG‑10 or a German 56‑58 HRC alloy is practical: it offers reasonable edge life, straightforward sharpening, and lower rust risk, which is helpful for beginners.

If the user already owns high‑carbon knives and is comfortable with extra upkeep, a high‑carbon or carbon‑core gyuto can serve as a “main workhorse” for superior performance.

## Maintenance & Sharpening
The sharpening routine ties directly to steel type:
- **Stainless steels**: fine stone (3000–6000 grit) for routine touch‑ups; medium stone (~1000 grit) for full resharpening.
- **High‑carbon steels**: same grit hierarchy but performed more frequently to avoid rust buildup.

Refer to [[concepts/sharpening-stone-grits]] for detailed grit recommendations.
