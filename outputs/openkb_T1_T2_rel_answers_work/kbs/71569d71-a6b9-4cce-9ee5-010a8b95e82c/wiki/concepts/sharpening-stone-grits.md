# Sharpening Stone Grits

**Definition**: The selection of abrasive stone grits used to maintain and restore the edge of a kitchen knife, ranging from coarse to fine depending on the sharpening task.

## Typical Grit Progression
- **Medium stone (~1000 grit)** – Used for full sharpening or edge resetting. It removes enough material to reshape a dull edge while keeping the blade geometry intact.
- **Fine stone (3000–6000 grit)** – Ideal for routine touch‑ups and final polishing. A fine stone restores sharpness without removing excessive steel, extending the interval between full sharpenings.

The source conversation recommends a two‑step routine: start on a ~1000 grit medium stone to rebalance the edge, then finish on a 3000–6000 grit fine stone for a keen, polished edge.

## Why Grit Choice Matters
- Coarser grits (below 1000) are best for repairing chips or re‑profiling heavily damaged edges, but they are not needed for regular home‑cook maintenance.
- Fine grits maintain a razor‑sharp edge while minimizing metal loss, which is especially important for high‑carbon steels that can be more prone to corrosion if the edge is left jagged.

## Integration with Knife Care
- Pairing the appropriate grit with regular drying, oiling, and storage (see [[concepts/high-carbon-knife-care]] and [[concepts/leather-roll-storage]]) maximizes edge longevity.
- Selecting the right steel (e.g., VG‑10, AUS‑8, or carbon steels) influences how often you need to move between medium and fine stones; harder steels hold an edge longer but may require finer grit work for polishing.

## Practical Tips from the Source
- **Touch‑ups**: Keep a 3000–6000 grit stone handy for quick refinements before the knife feels noticeably dull.
- **Full sharpening**: Use a 1000 grit stone to reset the edge, then finish on the fine stone for a polished bite.
- **Frequency**: Frequent light work on the fine stone reduces the need for frequent medium‑grit sharpening.
