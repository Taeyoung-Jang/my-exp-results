**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about what actually drives people at work—like whether they’re energized by quick, visible wins or by longer-term bets that might not pay off for a while. How do you help someone figure out what kind of impact they’re really motivated by, and what signals should they look for in their day-to-day to confirm it?

**Assistant:** It usually helps to get people out of the abstract and into the patterns of their own behavior. I’d ask them to think about moments when they’ve felt genuinely energized at work—what they were doing, how quickly they saw results, and what kind of feedback mattered to them. Most people already have a “signature” response; they just haven’t named it.

A good signal is how they react to delayed payoff. If long stretches without visible progress feel draining rather than motivating, they probably need quicker, tangible wins. If they light up at the thought of building something that takes months or years, even without immediate recognition, they’re likely driven by long-term impact.

Day to day, they can watch for when time seems to move faster because they’re absorbed in the work, when they voluntarily go above the bare minimum, or when they feel a sense of pride without anyone else validating it. Those moments usually reveal the type of impact that actually fuels them.

**User:** After sitting in on the leadership meetings this quarter, I’ve realized I’m most motivated by shaping strategy and moving up into roles with broader influence, even if the impact takes longer to show up. How would you suggest I validate that this is truly what motivates me (vs. it just feeling exciting or prestigious right now), and what kinds of projects or responsibilities should I seek out to test it?

**Assistant:** A good way to validate it is to watch how you feel once the novelty fades. Strategy work often has slow feedback loops, ambiguous problems, and long stretches where nothing looks “done.” If you still feel energized when the excitement dies down and the ambiguity sets in, that’s a strong signal it’s genuine motivation rather than prestige.

You can also look at what you naturally gravitate toward. If you find yourself thinking about systems, trade‑offs, and long‑term consequences even when no one asks you to, that’s another clue. And pay attention to what drains you. If execution-heavy tasks feel like they limit your impact rather than letting you contribute, you’re probably leaning toward broader, strategic roles.

To test it, try taking on work that forces you to think beyond your immediate team—things like defining direction for a cross‑functional effort, shaping a roadmap, or advising leaders on a problem that doesn’t have a clear answer yet. Owning a problem that spans multiple quarters, where you have to bring people along and influence without authority, is especially telling. If that kind of responsibility feels satisfying rather than frustrating, you’re likely on the right path.

**User:** What are a few concrete “experiments” I could run over the next 60–90 days to test whether I actually enjoy the messy, slow-feedback parts of strategy and influence? And how should I measure whether it’s a good fit—like what specific signals (energy, outcomes, feedback) should I track?

**Assistant:** You can treat this like trying on parts of the job before you fully step into it. Over the next couple of months, you might volunteer to shape the early thinking for a cross‑team initiative, take ownership of a problem that doesn’t have a clear path yet, or draft a proposal that requires aligning several stakeholders. The point is to put yourself in situations where the work is ambiguous, the payoff is slow, and you can’t rely on quick execution wins.

As you do this, pay close attention to how you feel during the “murky middle.” Notice whether you still want to come back to the problem after a string of unclear days, whether you find yourself thinking about it even when no progress is visible, and whether influencing people energizes you or simply drains you. Track how you show up in the meetings that revolve around alignment rather than decisions, and how much initiative you take without external pressure.

If you find that you stay engaged, that you’re pulling yourself toward this work instead of pushing yourself through it, and that feedback from others mentions clarity, thought partnership, or influence rather than just execution, that’s a strong sign the strategic side genuinely fits you.

**User:** Can you give me 3–5 specific experiment ideas I could propose to my manager that fit into a normal workload (not a huge “extra project”), ideally ones that build strategic influence without formal authority? For each, include what “success” would look like and a simple way to track whether I’m energized vs. just chasing novelty.

**Assistant:** You can frame these as small, scoped opportunities rather than extra projects.

One idea is to take the lead on shaping an early problem statement for an upcoming cross‑team initiative. You’d talk to a few stakeholders, synthesize what you learn, and propose a clear definition of the problem. Success would look like people using your framing in their conversations. To track your energy, notice whether you enjoy the messy interviews and synthesis once the initial excitement wears off.

Another idea is to draft a lightweight strategy memo for your team on a topic your manager already cares about—something like risks, opportunities, or a decision that needs a clearer rationale. Success is when the memo helps clarify thinking or drives a useful discussion. Track whether writing it feels absorbing or like you’re forcing yourself through ambiguity.

A third idea is to run a small alignment conversation across two or three stakeholders who aren’t on the same page. You don’t own the project; you’re just helping unblock it. Success is when the group leaves with more clarity or shared direction. Track whether influencing without authority feels energizing or exhausting.

A fourth idea is to own a multi‑week question your manager hasn’t had time to dig into, such as evaluating a trade‑off or testing a hypothesis for next quarter’s roadmap. Success is when your manager considers your analysis credible enough to factor into decisions. Track whether you stay motivated through the slower feedback cycles.

A fifth idea is to quietly pilot a small process improvement that affects more than your immediate team—something low‑risk but cross‑functional. Success is when others adopt it or ask for it to continue. Track whether you like the behind‑the‑scenes persuasion work required to get people on board.

If you find yourself consistently leaning in once things get unclear or slow, that’s a strong sign you’re motivated by the strategic side rather than just the novelty.
