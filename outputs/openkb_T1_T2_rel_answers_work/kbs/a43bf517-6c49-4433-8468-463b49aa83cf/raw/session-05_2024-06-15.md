# Conversation session 5

Date: 2024-06-15 11:00

**User:** I’m putting together a gear plan for a couple upcoming shoots and could use a sanity check. I’ve got my own set of fixed-focal lenses now, so I don’t usually need to rent glass for jobs, but I’m debating whether it’s still worth renting a zoom (like a 24-70 or 70-200) for run-and-gun coverage or just sticking to primes and moving more. How do you decide when a zoom rental is actually worth it, and what situations tend to justify it?

**Assistant:** I usually think of zoom rentals as worth it when the pace of the shoot makes changing lenses feel like a liability rather than a creative choice. If you know you’ll be moving constantly, covering unpredictable action, or working in tight spaces where you can’t physically re‑position, a zoom earns its keep fast.  

If the job is more controlled, with time to reset between angles, primes tend to be the better experience both aesthetically and ergonomically, and you won’t miss the zoom much.  

The situations that most often justify a zoom rental are things like event coverage, doc or corporate b‑roll where moments are fleeting, or any multi-location day where you’re racing daylight and can’t keep stopping to swap lenses. Outside of that, sticking with your primes is usually the more satisfying choice.

**User:** Got it. For something like a small corporate doc day (interviews + b-roll in an office) where I’m mostly solo, would you lean toward renting a 24-70, a 70-200, or neither if I already have, say, 24/35/50/85 primes? Also, how much does stabilization (lens IS vs in-body) factor into that decision for handheld b-roll?

**Assistant:** For a small corporate doc day where you’re solo, a 24–70 is usually the only zoom that genuinely changes the experience. It covers the range you’ll use most for office b‑roll, lets you shift quickly between wide context and tighter details, and saves you a lot of lens swaps in cramped spaces. A 70–200 is great for events or long‑lens moments outdoors, but it rarely earns its weight in a typical office doc unless you know you’ll need distant compression shots.

If your primes are fast and you’re comfortable moving around, you can absolutely get through the day without a zoom. The 24–70 just makes the flow smoother and keeps you from constantly breaking rhythm to swap glass.

Stabilization matters most if your camera’s IBIS is only okay. Lens IS plus IBIS usually gives the cleanest handheld look for slow push‑ins or subtle moves around a subject. If your camera has strong IBIS, the difference between a stabilized zoom and your primes becomes less critical, though zooms often still look a bit smoother because of how their optical IS behaves.

So the short version: for this kind of shoot, the 24–70 is the only rental likely to noticeably improve your day, and lens IS becomes more valuable the weaker your in‑body stabilization is.

**User:** If I’m already planning to bring a small gimbal for some b-roll, does that change your recommendation on the 24–70 rental? Like, would you still rent it for speed, or would you just run primes on the gimbal and accept more swaps?

**Assistant:** Bringing a gimbal helps, but it doesn’t completely replace the convenience of a 24–70. A zoom still speeds up the whole day, especially for the off‑gimbal stuff where you’re grabbing quick handheld shots or moving from wide office context to tighter detail moments without resetting the gimbal balance.

If you’re comfortable balancing your primes quickly and you’re okay with a few extra pauses, you can definitely run the whole shoot on primes and be fine. But if you want the day to feel smoother and you want to stay in flow without constantly thinking about lens changes or rebalance time, the 24–70 still earns its keep.

So the gimbal reduces the *need* for the zoom, but it doesn’t remove the *benefit*. It just comes down to how fast you want the day to move and how much you mind swapping lenses.

**User:** Makes sense. Last piece: if I do rent the 24–70, would you prioritize f/2.8 or is f/4 usually enough for an office doc day? I’m thinking about interview lighting, background separation, and also gimbal weight/balance.

**Assistant:** For a typical office doc day, f/4 is usually enough. You’re lighting the interview anyway, so you can shape depth with distance more than aperture, and most offices don’t give you huge spaces where f/2.8 makes a dramatic difference. The lighter weight of an f/4 zoom also makes gimbal work noticeably easier.

I’d only spring for the f/2.8 version if you expect low-light pockets you can’t control, or you specifically want that slightly shallower look for b‑roll without switching to your primes. Otherwise, the f/4 keeps things simpler and you won’t miss the extra stop much.
