**Assistant:** Think of bulk fermentation as the part of the process where the dough wakes up and starts building personality. The yeast and bacteria in your starter are basically eating the sugars in the flour and releasing gas, which makes the dough expand. At the same time, the bacteria are creating mild acids that give sourdough its tang and help strengthen the gluten network so the dough feels bouncier and more elastic.

Proofing is just the later, gentler stage of the same activity. The dough has already built its structure, so it’s mostly about giving it a final rise and letting the flavors settle in. The microbes are still working, just not as energetically as they do earlier.

Acidity matters because it keeps the balance between yeast and bacteria in check. A slightly more acidic dough slows the yeast down a bit, which lets the bacteria contribute more flavor without the dough over-proofing too quickly. Too much acidity, though, can weaken the gluten and make the dough sluggish.

Temperature is basically the thermostat for the whole ecosystem. Warmer dough means faster activity and shorter timelines; cooler dough slows everything down and gives the flavor more time to develop. Bakers use that to their advantage—speed things up on a busy night or stretch things out when they want deeper flavor.

That’s really all the chemistry you need to get across: it’s a little community eating, breathing, and shaping the dough as it goes.

**Assistant:** **Sourdough Fermentation: A Simple Guide for Baking Night**

**What’s Happening in the Dough**  
During bulk fermentation, the yeast and bacteria in your starter are eating the flour’s sugars and filling the dough with tiny bubbles. They’re also creating gentle acidity that helps flavor and strengthens the dough. Proofing is the quieter, final rise where the dough settles into its shape and builds a bit more lift before baking. Temperature controls how quickly all of this happens—warm dough moves faster, cool dough slows down.

**Bulk Fermentation: What to Look For**  
• The dough feels puffier, smoother, and more relaxed.  
• You can see small bubbles under the surface.  
• It stretches more easily during folds.

**Signs It’s Not Quite Right (Bulk)**  
• Under: dough feels dense, tight, and unwilling to stretch.  
• Over: dough looks slack or watery around the edges and won’t hold shape when folded.

**Proofing: What to Look For**  
• The dough feels lighter and gently wobbly.  
• A light poke springs back slowly but doesn’t collapse.  
• The shaped loaf looks slightly domed and settled.

**Signs It’s Not Quite Right (Proof)**  
• Under: dough feels firm, springs back too quickly, and looks a bit “unfinished.”  
• Over: dough looks fragile or overly puffy; a poke leaves a dent that doesn’t come back.

**Hydration: How Wet Dough Changes the Cues**  
• High‑hydration doughs always look softer and wobblier, so they can seem “done” early even when they’re just naturally relaxed.  
• A good sign is airy softness rather than simple wetness.  
• When truly over‑fermented, these doughs turn soupy and hard to handle.

**Whole‑Grain and Rye Doughs**  
• These ferment faster because of extra nutrients, but their weaker gluten makes them feel sticky even when fermentation is going well.  
• They won’t show big bubbles; look for a gentle puff, slight dome, and a more velvety feel.  
• Over‑fermented dough turns gummy and loses structure. Under‑fermented dough stays dense and clay‑like.

This is all you need to watch for: how the dough feels, how it moves, and how it responds when you touch it. The microbes are doing the rest.
