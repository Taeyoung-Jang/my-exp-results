# Vehicle Depreciation

Vehicle depreciation refers to the decline in a vehicle's market value as it ages, accumulates mileage, and experiences wear. It is a key economic factor when evaluating whether to keep an existing car or replace it.

## Role in [[concepts/keep-vs-replace-decision]]
When performing a [[concepts/two-year-cost-framework]] analysis, depreciation is treated as an "on‑paper" cost for the keep scenario and as a projected loss for the replace scenario. By estimating how much value a car will lose over the analysis period, owners can compare that loss against repair expenses, loan payments, insurance differences, and fuel costs.

## Example from [[summaries/session-03_2024-06-15]]
- **Current car**: The 2016 [[entities/honda-accord-2016]] was valued at $10,000–$12,000. Over the next two years the summary assumes its value would drop to roughly $7,000–$9,000, a depreciation of $3,000–$5,000.
- **Replacement car**: A used vehicle in the $22,000–$28,000 range (e.g., a newer [[entities/toyota-camry]] or a small SUV) is expected to lose about $3,000–$5,000 of value in two years.

These figures illustrate how depreciation can be a comparable line‑item for both options, but the keep scenario often shows a smaller absolute loss because the starting value is lower.

## Factors Influencing Depreciation
- **Age and model year**: Newer models depreciate faster, especially in the first few years.
- **Mileage**: Higher annual miles accelerate value loss.
- **Condition and maintenance history**: Well‑maintained cars retain value better.
- **Market demand**: Popular segments (e.g., compact sedans like the Accord or Camry) may hold value longer.
- **Economic conditions**: Fuel prices, interest rates, and incentives can affect resale prices.

Understanding these drivers helps users provide more accurate estimates when filling out the seven data points required for the [[concepts/two-year-cost-framework]] analysis.
