# Load Estimation

**Description:** Simple guidelines for estimating the load on a residential circuit to decide if it can safely accommodate additional fixtures or outlets.

---

## Core Principles

1. **Breaker rating vs. usable continuous load**
   - 15 A breaker → comfortable continuous load ≈ **12 A**
   - 20 A breaker → comfortable continuous load ≈ **16 A**
   - Continuous load means a device that runs for ≥3 hours without cycling (e.g., lighting, space heaters).
   - Non‑continuous loads (most receptacle devices) are not counted toward the continuous limit unless they run continuously.

2. **Estimating existing usage**
   - Identify all devices on the circuit (turn breaker off, note what loses power).
   - Estimate typical current draw for each device (e.g., LED fixture ≈ 0.5 A, outlet with charger ≈ 1 A, TV ≈ 2 A).
   - Sum the draws; compare to the continuous‑load thresholds above.
   - If the sum approaches **12 A** on a 15 A circuit or **16 A** on a 20 A circuit, plan a new dedicated circuit.

3. **Safety margin**
   - Keep at least a **20 %** headroom beyond the calculated load to accommodate occasional spikes.
   - Example: 15 A circuit with 9 A estimated load → 9 A × 1.2 = 10.8 A, still comfortably below the 12 A continuous limit.

## Practical Checklist (from [[summaries/session-10_2022-06-12]])

- **Identify breaker size** (15 A vs 20 A).
- **Map the circuit** – note all lights, outlets, and appliances.
- **Calculate typical draw** using common appliance amperage values.
- **Apply continuous‑load limits** (12 A / 16 A).
- **Check for red‑flags**: multiple high‑draw devices, mixed uses (e.g., bathroom + bedroom on one circuit), or existing loads near the threshold.

If the load estimate exceeds the comfortable continuous limit or the circuit serves diverse high‑draw loads, the recommendation is to run a new circuit and obtain the appropriate permit.
