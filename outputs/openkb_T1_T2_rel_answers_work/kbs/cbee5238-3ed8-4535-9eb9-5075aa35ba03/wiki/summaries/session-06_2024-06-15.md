# Summary of session‑06_2024‑06‑15

**Main contribution:** Practical advice on how subtitles affect the experience of watching foreign films and concrete settings/ habits to minimise distraction while preserving acting and visual appreciation.

## Key ideas
- The overall impact of a foreign film comes from a blend of dialogue, acting, and cultural context rather than any single element. [[concepts/foreign-film-experience]]
- Subtitles can momentarily draw attention away from facial expressions or cinematography, but viewers quickly adapt and may even gain deeper nuance. [[concepts/subtitle-distraction]]
- Repeated exposure trains the brain to read subtitles while simultaneously processing visual cues – turning a distraction into a rhythm. 

## Practical subtitle‑setting recommendations
### TV viewing
- Font size: medium‑large (larger than strictly needed, but not dominating the lower third).
- Colour: white text with a thin black outline for contrast.
- Placement: centre‑bottom; integrates with the frame when seated farther back.
- Goal: keep text readable at a glance so eyes return quickly to actors. [[concepts/subtitle-settings]]

### Laptop viewing
- Font size: slightly smaller, suitable for close‑up screen.
- Colour: off‑white or light‑gray with a soft outline for gentler appearance.
- Placement: just above the bottom edge to avoid covering visual details.
- Goal: minimise eye travel distance while preserving clarity. [[concepts/subtitle-settings]]

## Tips to reduce “chasing” subtitles
- **Adjust size & contrast** so the text is instantly readable.
- **Sit a bit farther** from the screen when possible; this makes subtitles feel part of the image.
- **Increase exposure**: watch short bursts of subtitled content regularly to let the brain adapt. 
- **Adopt a loose gaze**: consciously avoid flipping between faces and text; let the scene and words blend together.
- **Exercise**: after each viewing session, replay a short clip, trying to focus first on the visual, then on the text, gradually merging the two.
