**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about the revision/editing stage of writing—how do you usually approach tightening a manuscript without losing your voice? Do you have a practical workflow (like checklists or passes) that actually makes the process feel manageable?

**Assistant:** I usually treat revision as a few gentle passes rather than one big overhaul. The first pass is just about clarity: I read through and look for places where I stumble, skim, or feel confused. That tells me where something needs tightening. I’m not trying to fix everything at once, just making sure the meaning is landing.

Once that feels solid, I do a pass focused on rhythm and voice. I read sections aloud and listen for anything that sounds flat, fussy, or unlike me. If a sentence feels overwritten, I trim it. If cutting something makes the voice feel weaker, I put it back or rephrase until it sounds natural again.

The last pass is more mechanical. I look for repeated words, vague phrasing, or sentences doing the same job twice. By this point the structure and voice are already set, so these edits feel low‑pressure.

Breaking it into passes keeps it manageable, and reading aloud is the thing that helps me keep the voice intact more than anything else.

**User:** That makes a lot of sense—especially separating “clarity” from “voice,” because I tend to try to do both at once and then everything turns into mush. I just got off a call with my agent—apparently the copyedits on my debut novel are due back in ten days, so my evenings are basically going to be a red pen and a pot of coffee until that’s done.  

Given that timeline, if you had to prioritize, what would your “must-do” passes be in copyedits specifically (like consistency, continuity, word choice, etc.)—and how would you keep yourself from spiraling into unnecessary rewrites when the clock’s ticking?

**Assistant:** In the copyedit stage, the safest way to hit the deadline without spiraling is to focus on the things that genuinely matter at this point and let everything else go. I’d start with anything that could cause factual or continuity problems—character ages, timing of events, spellings of place or character names, little world‑building details that need to stay consistent. Those are the sorts of mistakes that slip through if you’re tired, and they’re also the things readers notice.

Once that’s solid, I’d skim for language that feels unintentionally odd or distracting. You don’t want to rewrite whole scenes now, but if a sentence makes you wince or a word feels off‑register, it’s fine to make small fixes. The key is to treat these as touch‑ups, not renovations.

To keep yourself from drifting into rewrites, it helps to remind yourself that copyedits aren’t the moment to reinvent anything. The story is already the story. If you catch something that would require real surgery, just flag it for your editor instead of trying to fix it under deadline. That way you stay in the lane the schedule actually expects of you, and you can get through the pages without feeling like you’re rebuilding the whole book from scratch.

**User:** Could you give me a concrete “10-day copyedits game plan” I can follow—like how many pages to aim for per night, what to track in a style sheet, and a quick set of rules for when to accept a change vs query it vs override it?

**Assistant:** You can map out ten days without turning it into a rigid checklist. Think of it as a steady rhythm: divide the total page count by ten, then add a small buffer so the last two days are lighter. If the manuscript is around 300 pages, for example, aiming for roughly thirty to forty pages per evening keeps you moving without exhaustion, and it leaves a bit of space on the final days for anything that needs a second look.

As you go, keep a simple running style sheet. It doesn’t need to be formal; just a single document where you jot down the spellings of names and places, any recurring capitalization choices, preferred punctuation quirks, and anything your worldbuilding depends on staying consistent. Treat it like a memory aid rather than a rulebook.

When you decide whether to accept, query, or override a copyedit, the guiding question is how much the change affects meaning, continuity, or voice. If a change fixes an obvious clarity or consistency issue, take it. If it alters the tone or changes something that matters to the story, push back gently. And if you’re uncertain or the edit touches something you didn’t intend to modify, query it instead of struggling to decide on the spot. That small habit keeps you from going down rabbit holes.

If you keep the pace steady, capture consistency notes as they appear, and treat every decision as “does this preserve the book I meant to write,” you’ll make it through the ten days without needing to reinvent anything.
