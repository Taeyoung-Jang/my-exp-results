# Decision Flowchart for Editing

**Type:** Concept  
**Description:** A concise, three‑tier decision flowchart that helps writers prioritize copy‑edit actions—accept, query, or override—while preserving voice, continuity, and deadline constraints.

---

## Origin
The flowchart originates from the *10‑day copy‑edits game plan* in [[summaries/session-33_2025-03-12]], which was created to help authors meet tight copy‑edit deadlines without spiralling into unnecessary rewrites.

---

## Structure of the Flowchart
The flowchart is expressed as plain‑text steps that can be printed or kept as a quick reference while editing:

```
1. Does the edit improve clarity or fix a clear error?
   → Accept.

2. Does it change tone, rhythm, or meaning?
   • If the original intent was deliberate → Override.
   • If you are unsure → Query.

3. Does it affect continuity, timeline, world‑building, or character data?
   • Correct factual error? → Accept.
   • Conflicts with established fact? → Override & note.
   • Unclear or needs editorial input? → Query.

4. Would it require a larger rewrite?
   → Do NOT fix now; Query.
```

These four decision points map directly onto the three major edit passes described in [[concepts/revision_passes]] (clarity, voice/rhythm, mechanical polish) and the priority hierarchy in [[concepts/copyedit_priorities]].

---

## How to Use It
1. **Keep it visible.** Print the flowchart and place it beside your manuscript or embed it in your digital notes.
2. **Apply it per edit.** When you encounter a suggested change, run through the steps sequentially.
3. **Document queries.** If you choose *Query*, note the location (page/line) and the reason in a separate query log; this keeps the edit session focused and prevents indecision.
4. **Leverage buffer days.** Use the two buffer days from [[concepts/buffer_days_in_writing_schedules]] to resolve any outstanding queries before final submission.

---

## Benefits
- **Prevents scope creep.** By forcing a quick decision, you avoid deep rewrites under deadline pressure.
- **Maintains voice.** The tone‑check step ensures that edits don’t unintentionally flatten the author's unique style.
- **Ensures consistency.** The continuity check catches factual mismatches that are easy to miss in a fatigued edit.
- **Streamlines communication.** Clear *Query* notes give editors concrete items to address, reducing back‑and‑forth.

## Example Application
During a night of editing a 300‑page manuscript, you encounter the suggested change: *Replace "walked" with "strolled".*
1. Improves clarity? No – both convey movement.
2. Changes tone? Yes, *strolled* feels more relaxed than *walked*.
   - Original intent? The scene is meant to be brisk and urgent → **Override** the change.
3. Continuity impact? None.
4. Larger rewrite? No.

Result: You keep *walked*, preserving the intended pacing.
