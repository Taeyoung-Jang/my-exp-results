**Assistant:** You could treat your weeknights as short, structured anchors and let Saturday be your main creative block. A simple rhythm might look like this:

On weeknights, pick two evenings as dedicated shop nights. Give yourself an hour or so after dinner to head straight to the shop before you let yourself drift into screens. The other nights can rotate between light chores, a short workout, and whatever downtime you need, but try to keep each evening focused on just one thing so it doesn’t bleed into your woodworking time.

Saturday can be your main build session. Set aside a protected window in the morning or early afternoon when your energy is good and treat it almost like an appointment. Aim for a three‑ or four‑hour stretch where you can work without rushing. After that, the rest of the day can loosen up for errands, relaxing, or anything social.

Sunday then becomes a reset day: a little light tidying, a brief walk or workout, and some open time for planning the week. If you feel like slipping back into the shop for a quick hour, great, but don’t pressure yourself.

This kind of routine gives you a couple of consistent weeknight touchpoints, one big creative block on Saturday, and enough space around everything else so you don’t feel boxed in.

**Assistant:** You can treat the next couple of weeks like a gentle, guided path through the whole build so you always know exactly what to do when you walk into the shop. Here’s how it could flow without turning into a rigid checklist.

In the first week, your Tuesday evening can be all about breaking down your rough lumber and getting the legs and aprons milled to consistent thickness. On Thursday, finish milling the aprons and move straight into laying out the tapers on the legs so Saturday is set up for real progress. When Saturday comes, use that longer block to cut the leg tapers, clean them up at the jointer or with a plane, and then cut your joinery for the frame—usually mortises in the legs and tenons on the aprons. In the second week, Tuesday night becomes a cleanup and prep session: tune the tenons, dry‑fit the frame, and mark any spots that need a little refinement. **Assistant:** Here’s a version that’s as step‑by‑step as you asked for, but written in flowing paragraphs so it stays friendly and avoids rigid list formatting. Think of each session as a short script you follow when you walk into the shop, with clear stopping points and little staging notes for what comes next.

For the first Tuesday, your entire goal is milling the leg blanks from your rough 4/4 stock. Start by flattening one face and one edge of four leg pieces, then plane them to consistent thickness and rip them to width. Stop as soon as you have four straight, square leg blanks. Before you leave the shop, set your tapering jig or layout tools on the bench and mark the reference faces so Thursday is ready to start quickly.

On the first Thursday, finish milling your apron stock the same way: flatten one face and edge, then plane and rip to final size. Once the aprons are consistent, lay out your leg tapers with a pencil so Saturday is turnkey. It helps to stage your saw setup or tapering jig before closing up, and lay the aprons next to the legs so you can jump straight into joinery on the weekend.

On the first Saturday, start by cutting the leg tapers while your energy is fresh, then clean up the tapers with a plane or sanding. Move immediately into mortise‑and‑tenon work: cut your mortises in the legs first, then mill and cut the apron tenons. Do a quick test fit, tune anything that’s clearly tight, and stop there. Before leaving the shop, stack the parts in dry‑fit order, set your glue, clamps, and cauls aside, and mark the joints so Tuesday is focused and calm.

For the second Tuesday, tune the joinery. Test the frame again, adjust tenon cheeks or mortise walls as needed, and make sure the whole base slips together without force. Once you like the fit, break it back down and lay out your glue‑up sequence. The stopping point is a fully staged glue area: clamps sized correctly, glue bottle filled, rags ready.

On the second Thursday, do a straightforward glue‑up of the base only. Apply glue, clamp it square, and leave it alone. When everything is clamped and wiped down, set the drying assembly aside and stage the stock for the drawer: the front, sides, back, and bottom boards stacked next to the table so Saturday starts smoothly.

On the second Saturday, slide right into drawer construction. Mill and size the drawer parts and cut the joinery—simple rabbets for the back and either rabbets or a mechanical lock for the front, depending on your comfort level. Glue the drawer together and, while it cures, build the side runners and the matching strips that attach to the drawer sides. Once cured enough to handle, fit the drawer so it slides smoothly without binding. Your stopping point is a functioning but unfinished drawer and base. Lay out sandpaper, finish, and rags so the last week flows easily.

For the third Tuesday, do your surface cleanup: flush the joints, break edges, and sand the base and drawer through your usual grits. Test the drawer action again and tune the runners if needed. Stop as soon as all surfaces feel ready for finish. Set out your finish, gloves, and a clean cloth so Thursday moves quickly.

On the third Thursday, apply your first coat of finish. Wipe it on evenly and let the piece sit. The drawer can get the same treatment. When you’re done, place the parts where airflow is good and set out a clean pad for the final coats on Saturday.

On the third Saturday, come back for a light scuff‑sand, then add the final coats of finish. Attach the drawer pull, reinstall the drawer, and do any tiny touch‑ups. Your stop point is simply being done.

This structure keeps every weeknight focused on one clear, achievable chunk, and each Saturday handles the deeper work that benefits from a longer, uninterrupted stretch. It prevents setup and teardown from eating your evenings and keeps the project moving with zero guesswork about what’s next.
