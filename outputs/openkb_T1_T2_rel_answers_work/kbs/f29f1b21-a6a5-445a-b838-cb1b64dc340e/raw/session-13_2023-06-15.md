# Conversation session 13

Date: 2023-06-15 14:30

**User:** I’ve been thinking about wearable tech lately—especially the kind that blends into everyday life. What do you think are the most practical uses for face-worn devices (like hands-free displays or audio-first wearables), and what are the biggest drawbacks people run into day to day?

**Assistant:** They’re most useful when they genuinely take friction out of everyday tasks. Things like quick-glance navigation, hands‑free messaging, discreet audio prompts, or real‑time translations tend to feel natural because they replace phone checks with something lighter and more ambient. They’re also good for activities where your hands are busy—cooking, cycling, commuting, or working with tools.

The biggest drawbacks are comfort, social acceptance, and battery life. Even subtle devices can feel intrusive or awkward to wear all day, and people around you may be unsure whether they’re being recorded. Many products also struggle to deliver enough functionality without becoming bulky or needing constant charging. The tech works best when it disappears into your routine, but right now it still calls attention to itself more than most people want.

**User:** That makes sense. On the day-to-day side, what tends to be the biggest “make or break” factor for people—comfort/fit, battery, or the social/privacy vibe? And are there any specific features you think are worth prioritizing so the device feels more like a convenience than a distraction?

**Assistant:** For most people, the social and privacy vibe ends up being the real make‑or‑break factor. Comfort and battery matter, of course, but they’re easier to tolerate if the device doesn’t make you feel self‑conscious or make others uncomfortable. The moment someone feels watched, judged, or “weird,” the device stops being something they reach for every morning.

As for features, anything that reduces cognitive load tends to make the device feel like a help rather than a distraction. Fast, glanceable information; audio cues that don’t demand attention; and interactions that never feel like you’re operating a mini‑computer on your face are the sweet spot. If it lets you do something quicker than pulling out your phone—and does it quietly and reliably—it becomes a convenience. If it asks for too much attention, it gets left at home.

**User:** If you had to pick just a few “must-have” design choices to reduce the social/privacy awkwardness, what would they be—like obvious recording indicators, physical camera shutters, certain lens styles, or even specific onboarding/etiquette cues?

**Assistant:** The most important choices are the ones that make your intentions instantly clear. A bright, unmistakable recording indicator and a real physical shutter go a long way because they remove ambiguity; people trust what they can see. A design that looks more like everyday eyewear helps too, since anything that feels “gadgety” tends to raise suspicion before you’ve even said a word.

The softer side matters just as much. A quick onboarding prompt that teaches wearers when to disable the camera, how to signal that it’s off, and how to be mindful in sensitive spaces sets social expectations early. When the device itself encourages good etiquette, people around you start to relax, and the tech blends in instead of standing out.

**User:** I actually own a pair of smart glasses with the tinted lenses. Given that, what are a few practical ways I can make it obvious to people around me that I’m not recording (or that recording is off), especially in casual settings like cafes or public transit?

**Assistant:** You can lean on simple, visible habits that make your intentions clear without drawing a lot of attention. Keeping the glasses pointed down or off to the side during conversations helps people feel at ease, and briefly mentioning that the camera’s off when you sit down with someone can diffuse any tension right away. Using the physical shutter or cover—if your model has one—and making sure it’s obviously closed gives people a clear visual cue without you having to say anything. And when you’re in close quarters, like on a bus or in a café line, taking the glasses off and hanging them on your shirt or placing them on the table signals immediately that nothing’s recording.

Those small gestures tend to matter more than the hardware itself. They show you’re aware of the social dynamic, and that reassurance is usually all people really need.
