**User:** I’m in the middle of buying a place and I’m trying to understand the whole closing process—what are the most common last-minute issues that can derail a closing, and what should I be doing now to avoid surprises?

**Assistant:** The things that most often throw a closing off track are usually pretty ordinary, which is why they’re easy to miss. The biggest ones tend to be sudden changes in your financial picture, issues that pop up on the title report, lender delays, or problems with the property right before closing.

The best way to avoid surprises is to keep everything as stable and predictable as possible. Don’t open new credit lines, change jobs, or make large unexplained deposits without talking to your lender first. Make sure you’ve sent in every document your lender has asked for and confirm that your loan is fully cleared to close. Touch base with your agent and the title company to confirm there are no lingering title issues, unpaid liens, or missing signatures. And when you do your final walk‑through, take it seriously—if something is damaged or not as agreed, it’s better to address it before you’re sitting at the closing table.

If you stay in close communication with your lender, agent, and the title company, you’ll catch most problems early enough that they don’t derail anything.

**User:** Closing just got moved up and my accountant is telling me I need to show enough liquid funds in my account by Friday. What actually counts as “liquid funds” to the lender/title company, and what kinds of transfers or deposits could raise red flags this late in the process?

**Assistant:** When lenders talk about “liquid funds,” they mean money that’s sitting in an account you can access immediately—checking, savings, or money‑market accounts. Funds that are locked up, invested in volatile assets, or require a sale to free them up usually don’t count the same way.

What they watch for this late in the process are deposits that look unusual. Large, sudden transfers from outside accounts, cash deposits, or money coming from someone who isn’t already documented as a gift source can trigger new questions, because the lender has to verify where the money came from. Even moving your own money between accounts can create extra paperwork if the amounts are big enough.

If you need to move funds, keep the sources simple and traceable, and loop in your lender before you shift anything around. As long as they know what you’re doing and the money has a clear paper trail, they can usually sign off without slowing things down.

**User:** If I move money from my brokerage account into checking today, is that typically okay as long as I can show the sale/transfer records, or does that still risk delaying the “clear to close”? Also, are cashier’s checks/wires treated differently for proving the funds versus just having the balance in the account?

**Assistant:** Moving money from a brokerage account is usually fine as long as the transaction is clean and you can show the statement or trade confirmation that explains where it came from. Lenders see this all the time. The only time it slows things down is if the transfer hits your account without any documentation and they have to circle back to ask for it, so sending the paperwork to your loan officer right away keeps things moving.

For proving funds, lenders care far more about the balance being in your account and documented than how you actually plan to send the money on closing day. A cashier’s check or wire is just the delivery method. They don’t replace the need to show the funds sitting in your account beforehand, but they also don’t create extra hurdles as long as the money is clearly yours and traceable.

If you’re moving the funds today and giving your lender the supporting documents at the same time, it’s unlikely to jeopardize your clear‑to‑close.

**User:** I spent my lunch break on the phone with my accountant because the closing got moved up—apparently I need to show liquid funds in my account by Friday or the seller can walk. Given that, what’s the safest way to get the money into one place without triggering a bunch of last-minute underwriting questions (e.g., brokerage sale proceeds vs. transferring from another bank vs. a gift), and who should I coordinate with first—my lender, the title company, or both?

**Assistant:** The safest route is to use only your own, clearly documented money and move it in the simplest, most traceable way possible. A transfer from a brokerage account into your checking is usually the cleanest option because you can show the trade confirmation and the transfer record, which satisfies most lenders without much back‑and‑forth. Moving money from another bank is also fine as long as the account is in your name and you can show a statement. A gift this late in the process is the one most likely to cause delays, because it usually triggers extra paperwork and verification.

Before you move anything, tell your loan officer exactly what you plan to do. Underwriting is the only party that can slow you down, so looping them in first keeps everything aligned. Once the lender confirms they’re good with the plan, the title company doesn’t usually need anything until the actual closing funds are sent. Coordination with them is mostly about how you’ll deliver the final payment, not how the money got into your account.

If you keep the source simple and let the lender know ahead of time, it’s very unlikely to create last‑minute issues.

**User:** Can you give me a concrete checklist for the next 48 hours—what documents I should pull (brokerage statements, trade confirms, bank statements), what to send to my loan officer, and what to ask the title company about wiring/cashier’s check timing—so I can hit the Friday “funds in account” requirement without reopening underwriting?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple, concrete checklist you can follow without overexplaining anything.

Documents to pull now:
• Your most recent brokerage statement plus the specific trade confirmations for any sales you’re making to generate cash.  
• A screenshot or PDF of the transfer from the brokerage account into your checking once it posts.  
• Your most recent bank statement for the checking account where the funds will land, plus a dated balance screenshot when the money arrives.

What to send your loan officer:
• A short note saying you’re moving your own funds from your brokerage to checking to meet the Friday requirement.  
• The brokerage statement, the trade confirmations, and then the transfer confirmation once it shows up.  
• A balance screenshot so they can clearly see the funds in place.

What to confirm with the title company:
• Whether they prefer a wire or cashier’s check for closing day.  
• The deadline for the wire or check to arrive.  
• The exact final amount they expect you to bring, or when they’ll send the final closing disclosure with that number.

If you gather the documents first, tell your loan officer what you’re doing before you move anything, and get the title company’s expectations on timing, you should meet the Friday requirement without triggering new underwriting conditions.

**User:** Should I wait until the brokerage sale actually settles (T+1/T+2) before initiating the transfer to checking, or is it better to start the ACH/wire as soon as the trade executes? And if the lender asks for “two months bank statements,” will a big incoming transfer right before closing force them to re-underwrite even if it’s clearly from my own account?

**Assistant:** It’s usually cleaner to wait until the sale settles, because the transfer shows up as cash actually leaving the brokerage rather than as “unsettled funds.” Lenders prefer seeing money that has fully cleared, and it makes the paper trail simpler. That said, if timing is tight, most lenders are fine with you initiating the transfer once the trade executes as long as the settlement date and trade confirmation clearly show the money is yours and about to settle. Your loan officer can tell you which they prefer, so it’s worth a quick check‑in before you move anything.

As for the bank‑statement concern, a large transfer from an account in your own name usually doesn’t trigger a full re‑underwrite. They may ask for the brokerage statement or the trade confirmation so they can connect the dots, but they don’t treat it the same way they treat an unexplained deposit or a gift. As long as the source is clearly yours and the documentation lines up, it’s usually just a quick verification, not a reopening of the entire file.
