**User:** Can you help me come up with a practical checklist (and maybe a short routine I can stick to) for scorpion-proofing my place and staying safe day-to-day? I’ve learned the hard way to always shake out my boots before putting them on; I found another bark scorpion hiding near the heel this morning, driven indoors by the relentless dry heat. I’m looking for steps that don’t require a full remodel—stuff like what to seal, what to move, where to place sticky traps, and any habits that actually make a difference.

**Assistant:** Here’s a practical, compact checklist you can actually use, with just enough structure to be easy to follow. Since you asked for something you can stick to day‑to‑day, I’ll also add a simple nightly routine.

**Checklist for scorpion‑proofing your place**

• Seal obvious entry points: caulk gaps where pipes enter walls, cracks along baseboards, spaces under exterior doors, and openings around window frames. Weatherstripping under doors makes a big difference.  
• Reduce hiding spots indoors: move stored items a bit off the floor, keep closets and laundry heaps tidy, and avoid piles of boxes touching walls.  
• Control moisture: fix minor leaks, don’t leave damp towels on the floor, and try to keep sinks and showers dry overnight.  
• Watch exterior access points: trim vegetation so it doesn’t touch the house, move firewood or clutter a few feet away from walls, and avoid leaving outdoor lights blazing (they attract insects, which attract scorpions).  
• Place sticky traps in “travel corridors”: along baseboards, behind furniture you rarely move, in the garage near the door, and near plumbing fixtures. Put them where you’d put roach traps—edges and corners, not open floor.  
• Reduce indoor insects: keep screens intact, store food securely, and take out trash regularly. A cleaner bug environment means fewer scorpions wandering in.  
• Keep bedding and clothes off the floor: shaking out shoes is great—add a quick shake of clothes, towels, and blankets that sit near the ground.

**A short routine you can stick to**

At night, do a 30‑second sweep: make sure doors are fully closed, shake out anything you’ll put on tomorrow that sat near the floor, dry out the sink or shower if they’re damp, and check that sticky traps are still in place. In the morning, shake out shoes or boots before wearing them and give the floor around your bed a quick glance.

If you want, I can help tailor this to your exact home layout or the specific species common in your area.

**User:** Where exactly would you put sticky traps if you had a typical layout—bedrooms, bathroom, kitchen, living room, and an attached garage—and how many per room is realistic? Also, how often should I check/replace them, and is there a “best” bait or type of trap for bark scorpions?

**Assistant:** For a normal home like that, you don’t need many traps. Think “edges and shadowy travel lanes” rather than open floor.

In bedrooms, tucking one trap along the baseboard near the door and another behind a nightstand or dresser is usually enough. In bathrooms, one beside the toilet or under the sink cabinet works well. In the kitchen, slide one behind the trash can area or under the sink where pipes come in. In the living room, place one behind the couch or a side table that rarely gets moved. In the garage, two or three near the garage door edges and one along the wall where stored items sit tends to catch the most.

Most people end up with one or two traps per room and a couple extras in the garage. That’s realistic without feeling like you’re land‑mining the place.

Check them once a week. Replace them when they’re dusty, full of bugs, or after about a month. There’s no special bait needed; scorpions don’t respond much to bait anyway. The flat, unscented glue boards sold for general insect or spider monitoring work fine.

**User:** That helps—thanks. One more thing: I’ve got kids and a curious dog, so I’m worried about glue boards where they can touch them. Any safer ways to place traps (or alternatives) that still work, especially in bedrooms and the garage?

**Assistant:** You can keep using glue traps; you just have to make them harder for kids and pets to reach while still sitting along the scorpion’s usual path.

The easiest option is to slide the traps slightly under furniture where there’s a gap but no way for a hand or paw to reach—like under a dresser, nightstand, couch, or the fridge. As long as the sticky surface is flush against a wall or baseboard and mostly hidden, they still work. In bedrooms, tucking a trap behind the nightstand or under the bed against the wall usually keeps it out of reach. In the garage, putting them behind stored bins, under shelving units, or against the wall behind the car is usually enough protection.

If you want something even safer, you can use enclosed “monitoring stations” made for glue boards. They’re basically small cardboard or plastic housings that keep the sticky part inside. Scorpions and insects still walk through the opening, but a kid or dog can’t step in it. They’re sold for general pest monitoring, not just scorpions.

If none of that feels secure, a non‑sticky alternative is using simple mechanical barriers: sealing gaps, tidy floors, and keeping beds pulled a few inches from walls. Those steps reduce encounters on their own, and a couple of hidden enclosed traps pick up the rest.

**User:** Could you list a few specific enclosed glue-board “stations” or DIY ways to make a safe housing for a standard glue board (so kids/paws can’t get to it), and where you’d put those in a garage with lots of stored bins and a couple bedrooms? Also, if my dog does get glue on its paws, what’s the safest way to remove it?

**Assistant:** A few brands make enclosed “monitoring stations” meant for insects and spiders, and they work fine for scorpions because the openings are big enough for them to walk through. Look for something like the Catchmaster EZ‑Set, the Protecta Pest Monitor, or any generic “insect monitor station” that uses a replaceable glue insert. They’re basically small boxes with side openings, and they keep the sticky surface recessed so kids and pets can’t touch it.

If you want a simple DIY version, you can slide a regular glue board into a shoebox‑sized cardboard box with two small entry holes cut low on the sides. Keep the openings big enough for a scorpion but too small for a paw or hand, and tape the lid shut. Another option is a short section of PVC pipe—around four inches in diameter—laid on its side with a glue board slid inside. The plastic pipe makes a tough little tunnel that dogs and kids can’t reach into and it stays put against a wall.

In garages, these work best tucked between the wall and your storage bins, or right behind the lowest edge of shelving where scorpions naturally travel. If you have bins that sit an inch or two off the wall, sliding a station into that gap is ideal. In bedrooms, placing them behind a nightstand or under the bed against the wall keeps them accessible to scorpions but invisible to kids.

If your dog does get glue on a paw, the safest approach is gentle and slow: use a little cooking oil or peanut butter to soften the adhesive, work it in with your fingers, and ease the fur free without pulling. Wipe off the residue with a warm, soapy washcloth, rinse well, and watch for any irritation. If the glue is stuck in a sensitive spot or the dog is panicking, call your vet for guidance rather than risking injury from struggling.

**User:** Can you sanity-check that glue-removal advice—are cooking oil/peanut butter actually safe, and should I avoid anything like Goo Gone or acetone? Also, for the DIY station, what size should the entry holes be so bark scorpions can get in but my dog’s paw can’t?

**Assistant:** Cooking oil, olive oil, or peanut butter are all safe for removing glue from a dog’s fur. Vets commonly recommend them because they break down the adhesive gently and aren’t harmful if a dog licks a bit. Mild dish soap afterward is fine. What you want to avoid are strong solvents like acetone, Goo Gone, mineral spirits, or anything with citrus solvents. Those can irritate skin, and dogs may lick them, which is the bigger risk.

For the DIY station openings, bark scorpions can slip through surprisingly small gaps—around the width of a finger—so openings about an inch high and an inch or so wide work. Most dogs’ paws can’t fit through anything that small, especially if the hole is low on the side of a box or a short PVC “tunnel.” If your dog is very small, just err a little smaller; as long as the scorpion can walk in without squeezing, it’ll work.

**User:** I’ve got a medium-sized dog (about 45 lbs) and a toddler, so I want to be extra cautious. Could you give me a more precise DIY plan (cardboard box vs PVC) with exact hole dimensions and placement height that should still let bark scorpions in but keep little fingers/paws out? Also, what’s the best way to secure the station so it doesn’t get dragged around the garage?

**Assistant:** With a 45‑lb dog and a toddler, you just want the opening small, low, and recessed so they can’t get a hand or paw inside. Bark scorpions can flatten themselves and walk through a gap about the size of a fingertip, so you don’t need much room at all.

For a cardboard‑box version, pick something roughly shoe‑box size so a glue board fits flat on the bottom. Cut two identical entry holes on opposite long sides. Make each hole about half an inch tall and about an inch wide, and keep the bottom edge of the hole right at floor level. That size is enough for scorpions but too small for toddler fingers or a dog paw to angle in. Close and tape the lid completely. Because cardboard is light, tape the box to the wall or floor with strong packing tape so it can’t slide around.

For a PVC version, a short length of four‑inch‑diameter pipe makes a sturdy tunnel. Slide the glue board inside so it sits flat, then cap both ends with simple cardboard or plastic caps you’ve cut yourself. Trim each cap so it leaves a narrow “mouth” at floor level, again about half an inch tall and an inch wide. That way the opening is recessed inside the tunnel, and fingers or paws can’t reach the sticky area. Secure the pipe by setting it flush against a wall and using a strip of heavy tape across the top, or place a brick on top so it can’t roll.

Either setup will let bark scorpions walk right in while keeping kids and pets safely out. If you’re unsure, you can send me a rough description of where you plan to place them and I can help you adjust the dimensions.
