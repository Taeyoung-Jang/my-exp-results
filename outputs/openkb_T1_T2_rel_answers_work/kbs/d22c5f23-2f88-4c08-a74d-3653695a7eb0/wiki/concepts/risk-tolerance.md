# Risk Tolerance

**Definition** – Risk tolerance is the degree of variability in investment returns that an individual is comfortable accepting, reflecting both psychological comfort with market swings and the financial capacity to withstand losses.

---

## Why it matters
- Determines the appropriate **stock‑bond allocation** (e.g., 50/50 vs. 60/40 vs. 2/3‑1/3).  
- Influences the choice between a hands‑off **robo‑advisor** and a DIY ETF approach.  
- Guides how aggressively an investor should pursue growth versus stability.

## Insights from [[summaries/session-08_2021-06-15]]
- The conversation highlights a **moderate** risk tolerance as a starting point for many beginners, recommending roughly two‑thirds stock exposure.
- For those who feel uneasy with market drops, a more **conservative split** (50/50 or 60/40) is suggested.
- The advisor advises monitoring **emotional reactions**: if a 20 % dip would cause anxiety, choose the lower stock weight.
- Signs that risk tolerance may be increased include reduced anxiety during dips, less frequent portfolio checks, and confidence in a long‑term timeline.

## New insights from [[summaries/session-32_2023-06-15]]
- A practical **scoring rubric** (0–5 per category) can quantify risk‑related factors such as time horizon, liquidity, drawdown tolerance, concentration, income stability, emergency‑fund adequacy, and behavior risk.
- Summing the category scores (max 35) yields posture bands: 0–14 = Conservative, 15–24 = Moderate, 25–35 = Aggressive. This helps map personal assessments to an appropriate investment style.
- The rubric emphasizes **drawdown tolerance** and **concentration risk** as key behavioral signals; high scores here support a more aggressive stance, while low scores suggest a need for a more defensive allocation.
- Guardrail ranges for portfolio buckets (speculative single names 0–10%, sector/theme funds 10–30%, core index/value 60–90%) provide concrete diversification targets that align with the assessed risk posture.
- A simple **rebalancing** rule—checking twice a year and adjusting when any bucket drifts > 5–10 percentage points—ensures the portfolio stays consistent with the evaluated risk tolerance over time.

## Practical ways to assess your risk tolerance
1. **Self‑assessment questionnaires** – rate comfort with hypothetical losses.  
2. **Scenario testing** – imagine how you’d react to a 15‑20 % drop in stock value.  
3. **Financial capacity** – ensure you have an emergency fund and can meet short‑term cash needs before allocating heavily to risky assets.  
4. **Scoring rubric** – apply the 0–5 scale across the seven categories, sum the scores, and compare to the posture bands to decide on an aggressive, moderate, or conservative stance.

**Takeaway**: Regularly evaluating both the psychological and financial dimensions of risk tolerance—using questionnaires, scenario testing, and a structured scoring rubric—helps you align your portfolio’s asset mix, diversification guardrails, and rebalancing discipline with your true comfort level.
