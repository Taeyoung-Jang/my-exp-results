# Conversation session 10

**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about what makes a visual design feel “right” versus just trendy—like why some posters or app screens instantly click while others feel off even if they’re technically well-made. What do you think are the biggest factors that separate strong visual communication from something that’s just decorative?

**Assistant:** I think the difference usually comes down to intention. Strong visual communication knows exactly what it needs to say, and every element is doing real work to support that message. Decorative design, even if beautiful, tends to focus more on style than purpose.

Clarity is a big part of it. When a layout guides your eye effortlessly and you understand the hierarchy without thinking, it feels “right.” Good design also has a kind of internal logic: spacing, scale, rhythm, and contrast all feel consistent with the message. Even expressive or experimental designs still follow their own set of rules.

Emotional tone matters too. When the visuals match the idea’s personality—calm, urgent, playful, serious—the whole thing feels coherent. Trendy styling can be fun, but if it doesn’t align with the underlying story, people sense the mismatch.

So it’s less about how polished something looks and more about whether form and meaning are working together instead of competing for attention.

**User:** These days, I'm working full-time as a graphic designer. Given that, when you’re evaluating whether a layout is truly communicating versus just looking nice, what are the first 3–5 things you check (especially for posters and app screens)?

**Assistant:** I usually run through a quick mental sequence rather than a formal checklist. First, I look at whether the main message is instantly clear—what the viewer should notice or understand within the first second. Then I pay attention to hierarchy to see if the eye moves through the layout in a deliberate, controlled way. After that, I check whether the visual tone actually matches the content, because a mismatch there can quietly undermine everything else. I also glance at spacing and rhythm to make sure the structure feels intentional, not accidental. And finally, I consider whether anything on the screen is merely decorative; if an element isn’t earning its place, it usually weakens the communication.

**User:** Can you turn that into a practical mini-checklist I can use during reviews—one version for posters (like event promos) and one for app screens? If possible, include a couple quick “red flag” examples for each.

**Assistant:** Here’s a version you can use without turning it into a rigid list, but still practical enough to run through quickly.

For posters, you can ask yourself whether the core message lands in a single glance, whether the visual hierarchy actually guides someone from headline to details, and whether the tone matches the event rather than just following a style you like. While doing that, watch out for things like a headline getting lost because the artwork is louder than the information, or type treatments that look cool but make the date or location harder to read.

For app screens, start by checking whether the primary action or purpose of the screen is obvious without thinking, then confirm the hierarchy feels natural and doesn’t fight the way people actually scan on a phone. Also make sure the visual styling supports the function rather than decorating it. Red flags tend to show up when a button looks like a graphic element instead of something tappable, or when spacing feels stylish but makes related items appear disconnected.

Both versions are basically quick gut checks that keep you focused on whether the design is communicating clearly rather than just impressing at first glance.

**User:** Could you rewrite the checklists in a more structured format (like 6–8 bullets each) that I can paste into our team’s review doc, and include one “quick test” per checklist (e.g., a 5‑second test or squint test) plus a couple concrete red-flag examples?

**Assistant:** Poster review checklist  
• Identify the main message in one glance.  
• Confirm the headline is clearly dominant over supporting details.  
• Check that the eye moves naturally from headline to date, location, and call‑to‑action.  
• Make sure the tone of typography and color matches the event’s mood.  
• Ensure imagery supports the message rather than competing with it.  
• Look for consistent spacing that creates an intentional rhythm.  
• Remove decorative elements that don’t add clarity or meaning.  
Quick test: Do a 5‑second glance. If you can’t restate what the poster is about and when it happens, the hierarchy isn’t strong enough.  
Red flags: Artwork is louder than the title, or the date is so small or stylized that people miss it.

App screen review checklist  
• Confirm the primary action or purpose is instantly obvious.  
• Check that the visual hierarchy matches natural mobile scanning patterns.  
• Ensure interactive elements look tappable and distinct from decorative shapes.  
• Verify spacing groups related items and doesn’t create accidental separation.  
• Match visual tone to function rather than general style trends.  
• Reduce anything that adds visual noise without helping comprehension.  
• Make sure text legibility holds up at typical phone‑holding distance.  
Quick test: Do a squint test. If the main action and key info don’t stand out when blurred, contrast or layout needs adjustment.  
Red flags: Buttons that look like illustrations, or aesthetic spacing that makes linked items feel unrelated.
