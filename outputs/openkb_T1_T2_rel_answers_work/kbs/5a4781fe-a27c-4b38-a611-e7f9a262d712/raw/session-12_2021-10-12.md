**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about how people decide what to study and how that choice shapes their career options later. If you were starting from scratch today, what would you look for in a field of study—interest, job market, flexibility, something else?

**Assistant:** If I were choosing from scratch, I’d look for a mix of a few things. I’d start with genuine interest, because it’s hard to stay motivated long‑term in something you don’t enjoy at least a little. At the same time, I’d pay attention to whether the field has a healthy job market and skills that transfer well across different roles, since careers change so much now. Flexibility matters a lot too—fields that let you move between industries, adapt to new technology, or reinvent yourself tend to age better than very narrow specialties.

So I’d look for something that feels meaningful, teaches durable skills, and leaves plenty of doors open later.

**User:** That makes sense. How would you actually test whether you’re genuinely interested in a field before committing to a major—especially if you don’t have much real-world exposure yet?

**Assistant:** One of the easiest ways is to give yourself small, low‑stakes tastes of the field before you make any big commitment. You can try a short online course, read a beginner‑friendly book, watch a few talks, or follow people who work in that area and see what their day‑to‑day looks like. If you find yourself wanting to keep going even when no one is making you, that’s usually a good sign.

Another helpful trick is to imagine spending a whole week doing tasks related to that field. If the idea feels energizing rather than draining, that tells you something. And if you can talk to even one person who actually works in the field, that conversation often gives you a clearer sense than months of guessing on your own.

**User:** If you were me and you had a few majors you’re curious about but you’re not sure which one fits, what kind of questions would you ask professionals (or older students) to get a real sense of what the work is actually like?

**Assistant:** I’d ask things that get past the glossy surface and into the feel of the work. For example, I’d want to know what their actual day looks like, not just the title on their business card. I’d ask what parts of the job they genuinely enjoy and what parts they quietly dread. I’d ask what surprised them once they started—what turned out to be very different from what they expected in school. I’d ask what skills they actually use the most, and which ones they wish they’d built earlier. And I’d ask how much their job or field has changed since they started, because that tells you a lot about stability and adaptability.

Mostly, I’d be trying to get a sense of whether their description sparks curiosity or makes me feel heavy. Your reaction to their answers can be more revealing than the answers themselves.

**User:** One quick update: I haven't finished my education yet; I'm currently an undergraduate student. Given that, what are the top 8–10 questions you’d recommend I ask professionals or older students to compare a few majors, and which answers should be “green flags” vs “red flags”?

**Assistant:** Here are the kinds of questions I’d ask, phrased in a way that keeps things conversational. I’ll also note what you’d hope to hear versus what might be concerning.

You can start by asking what a typical day actually feels like for them. A green flag is a clear, grounded description that sounds varied or engaging; a red flag is vague answers or “it depends” without substance, which can signal burnout or lack of clarity.

You might ask which tasks they genuinely look forward to and which they avoid. It’s a good sign if the enjoyable parts match what you imagine liking; it’s a warning sign if the disliked parts dominate most of their time.

It helps to ask what surprised them about the field once they got deeper into it. Positive surprises—more creativity, more autonomy—are green flags. Negative surprises like constant stress or repetitive work are red flags.

Try asking which skills they actually use every day versus the ones that mattered only in school. It’s encouraging if the everyday skills align with your strengths or interests; it’s less encouraging if the job relies heavily on things you dislike or struggle with.

Ask how much flexibility they feel they have in their career path. Hearing that people move between roles or industries easily is a green flag. Hearing that the field is extremely narrow or hard to pivot out of is a red flag.

You can also ask how the field has changed in the last few years. Adaptable fields with growing opportunities tend to be healthier. Fields described as shrinking, volatile, or bottlenecked may be riskier.

It’s useful to ask what type of person tends to thrive in the major or career. A green flag is when their description sounds like you; a red flag is when it sounds like someone fundamentally different.

And finally, ask what they wish they’d known as an undergraduate. Helpful, concrete advice is a good sign. If they say they would have chosen differently for reasons that concern you, pay attention to that.

Your goal isn’t to score each major, but to notice your reactions. When an answer makes you lean in rather than pull back, that’s usually the signal that matters most.
