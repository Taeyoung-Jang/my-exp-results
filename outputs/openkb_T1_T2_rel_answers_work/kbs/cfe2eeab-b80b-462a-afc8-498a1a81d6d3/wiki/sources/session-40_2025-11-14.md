**User:** I’ve been curious about climbing lately—especially bouldering. If someone’s just starting out, what are the biggest beginner mistakes to avoid, and what kind of simple routine would you recommend for building grip strength and confidence without overdoing it?

**Assistant:** A lot of beginners make the same few mistakes. The biggest one is trying to muscle through every move with your arms. Bouldering is way more about body position and using your legs, so if your forearms are burning out fast, that’s usually a sign to slow down and think about your feet. Another common issue is rushing grades. It’s totally fine to repeat easy problems until you feel fluid; confidence comes from comfort, not from jumping to the hardest color on the wall. And finally, people tend to hang on holds with straight, locked‑out fingers, which strains tendons. A relaxed, slightly curved grip is much kinder to your hands.

For a simple routine to build grip and confidence, keep it light. A few times a week, spend a few minutes on an easy traverse around the gym, staying low so you can focus on technique without falling. After climbing, gently hang from a bar for short, comfortable intervals—just a few seconds at first. Stop long before you feel any sharp strain. Even squeezing a soft stress ball at home helps wake up those forearm muscles. The key is consistency and staying below your limit so your tendons adapt slowly. You’ll feel stronger without risking overuse.

**User:** That makes sense. When you say “short, comfortable hangs,” what does that look like in numbers (like seconds/sets), and should I be doing them on a big jug hold or just a pull-up bar? Also, how do I tell the difference between normal forearm pump and the kind of finger/tendon discomfort that means I should stop?

**Assistant:** For true beginners, “short, comfortable hangs” usually means something like five to ten seconds per hang, done two or three times with plenty of rest in between. If that already feels tiring, drop it to just a few seconds. You’re not training max strength yet; you’re just letting your tendons get used to load. A pull‑up bar or a big, rounded jug both work fine. Most beginners find a bar easier because it’s consistent and gentle on the fingers.

Normal pump feels like swelling, heat, and a dull, tight fatigue in your forearms. It comes on gradually and goes away after a few minutes of rest. Problem discomfort is sharper or more localized, especially along the sides of your fingers or right at a joint. If you feel anything that’s pinchy, zingy, or feels like it’s “pulling wrong,” stop immediately and give it time to settle. Tendons almost never feel sore in a general, warm way—they complain in a very specific spot. When in doubt, rest and keep hangs well below the level where you start testing your limits.

**User:** I finally got back into bouldering last week—nothing fancy, just a few easy routes—but it felt good to hang on the wall again without thinking about it every second. Given that, if I’m doing those 5–10 second hangs, how many days per week should I add them without risking finger tweaks, and should I skip them on days I boulder?

**Assistant:** If you’re easing back in, two or three days a week is plenty for those gentle hangs. You don’t need more than that to wake your fingers up, and doing them too often is what usually leads to tweaks. On days you boulder, you can skip them entirely. The climbing itself gives your fingers all the load they need, and stacking extra hanging on top just adds stress without real benefit. If a session felt especially light, a single easy hang at the end is fine, but you should never feel like you’re “training” after climbing. The goal is to keep your fingers fresh and adapting, not to test them.

**User:** Got it. If I’m bouldering 2x/week right now, would you recommend doing the hangs on two separate non-climbing days (so 4 total “finger load” days), or just add one hang day between sessions? Also, how long should I stick with the super-easy hang approach before progressing?

**Assistant:** If you’re climbing twice a week, one extra hang day in between sessions is usually enough. Four finger‑load days in a week can feel like a lot when you’re just rebuilding tolerance, and it’s better to come back wanting more than to edge toward soreness.

Most people benefit from staying in the super‑easy phase for at least three to four weeks. What you’re really waiting for is a sense that your fingers feel normal the next day—no lingering tightness in a specific joint, no odd twinges when you warm up, and no hesitation grabbing holds. When everything feels routine and your hangs genuinely feel too easy rather than “manageable,” that’s when you can think about adding a few seconds or an extra set. Slow progression keeps you on the wall instead of on the couch with an ice pack.
