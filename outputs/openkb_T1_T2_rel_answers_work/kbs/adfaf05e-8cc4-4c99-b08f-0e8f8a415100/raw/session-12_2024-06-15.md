# Conversation session 12

Date: 2024-06-15 10:30

**User:** I’ve been getting back into photography lately and want to take it more seriously—less “random snapshots,” more intentional projects. If you were coaching someone to level up, what would you suggest focusing on first: composition fundamentals, lighting, or developing a consistent style?

**Assistant:** I’d start with composition. It’s the foundation that makes every photo—no matter the gear, lighting, or style—feel intentional. Once you’re comfortable making deliberate choices about framing, balance, and what you want the viewer to notice, lighting becomes much easier to approach with purpose. Style tends to emerge naturally as you make more decisions on those first two pieces, so you don’t have to force it early on.

**User:** Just for your records, I'm still an active member of the National Photography Society. With that in mind, could you tailor a 4-week leveling-up plan that I could realistically stick to—weekly assignments and a simple critique checklist—starting with composition and then moving into lighting?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple four‑week plan you can follow without it feeling like homework. I’ll keep everything in plain language so it’s easy to stick with.

Week 1 focuses on composition. Pick one location you can return to a few times and make a series of photos built around a single compositional idea each day—one day for leading lines, one for framing, one for balance, one for negative space. At the end of the week, look at your set and ask yourself whether the main subject is obvious, whether anything distracts from it, and whether the framing feels intentional rather than accidental.

Week 2 expands composition into small projects. Choose a simple theme—shapes, reflections, pairs of objects, whatever feels interesting—and make ten images that fit the theme. When you review them, check whether the photos feel like they belong together and whether you made consistent compositional choices instead of jumping around.

Week 3 shifts to lighting. Spend the week experimenting with one kind of light per session: soft window light, hard midday sun, shade, or artificial light at home. For each session, make a handful of images of the same subject but change the angle of light or your shooting position. When critiquing, see if the direction and quality of the light actually serve the subject and whether shadows add shape instead of muddling things.

Week 4 combines everything. Pick a small project—something you could finish in a couple of outings—and shoot it with both composition and lighting in mind. When you review this set, check three things: whether the light is doing something purposeful, whether the composition leads the eye where you want it to go, and whether the images feel like part of the same body of work.

If you stick to one clear intention each week and keep your critiques short and honest, you’ll notice your style starting to tighten up without forcing it.

**User:** Could you make that 4‑week plan more concrete with specific “shoot this” prompts for each day (even if it’s just 3 shooting days per week), plus a quick way to score each photo using the critique checklist?

**Assistant:** Here’s a clearer version you can follow without needing a rigid list. I’ll keep it conversational, but still give you concrete daily prompts and a simple scoring method you can run through quickly.

In Week 1, focus on composition and shoot three days. On day one, photograph leading lines by visiting a street, path, or hallway and making five images where lines guide the viewer straight to the subject. On day two, work on framing by finding natural frames like doorways, branches, or shadows and placing your subject inside them. On day three, explore negative space by choosing a simple subject and surrounding it with plenty of breathing room, either sky or blank walls or open ground.

In Week 2, stay with composition but make it more thematic. On day one, choose a shape, like circles or triangles, and create images that highlight it clearly in the frame. On day two, shoot reflections, whether in windows, puddles, or metal surfaces, and try to make the reflection the main story rather than an afterthought. On day three, build a mini‑series around repetition, such as patterns in architecture or repeating objects, and try to make the set feel visually consistent.

In Week 3, shift to lighting. On day one, work with soft window light by placing any subject near a window and making images from different angles to explore how the light wraps or falls off. On day two, shoot in harsh midday sun and play with strong shadows, trying to use the contrast intentionally rather than fighting it. On day three, use whatever artificial light you have at home, even a lamp or phone light, and create simple portraits or object studies that show deliberate direction and intensity.

In Week 4, combine everything into small projects. On day one, pick a single subject you can return to, like a favorite café corner or a particular tree, and photograph it in different light with strong compositional intent. On day two, create a short narrative by shooting three to five images that feel like a sequence, paying attention to how composition and lighting support the mood. On day three, choose a setting with people and make candid or semi‑posed images where you intentionally control both composition and whatever light is available.

For scoring, keep it fast. Look at each photo and give yourself up to three points: one point if the main subject is immediately clear, one point if the composition directs the eye without distractions, and one point if the light noticeably helps rather than hinders the image. A three‑point photo is one you’d be proud to show; a one‑point photo just tells you what to focus on next time.

**User:** Can you also suggest a simple workflow for reviewing and selecting the “keepers” each week (like how many to pick, how to compare similar frames, and what to do with the low-scoring shots) so I actually learn from the scoring instead of just rating and moving on?

**Assistant:** You can keep this really straightforward so it doesn’t feel like a chore. At the end of each week, sit down with all your shots and do a quick first pass where you mark anything that feels promising at a glance. Don’t overthink it. You’re just flagging the frames you naturally respond to.

Once you have that smaller group, compare similar shots side by side. Trust your gut and choose the one that communicates the idea most cleanly. When two frames feel close, let the scoring break the tie. Aim to end up with three to five keepers for the whole week. That small number forces you to be selective without losing the fun of shooting.

For the low‑scoring shots, don’t delete them right away. Take a minute to ask why they scored low. Was the subject unclear, the framing messy, or the light working against you? Make a tiny note to yourself, even one sentence, about the main reason. After that, they’ve served their purpose and you can archive or delete them as you like.

Over time, those little notes will start repeating themselves, and that repetition is what actually levels you up.
