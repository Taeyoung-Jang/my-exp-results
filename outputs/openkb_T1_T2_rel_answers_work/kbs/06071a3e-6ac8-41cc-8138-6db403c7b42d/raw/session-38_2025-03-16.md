# Conversation session 38

Date: 2025-03-16 11:00

**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about what it actually takes to pick up your life and move to a new city for a long-ish stint—logistics, emotions, the whole reset. If you were advising someone planning a year abroad, what would you prioritize first: housing, budgeting, social life, or work setup?

**Assistant:** I’d start with housing, simply because it affects everything else. Knowing where you’ll live, what it costs, and what your day‑to‑day environment will feel like gives you a stable foundation for the rest of the move.

Once you have that pinned down, budgeting comes into focus naturally, since you’ll have a clearer sense of fixed costs. Work setup tends to fall into place after that, especially if you already know the nature of your job or program. Social life is something I’d treat as an ongoing process rather than a pre‑move task—you can’t fully build it until you’re there, but you can set yourself up by choosing a neighborhood or housing style that makes meeting people easier.

So: get the roof and the rhythm first, and the rest becomes much simpler to shape.

**User:** That makes sense. I finally signed the papers on that year-long fellowship in Copenhagen, so I’ve been selling off most of my furniture and setting up a tiny sublet near the lab. Given that, what would you tackle next in the first month on the ground to make the transition smoother—especially around budgeting and building a social routine without letting the work setup consume everything?

**Assistant:** Once you’ve landed and settled into the sublet, I’d spend the first month getting a feel for your actual cost of living. Track what you spend without trying to optimize too hard at first; after a couple of weeks you’ll see the real patterns and can adjust your budget in a way that feels natural instead of forced.

At the same time, start weaving in small, repeatable social habits. Pick one or two things you can do weekly—grabbing coffee at the same spot, joining a casual meetup, showing up to a lab social hour—and let those become anchors. They don’t have to be big gestures; consistency is what creates familiarity and makes a new city feel less like you’re floating.

For work, set clear boundaries right away. Decide what your “default” workday looks like, both start and stop, and hold yourself to it unless there’s a genuine exception. It’s much easier to loosen boundaries later than to tighten them once you’ve slipped into overwork.

If you manage those three—honest budgeting, small social routines, and early work boundaries—you’ll settle in far more smoothly than you expect.

**User:** Could you help me sketch a simple “first 30 days” plan for Copenhagen—like a weekly checklist that covers: (1) getting my budget calibrated (banking, recurring costs, groceries/transport), (2) building a social routine that doesn’t feel forced, and (3) setting work boundaries so I don’t just live at the lab?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple way to frame the first month without leaning too hard on list formatting.

For week one, sort out the basics: get your banking fully set up, note your fixed costs, figure out your commute, and do a few grocery runs to understand local prices. Treat it as data‑gathering rather than optimization.

In week two, start shaping a rough budget based on what you actually spent in week one. At the same time, pick one low‑effort social habit, like returning to the same café or attending one recurring event near your neighborhood. Keep it light so it doesn’t feel like a project.

Week three is when you tighten things a bit. Adjust your budget so it matches reality, not aspiration. Add one more social touchpoint—maybe a lab social hour or a casual meetup—just enough to create a rhythm. This is also when you set a default workday: a start and stop time you stick to unless there’s a real reason not to.

By week four, you’re mostly refining. See if your spending feels sustainable, tweak anything that’s off, and notice which social habits feel natural. Reinforce the work boundaries you set, because this is when they’re most likely to slip as you get more comfortable in the lab.

If you hit those marks each week, you’ll feel settled without overloading yourself.

**User:** Can you turn that into a clearer week-by-week checklist (like 5–8 bullets per week) and add a couple Copenhagen-specific ideas for low-pressure social routines—especially if I’m not a big nightlife person?

**Assistant:** Week 1  
• Open or finalize local banking and make sure your payments/transfer options work.  
• List all fixed costs: rent, utilities, transit pass, phone.  
• Do two or three grocery runs to map out prices.  
• Try different transport routes to the lab to see what feels best.  
• Walk your neighborhood so you know cafés, parks, and basic shops.

Week 2  
• Review week‑one spending and sketch a rough budget.  
• Get a Rejsekort or monthly transit card if you haven’t yet.  
• Establish one low‑pressure social habit, like a weekly visit to the same café.  
• Attend one lab social or informal lunch.  
• Define your default work hours and stick to them for the week.

Week 3  
• Adjust your budget based on actual patterns.  
• Identify one or two “go‑to” grocery stores for price and convenience.  
• Add one more social touchpoint, like a casual meetup or interest group.  
• Protect your work stop‑time and avoid “just one more hour.”  
• Try a new walking route or bike path to explore more of the city.

Week 4  
• Re‑check spending and fine‑tune anything that feels off.  
• Start building a simple meal routine that fits your budget.  
• Keep the social habits that feel easiest and drop the rest.  
• Recommit to work boundaries before habits calcify.  
• Plan one small weekend outing to a new neighborhood.

Copenhagen‑specific low‑pressure social ideas  
• Join a weekly “language café” at a library—they’re relaxed and mostly about chatting.  
• Visit outdoor swimming spots or saunas like Islands Brygge or Nordhavn; people often chat casually.  
• Drop into community events at Kulturhuset Indre By or local library talks.  
• Join a casual cycling group or beginner‑friendly running club; very low nightlife energy.
