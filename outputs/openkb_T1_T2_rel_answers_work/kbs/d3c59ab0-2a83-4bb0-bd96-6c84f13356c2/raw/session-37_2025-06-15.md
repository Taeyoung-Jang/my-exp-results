**User:** Any tips for navigating a company reorg without losing your mind? I’m trying to stay productive while roles and reporting lines shift, but it’s hard to know what to prioritize and how to communicate without stepping on toes.

**Assistant:** It helps to treat a reorg like a period of controlled ambiguity. You won’t get perfect clarity right away, so the goal is to stay steady until things settle.

The biggest thing is to anchor yourself to what you *know* still matters: the core work that clearly supports your team or customers. Keep those pieces moving, even if everything around them feels uncertain.

At the same time, keep your communication simple and frequent. When priorities feel fuzzy, ask short, direct questions like “Is this still the most important thing for me to focus on this week?” or “Who should I loop in on this?” That shows initiative without overreaching.

Try not to over‑interpret shifting reporting lines. In most reorgs, people are also waiting to see how things land, so a calm, transparent tone goes a long way. Something like “Here’s what I’m planning to tackle next—does that fit with where we’re heading?” keeps you aligned without stepping on anyone’s toes.

And give yourself a little grace. Feeling unsettled is normal, but staying steady, visible, and easy to collaborate with tends to pay off once the dust settles.

**User:** I’m still getting used to the new org chart—HR had me sign a transfer packet last week, and now my calendar’s full of onboarding sessions and mandatory training for the role. Given that, how would you prioritize day-to-day work vs. all the onboarding/training, and what’s a good way to communicate bandwidth to both my old and new managers without sounding like I’m complaining?

**Assistant:** I’d treat the onboarding and training as real work, not as something extra you squeeze in around everything else. For the first couple of weeks, it’s completely reasonable to let your day‑to‑day tasks slow a bit so you can get fully calibrated in the new role. Most managers expect that, especially right after a formal transfer.

A simple way to set expectations is to frame things as planning rather than apologizing. You can say something like, “I’m ramping up with the new onboarding sessions this week, so my availability for project work is a little tighter than usual. Here’s what I can realistically get done—does that line up with your expectations?” That makes it clear you’re being thoughtful and organized, not complaining.

For your old manager, you can keep it even lighter: “I’m still tying up a couple of loose ends, but most of my time is shifting to the new team’s onboarding this week. Let me know if there’s anything that needs a handoff.” It signals closure without implying you’re stretched beyond reason.

As long as you sound matter‑of‑fact and proactive, people interpret it as professionalism, not pushback.
