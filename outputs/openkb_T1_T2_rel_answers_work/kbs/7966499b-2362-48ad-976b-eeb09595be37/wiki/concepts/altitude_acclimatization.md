# Altitude Acclimatization

**Definition**: The set of physiological adaptations and practical tactics that allow a person to function safely and comfortably at elevations where atmospheric oxygen pressure is reduced.

---

## Why Acclimatization Matters
When you move to a mountain town or hike above 2,500 ft, the thinner air means your body receives less oxygen per breath.  This can trigger:
- Elevated heart rate
- Faster breathing (ventilation)
- Mild headaches or a sense of being "winded"
- Reduced exercise performance

If these symptoms are ignored, they can progress to acute mountain sickness, which requires immediate descent.

---

## Core Strategies (drawn from [[summaries/session-36_2024-06-15]])
1. **Gradual Load Increases** – Follow a stepwise plan such as the [[concepts/gradual_hike_progression]] outlined in the 4‑week schedule.  Increasing distance and elevation gain by ~10–20 % each week lets the cardiovascular system adapt without triggering severe symptoms.
2. **Controlled Exertion** – Use a perceived effort scale (RPE) of 4–7 /10 and stay roughly in the 60–75 % of max heart‑rate zone during climbs.  At altitude HR tends to be higher, so combine HR monitoring with the **talk test** for safety (see [[concepts/rpe_and_heart_rate]]).
3. **Breathing Technique** – Adopt a steady rhythm (e.g., inhale for two steps, exhale for two‑three) to keep ventilation efficient and avoid rapid, shallow breaths.  This is covered in [[concepts/breathing_technique]].
4. **Hydration & Nutrition** – Dehydration worsens altitude symptoms.  Sip water regularly, avoid large chugs, and keep a small snack on hand for quick energy (see [[concepts/hydration_guidelines]]).
5. **Monitor Symptoms** – Differentiate normal altitude discomfort (mild headache that eases with slower pace, water, rest) from emergency signals (sharp headache, dizziness, nausea, chest tightness).  Use the on‑trail decision tree from the [[concepts/symptom_checklist]] to decide when to turn back.
6. **Use Trekking Poles** – Poles shift work to the upper body, produce a steadier cadence, and reduce perceived exertion on steep sections (see [[concepts/trekking_pole_technique]]).

## Practical Checklist for Acclimatization
| Step | Action | Goal |
|------|--------|------|
| 1 | Start each hike slower than you think you need; the first 10‑15 min at a very easy pace. | Prevent early spikes in HR and breathing. |
| 2 | Follow the weekly distance/elevation targets in the progressive plan. | Build aerobic capacity safely. |
| 3 | Keep effort at RPE 4–7/10; if you cannot hold a short conversation, drop back. | Maintain a safe physiological load. |
| 4 | Apply the symptom decision tree if a headache or other sign appears (see [[concepts/symptom_checklist]]). | Early detection of problematic altitude reactions. |
| 5 | Hydrate regularly, snack every 60‑90 min, and use poles on steep climbs. | Support oxygen delivery and lower perceived effort. |

## Summary
Effective altitude acclimatization blends a **gradual increase in workload**, **controlled breathing and effort**, **consistent hydration**, and **vigilant symptom monitoring**.  By adhering to these practices—illustrated in the 4‑week hike plan from [[summaries/session-36_2024-06-15]]—hikers can safely build stamina, enjoy mountain terrain, and avoid the escalation of altitude‑related illness.
