# Clinician Check‑In Red Flags for Sleep Interventions

**Definition**: Specific symptoms or patterns that indicate a sleep‑reset or habit‑change program may be harming health and should prompt immediate consultation with a health professional.

## Core Red‑Flag Criteria (from [[summaries/session-11_2024-01-20]])

1. **Worsening of underlying sleep disorder symptoms** for several consecutive days rather than gradual improvement.
2. **New or intensified nocturnal breathing issues** (e.g., louder snoring, apnea‑type pauses, choking sensations).
3. **Sharp decline in total sleep time** that does not rebound within 1‑2 days.
4. **Unsafe daytime sleepiness** – difficulty staying awake during routine activities, driving, or operating machinery.
5. **Emergence of confusion, disorientation, or cognitive lapses** that are atypical for the individual.
6. **Cardiovascular or other systemic warnings** previously flagged by the clinician (e.g., irregular heart rate, chest discomfort).
7. **Any other symptom** the treating clinician has specifically warned could indicate a problem.

**Additional criteria from [[summaries/session-37_2024-10-20]]**

8. **Pattern of multiple bad nights** – three or more poor‑quality nights within a single week, especially when they feel similar.
9. **Persistent daytime red‑flags** lasting more than one to two weeks, such as:
   - Ongoing fatigue or excessive sleepiness that interferes with daily tasks.
   - Noticeable irritability, mood instability, or emotional fragility.
   - Trouble concentrating, memory lapses, or reduced cognitive performance.
   - Uncontrolled sleepiness that endangers safety (e.g., while driving).

> These flags are meant to be **high‑sensitivity triggers**; encountering one does not guarantee a serious issue, but it warrants a pause in the plan and professional evaluation.

## Relationship to Other Sleep Concepts

- Consistent **[[concepts/circadian_rhythm]]** anchors help reduce the likelihood of many red flags, but when a disorder disrupts the rhythm, vigilance is essential.
- Adequate **[[concepts/light_therapy]]** in the morning supports melatonin regulation; insufficient exposure may exacerbate daytime sleepiness.
- Managing **[[concepts/caffeine_and_sleep]]** timing reduces the risk of nighttime awakenings that could mask breathing problems.
- Proper **[[concepts/exercise_and_sleep]]** timing can improve sleep quality without over‑stimulating the nervous system.
- A well‑structured **[[concepts/sleep_hygiene]]** routine (wind‑down, screen management, bedroom environment) mitigates many triggers for symptom flare‑ups.
- The **[[concepts/decision_rule_sleep_monitoring]]** provides a concrete decision rule (e.g., ≥3 bad nights/week or persistent daytime symptoms) that aligns with these red‑flag criteria.
- When red‑flag symptoms appear, the **[[concepts/troubleshooting_sleep_reset]]** guide offers immediate self‑help steps (e.g., adjusting light exposure, shortening exercise) while awaiting clinician input.

## Practical Use

- **Daily monitoring**: Use the simple log from the 2‑week plan to capture any of the above signs.
- **Decision tree**: If any red‑flag item is noted, **stop** the current reset actions and contact your clinician before resuming.
- **Clinician communication**: Provide a brief summary of observed red flags, along with the daily log data, to facilitate rapid assessment.
