# Emergency Fund

**Definition**: A readily accessible cash reserve set aside to handle unforeseen expenses, preventing reliance on credit cards or other high‑interest debt.

## Why It Matters
- Provides a financial *buffer* for emergencies such as car repairs, medical costs, or temporary loss of income.
- Reduces the likelihood of missing minimum payments on existing debts, which can increase interest and damage credit.
- Supports the stability of a [[concepts/zero-based-budgeting]] plan by allocating a dedicated line item for unexpected costs.
- Serves as a core element of overall [[concepts/liquidity-management]], ensuring that funds needed for emergencies are not tied up in longer‑term investments.

## Recommended Size
- A *starter* emergency fund of **$1,000** is often suggested for individuals with limited cash on hand. This amount can cover most minor emergencies and gives enough confidence to avoid new credit‑card charges.
- After reaching the starter goal, the fund can be expanded to cover **3–6 months** of essential living expenses for greater security.
- When income is stable but expenses are variable, aiming toward the upper end of that range (5–6 months) is advisable.

## Tiered Storage Strategy (from Session 37)
1. **High‑Yield Savings Account** –≈ 2 months of expenses for instant access. [[entities/high-yield-savings-account]]
2. **Money‑Market Fund** –≈ 2 months; still highly liquid, slightly better yield. [[entities/money-market-fund]]
3. **Short‑Term Treasury Bills** –≈ 2 months; low risk, modest return, often organized in a ladder. [[entities/t-bill]]

- A simple **T‑bill ladder** can be built by purchasing three‑month T‑bills staggered one month apart, creating a monthly maturity cycle that can be rolled over or used for cash needs.
- Holding the T‑bills and money‑market fund in a single **brokerage account** simplifies management. [[entities/brokerage-account]]
- If you prefer, T‑bills can be bought via **TreasuryDirect**, though a brokerage is usually more user‑friendly for quick access and liquidation. [[entities/treasurydirect]]

## Application in Session 11 (2023‑06‑15)
- The user had **$900** in a [[entities/checking-account]] and no real emergency cushion.
- The proposed budget advised **pausing the $100 monthly savings** and redirecting it to build a $1,000 starter fund over roughly **10 months** before resuming the full debt‑avalanche payments.
- This approach aligns with the principle of establishing a safety net before accelerating debt repayment, ensuring the user does not fall back on credit cards during the buildup period.

## Interaction with Debt Repayment Strategies
- When an emergency fund is in place, the user can confidently follow the **[[concepts/debt-avalanche-method]]** (paying highest‑interest debt first) without fearing cash‑flow shortfalls.
- If the user also has access to an **employer match**, capturing that match first (by directing part of the freed‑up money to retirement) is recommended before further expanding the emergency fund. [[concepts/employer-match]]
- Conversely, attempting aggressive debt payments without any cushion can lead to missed payments and the need to re‑borrow, negating the benefits of the avalanche approach.

## Practical Steps to Build an Emergency Fund
1. **Set a target** (e.g., $1,000 starter, then 3–6 months of expenses).  
2. **Allocate a fixed amount each month** (the session suggested $100, or a higher amount once an employer match is captured).  
3. **Choose tiered accounts**: keep the most immediately needed cash in a high‑yield savings account, the next layer in a money‑market fund, and the remainder in short‑term T‑bills via a brokerage.  
4. **Treat the fund as untouchable** for non‑emergencies; only draw on it for true unexpected costs.  
5. **Adjust the target** based on income stability and expense volatility – aim for the higher end of the 3–6‑month range if expenses swing widely.
