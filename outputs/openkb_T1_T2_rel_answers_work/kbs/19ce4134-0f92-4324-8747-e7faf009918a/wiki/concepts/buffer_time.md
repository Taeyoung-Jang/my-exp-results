# Buffer Time

**Definition**: Deliberate slack built into a schedule to reduce rush and increase flexibility.

## Overview
Buffer time refers to short periods of intentional slack built between planned activities.  In morning‑routine design it creates a safety net that absorbs small overruns, prevents cascading delays, and helps keep the overall flow feeling relaxed.

## How it appears in the source
In the *session‑08_2024‑06‑15* summary the assistant recommends:
- A **5‑minute buffer** after getting ready on a slow day (7:55 – 8:00).
- A **10‑minute buffer** on a busy day (7:50 – 8:00).
- A **5‑minute buffer** even in a contingency plan when waking at 7:10, leaving a tiny margin before leaving the house.
These buffers are explicitly called out as “buffer time” and are presented as a core part of the routine’s resilience.

## Why it matters
1. **Prevents feeling rushed** – By allocating extra minutes, the routine can absorb minor delays (e.g., a longer shower or a stray email).
2. **Supports habit consistency** – The core rhythm (meditation → movement → breakfast → getting ready) stays intact because the buffer absorbs timing variations rather than forcing habit truncation.
3. **Psychological safety** – Knowing there is slack reduces stress and makes it easier to stick to the routine over the long term.

## Practical tips from the source
- **Plan a 5‑10 minute slack** after the final activity before you need to leave.
- **Adjust buffer length** depending on how tight the morning is (shorter on slow days, longer on busy or uncertain days).
- **Keep the buffer at the end** of the sequence rather than in the middle, so it protects the most critical deadline (the time you need to be out the door).
