# Hybrid Commuting

**Definition**: A transportation strategy that combines occasional personal‑car trips with regular use of public transit and bicycle travel to reduce expenses while retaining flexibility for trips where a car is clearly advantageous.

---

## Overview
Hybrid commuting emerged from the analysis in [[summaries/session-01_2024-06-15]], where the user sought to keep their car but minimize driving. The approach prioritizes transit or biking for routine weekday trips (commute and errands) and reserves the car for occasional needs such as large grocery runs, late‑evening outings, or weekend trips that are less convenient by other modes.

---

## Key Elements
- **Fixed Costs Remain**: Insurance, parking (if retained), and vehicle maintenance are largely unchanged regardless of mileage.
- **Variable Costs Drop**: Fuel consumption falls dramatically when mileage is reduced; for the user, fuel can drop from roughly **$66 / mo** to **$15‑$25 / mo**.
- **Transit Pass**: Maintaining a $105 monthly transit pass covers most weekday trips.
- **Parking Decision**: Dropping a $180 monthly parking pass in favor of $18 daily parking becomes cheaper when driving ≤ 9 days per month, a typical hybrid‑commuting pattern.

---

## Cost Implications
- **Baseline Fixed Costs** (insurance $140, parking $180, maintenance share ≈ $75) ≈ $395 / mo.
- **Fuel**: ~ $20 / mo when driving ~30‑40 mi weekly.
- **Transit Pass**: $105 / mo.
- **Total**: Approximately **$520 ‑ $530 / mo**.
- **If Daily Parking**: $18 × 4‑8 days = $72‑$144, lowering total to **$447‑$459 / mo**.

## When Hybrid Commuting Makes Sense
- The user values car ownership for occasional trips but wants to curb overall spending.
- Fixed expenses dominate; only by eliminating the car entirely would substantial savings be realized.
- However, hybrid commuting offers non‑financial benefits: reduced driving stress, lower vehicle wear, and more predictable commuting via transit or biking.
