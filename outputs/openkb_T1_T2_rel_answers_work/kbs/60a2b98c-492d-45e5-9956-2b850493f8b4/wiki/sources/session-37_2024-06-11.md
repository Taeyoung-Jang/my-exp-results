**User:** I’ve been thinking about joining a faith formation program as an adult, but I’m not sure what to expect emotionally or practically. If someone’s exploring Catholicism (or Christianity more broadly) later in life, what usually helps them stick with it—structure, community, personal study, something else? And how do you tell the difference between genuine curiosity and just craving routine?

**Assistant:** It’s pretty normal to feel a mix of curiosity, uncertainty, and even a little vulnerability when thinking about joining a faith formation program as an adult. Most people don’t walk in with perfect clarity about why they’re there. What tends to help them stay engaged is usually a blend of structure, community, and the space to explore at their own pace. Programs like RCIA or other adult groups give a rhythm to learning and reflection, which many people find grounding. Just as important, though, is being around others who are asking similar questions; that sense of not doing it alone can make the process feel more human and less abstract.

Personal study can help, but it’s usually most helpful when it supports what you’re already encountering in conversation and lived experience. People often stay not because of one single element, but because the combination feels like it’s feeding something in them—intellectually, socially, or spiritually—in a way that’s gentle rather than pressured.

As for knowing whether your interest is genuine curiosity or more of a desire for routine, it can help to notice what part of the idea actually draws you. If you feel pulled toward the beliefs, the questions, or the way the tradition understands meaning and purpose, that’s usually curiosity. If what feels appealing is mainly the predictability or structure of showing up somewhere regularly, that might be a desire for routine. Most of the time, though, people carry a bit of both, and that’s not a bad thing. You don’t have to sort it all out before you begin; the exploration itself often clarifies what’s really going on.

**User:** That makes sense, and honestly I suspect it *is* a mix for me. If I do start something like RCIA or an adult faith group, what are some practical ways to stay grounded when the emotional stuff gets intense (or when it feels like nothing is happening)? And are there any “green flags” in a parish/program that suggest it’s a healthy place to explore without being pushed?

**Assistant:** It helps to have a couple of steadying habits you can fall back on when the experience feels overwhelming or flat. One simple approach is to give yourself a regular moment each week—outside the meetings—to pause and check in with yourself. Not to judge how you’re “performing,” but to notice what’s resonating, what feels confusing, and what feels heavy. Keeping that small rhythm tends to keep things from piling up emotionally. Talking things through with one trusted person who isn’t trying to steer you—whether a friend, a sponsor, or someone on the parish team—can also make the process feel less interior and less dramatic. And when nothing seems to be happening, it’s usually enough to stay present and allow the slower stretches to be part of the journey rather than a sign you’re doing something wrong.

As for choosing a healthy parish or program, the biggest positive sign is a sense of spaciousness: people should be willing to answer questions without rushing you toward conclusions. If the group leaders seem genuinely interested in your experience rather than just presenting information, that’s usually a good indicator. A healthy environment feels welcoming but not clingy, structured but not rigid, and curious about who you are rather than who they want you to become. If you walk in and feel like you can breathe, ask anything, and take your time, that’s almost always a green flag.

**User:** Could you give me a short list of red flags too—things that might look “zealous” or “devout” on the surface but actually signal an unhealthy dynamic in an adult formation group? And if I notice one or two of those, is it better to bring it up with someone, switch parishes, or just step back quietly?

**Assistant:** A few patterns tend to show up in groups that look enthusiastic on the outside but aren’t great for someone who’s sincerely exploring. One is when questions are treated as problems to fix rather than invitations to understand. Another is when leaders talk as though doubt or hesitation means you’re “not committed enough.” A third is a kind of emotional pressure—subtle or not—where you feel you have to match the group’s intensity to belong. And any hint that your personal life or boundaries are suddenly “their business” is a sign to be careful.

If you notice something small—like one awkward comment or a moment that felt off—it can be worth mentioning it to a leader you trust, because sometimes it really is just a misstep. But if the pattern repeats, or you feel yourself shrinking, masking, or getting swept along faster than you’re choosing, it’s usually healthier to step back or visit another parish rather than try to fix it from inside. Exploring faith should feel like you’re gaining space, not losing it.

**User:** I keep thinking about how I spent most of last year going through the RCIA classes at St. Brigid’s—Wednesday nights in the parish hall, coffee that tasted like burnt toast, and a lot of quiet journaling afterward. It was strange how much I needed the structure. If I tried RCIA again somewhere else (or re-engaged more intentionally), what would you suggest I do differently so it’s not just me clinging to routine—like how do I build real community or discernment into it without forcing it?

**Assistant:** You can approach it a little differently this time by giving yourself a gentler posture from the start. Instead of trying to “do RCIA better,” you might treat it as a chance to stay curious about what actually draws you in week to week. The structure can still be comforting, but you don’t have to let it be the whole point.

Community usually grows from small, natural openings rather than big efforts. Simply talking with one or two people after sessions, or letting someone know when something in the discussion hit home for you, can create more connection than trying to “join the group” all at once. Most people in those settings are relieved when someone else breaks the ice.

For discernment, it helps to pay attention to how you feel leaving each meeting rather than during it. If you walk out thoughtful, peaceful, stirred, or even unsettled in a meaningful way, that tells you something. If you walk out numb or just relieved it’s over, that tells you something too. Noticing those patterns over time keeps the process from becoming automatic.

You’re not trying to force community or force clarity—just giving both a bit more room to find you while you show up in a way that feels honest instead of purely habitual.
