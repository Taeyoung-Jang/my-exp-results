**User:** I’ve been thinking about taking on a new role and I’m trying to set myself up for success—what are the most important things to do in the first couple of weeks to make a strong start with a new team?

**Assistant:** In your first couple of weeks, the most valuable thing you can do is focus on relationships and clarity. Spend time getting to know your teammates as people—how they like to work, what they care about, and what they’re currently juggling. It builds trust quickly and gives you a feel for the team’s rhythm.

At the same time, make sure you understand what success looks like in your new role. Ask your manager early about priorities, expectations, and any unspoken norms you should be aware of. You’ll come across as proactive, and it helps you avoid drifting in the wrong direction.

It also helps to observe more than you act at first. Pay attention to how decisions get made, how information flows, and what the team values. Once you understand that, you can start contributing in ways that fit naturally with how the team already works.

And try to pick one small, meaningful win early on. It doesn’t have to be big—just something that shows you’re engaged and reliable. It sets a positive tone and builds momentum for everything that follows.

**User:** Can you help me turn that into a concrete 2-week plan? Like who I should meet with first, what questions to ask my manager vs teammates, and what a “small meaningful win” could look like without stepping on toes.

**Assistant:** You can think of your first two weeks as a gradual widening of your circle: start with your manager, then your closest collaborators, and then the broader team.

Begin by sitting down with your manager as early as possible. Use that conversation to clarify what success looks like in the first three months, what your top one or two priorities should be right now, and how they prefer to communicate. It’s also helpful to ask about any team dynamics you should be aware of and who they recommend you meet first. That gives you a clear starting map.

Once you’ve done that, spend the next several days having short, informal introductions with the people you’ll work with most. These chats don’t need an agenda, but it helps to ask what they’re currently focused on, where their biggest frustrations are, and how you can make collaboration smoother for them. You’re mostly listening and learning—showing interest without trying to solve things too quickly.

During the second week, widen the circle to people adjacent to your role: cross‑functional partners, people whose work you’ll rely on, and anyone who regularly influences decisions. These conversations help you understand how information travels, what the unspoken expectations are, and how things really get done.

A small, meaningful win usually reveals itself during these early conversations. It could be something like cleaning up a lingering documentation gap, taking an annoying recurring task off someone’s plate, fixing a tiny but visible workflow issue, or preparing a clearer version of something the team uses often. The key is choosing something that helps others without changing anyone’s territory or strategy. If you’re unsure, you can simply check with your manager: “I noticed X—would tackling that be helpful, or should I wait?”

By the end of two weeks, your goal isn’t to be fully productive, but to have relationships forming, expectations clarified, and one simple contribution that signals you’re attentive, thoughtful, and here to make the team’s life easier.

**User:** I just signed the paperwork for an 18-month assignment with my new team, and my start date is next Monday. Can you tailor that 2-week plan to a Monday start—like what I should do on day 1 vs the rest of week 1—and include a short set of manager questions I can copy/paste into a calendar invite?

**Assistant:** You can treat your first Monday as orientation and foundation‑setting, then ease into relationship‑building the rest of the week.

On day 1, focus on getting settled, meeting your manager, clarifying the plan for the week, and taking care of any systems or access setup. Keep the day light and observational. Your main goal is to get a feel for the team’s environment and confirm the priorities for your first stretch.

From Tuesday through Friday, start meeting the teammates you’ll work with most closely. Keep those conversations short and friendly, and use them mainly to understand what they’re working on, how they prefer to collaborate, and where you can make their lives easier. Toward the end of the week, you can start noting one or two small tasks you could pick up early in week two to create that “quick win.”

In week two, widen the circle to people slightly outside your immediate group. These conversations help you understand how work flows across the team and reveal small gaps or opportunities where you can contribute without overstepping. By the end of the second week, aim to complete that one small improvement you identified—something tidy and helpful that doesn’t require big decisions.

Here’s a short set of manager questions you can copy into your calendar invite:

What are the top one or two priorities you’d like me to focus on in my first month?  
How will we know I’m on the right track in the first 60–90 days?  
Who should I meet with first, and is there any team history or context I should know before I do?  
How do you prefer to communicate day‑to‑day, and what’s the best way to keep you updated?  
Is there anything I should avoid jumping into too quickly?

Now here are a handful of small, safe wins that work in most environments:

Create a simple, optional template (like a meeting notes doc) that makes things easier without changing anyone’s process.

Proposing or implementing any tool changes, permission revamps, or restructuring—even small ones—without deep context.
