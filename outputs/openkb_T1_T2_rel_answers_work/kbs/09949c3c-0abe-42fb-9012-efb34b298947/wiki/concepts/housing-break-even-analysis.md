## Housing Break‑Even Analysis

**Housing Break‑Even Analysis** is a cost‑comparison framework that estimates the point at which the total expense of renting a furnished unit equals (or exceeds) the combined upfront and resale‑adjusted costs of furnishing an unfurnished unit for a specific stay length.

### Core Elements
1. **Rental price differential** – Monthly rent gap between furnished and unfurnished listings.
2. **Stay duration** – Number of months the renter plans to remain in the location.
3. **Up‑front furniture outlay** – Estimated purchase price for essential used items (bed, sofa/chair, desk/table, basic cookware, etc.).
4. **Resale recovery factor** – Expected proportion of the purchase price that can be recouped when the items are sold or given away at the end of the lease.
5. **Net furniture cost** – Purchase price minus expected resale value.

The break‑even point is reached when:
```
Net furniture cost + (Unfurnished rent × Stay months)  =  Furnished rent × Stay months
```
If the left‑hand side is lower, the unfurnished route is financially preferable; otherwise, the furnished option wins.

### Example from [[summaries/session-08_2024-06-15]]
- **Furnished rent:** $2,300/month
- **Unfurnished rent:** $1,900/month
- **Difference:** $400/month → $2,000 over 5 months
- **Estimated furniture purchase:** $700‑$900
- **Expected resale:** $350‑$500 (≈ 50‑70% recovery)
- **Net furniture cost:** $400‑$600

**Result:** Net cost of unfurnished option ($1,900×5 + $400‑$600 ≈ $9,900‑$10,200) is well below the furnished total ($2,300×5 = $11,500), giving a savings of roughly $1,300‑$1,600.

### Relationship to Other Concepts
- This analysis is a practical application of [[concepts/rental-tradeoffs]], weighing convenience against cost.
- It relies on assumptions about the short‑term rental market, linking to [[concepts/short-term-lease-economics]].
- The resale recovery factor depends on conditions in the [[concepts/used-furniture-market]], such as demand on the local marketplace.

### How to Apply
1. Gather monthly rent figures for both furnished and unfurnished options.
2. List essential items and research local‑marketplace prices for used furniture.
3. Estimate resale values based on condition and market demand.
4. Plug numbers into the break‑even formula.
5. Compare the net total to decide the most economical housing choice.
