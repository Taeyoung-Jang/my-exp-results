## Short‑Term Lease Economics

**Definition**: The set of cost, convenience, and market dynamics that affect the choice between different housing options when a renter expects to stay for a limited period (typically a few months).

### Core Elements
1. **Rent Premium vs. Convenience**
   - Furnished rentals often carry a higher monthly rate but eliminate upfront furniture costs and setup time. For a five‑month stay, the premium can be quantified by comparing the per‑month rent differential (e.g., $400/month in the case study) against the total cost of acquiring and later reselling used furniture.
2. **Up‑Front Investment**
   - Unfurnished units have lower base rent but require an initial outlay for essential items (bed, sofa, desk, cookware). The net expense depends on the purchase price and expected resale value.
3. **Resale Potential**
   - The **used‑furniture market** influences how much of the initial investment can be recovered. In a healthy local marketplace, resale rates of 50‑66% are realistic for items in good condition.
4. **Time & Effort Costs**
   - Time spent sourcing, transporting, and later reselling furniture should be considered an implicit cost. For short stays, the convenience of moving directly into a furnished space often outweighs these hidden expenses.
5. **Market Availability**
   - In some cities, short‑term furnished listings are abundant, while unfurnished options may dominate the long‑term market. The local market condition (often visible on platforms like **local‑marketplace**) can shift the break‑even point.

### Application to the Session‑08 Example
- **Scenario**: $2,300/month furnished vs. $1,900/month unfurnished for a five‑month period.
- **Break‑Even Calculation**: The furnished premium totals roughly $2,000 over the stay. If the net cost of used furniture (purchase minus resale) stays well below this figure—e.g., $400‑$600 as illustrated—the unfurnished route is economically favorable.
- **Key Takeaway**: Short‑term lease economics hinges on quantifying both explicit monetary differences and implicit costs (time, effort, resale risk). When the net cost of furnishing oneself is significantly lower than the rent premium, the unfurnished option wins; otherwise, the furnished premium may be justified.
