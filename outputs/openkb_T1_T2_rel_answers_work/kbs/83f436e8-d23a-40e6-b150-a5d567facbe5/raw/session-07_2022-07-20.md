**Assistant:** You can frame your intentions as ambition pointed in a different direction rather than less ambition overall. One way to say it is that you’re excited about growing your impact through deeper technical mastery, broader architectural responsibilities, and owning more complex problems, rather than through people‑management responsibilities. Another is to emphasize that you want to become someone the team relies on for deep problem‑solving, technical leadership, and setting high engineering standards, and that you believe that’s where you’ll be most valuable.

In a performance review, you can describe your north star as becoming a senior technical expert who raises the bar for the team. That makes it clear you’re choosing a path, not stepping away from growth.

For goals, aim for things that show leadership without management. You can propose owning an end‑to‑end technical area, driving a redesign or optimization project that materially improves system reliability or performance, or becoming the primary subject‑matter expert for a critical part of the stack. It also helps to set goals around influencing technical direction through design docs, RFCs, or mentoring other engineers in specific skills rather than general people management.

For skill growth, call out deeper expertise in a key technology, improved system‑design capabilities, better performance analysis, or leveling up in areas like reliability engineering or data modeling. Progress becomes easy to measure when you tie it to shipped improvements, reduced incidents, measurable performance gains, accepted design proposals, or the number of engineers who successfully adopt the practices or tools you introduce.

If you position it as wanting to increase your impact, gain harder technical responsibilities, and strengthen the team through expertise, managers almost always interpret that as ambition—just the IC kind.

**Assistant:** You can make the OKRs much more “review‑proof” by phrasing them so that (1) they describe *what you are directly driving*, even if the outcome is shared, and (2) you already know what evidence you’ll collect as you go. Here’s how to tune them.

For the distributed‑systems OKR, you can phrase the KRs so they focus on your ownership of the technical direction, the design work, and the execution of the key changes. Something like: “Drive the set of latency improvements that result in a 25% reduction in p99 for the workflow,” or “Lead the reliability initiative that brings incident volume down by 30%.” This makes it clear that you’re doing the leading and the designing, even if other people help implement pieces. Evidence you should gather along the way includes before‑and‑after latency dashboards, links to incident data over time, the design docs you authored and the comments/approvals on them, and any launch notes that show which parts you owned.

For the platform OKR, you can word things the same way: “Lead the effort to introduce an auto‑recovery mechanism that resolves a major failure class,” or “Drive the analysis and optimization work that reduces compute cost by 10–15%.” The idea is that the verbs—lead, drive, design, define, implement key components—make the attribution obvious. Good evidence here is recurring‑incident reports, cost dashboards with snapshots at each stage, the design or ADRs you authored, the rollout plan you drove, and any adoption stats from teams who started using the new platform capabilities.

For both sets, it also helps to keep a simple folder or running doc where you save everything: screenshots of dashboards at milestones, summaries of incidents closed, design docs with timestamps, and short notes on what you personally delivered.

For the monthly check‑in, something very lightweight works best. A quick message or short document that says what you shipped, what you learned, and what’s next is enough. You can frame it like this: a short opening line saying you’re sending a quick update on your IC‑track goals, a paragraph summarizing the progress for the month with the numbers you moved, another paragraph calling out any design work, architecture discussions, or decisions you drove, and a final short note on what you’re planning for the next month. Keeping it brief and factual makes it easy for your manager to forward or reuse in your review later, and it builds a clear paper trail of consistent IC‑track impact.
