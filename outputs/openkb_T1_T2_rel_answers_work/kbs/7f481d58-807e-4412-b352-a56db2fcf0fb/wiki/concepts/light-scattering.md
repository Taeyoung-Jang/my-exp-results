# Light Scattering

Light scattering refers to the redirection of incoming light rays by particles, droplets, or molecules in the atmosphere. The phenomenon determines how we perceive visibility, colour, and the overall “soft focus” appearance of a scene.

## How it works
- **Interaction with particles** – When sunlight encounters tiny aerosols, water droplets, or dust, the light is redirected in many directions. The smaller the particles relative to the light wavelength, the more uniformly the light is scattered.
- **Angle of sunlight** – Early morning sun travels through a longer atmospheric path, increasing the chances of scattering and producing a muted, glowing look.
- **Effect of humidity** – Higher moisture content adds more water droplets, enhancing scattering and creating haze.
- **Temperature inversions** – When a warm layer sits above cooler surface air, it can trap aerosols, allowing a sustained scattering layer that appears as a uniform veil.

## Observational clues linked to scattering
- **Low‑contrast, pastel lighting** – Distant objects appear washed out while nearby edges remain relatively sharp when scattering is dominated by an inversion layer.
- **Diffuse shadows** – Scattering softens shadows, especially when moisture‑driven haze is present.
- **Persistence** – An inversion‑held scattering layer resists light breezes and can linger until midday, whereas moisture‑driven scattering dissipates as the sun warms the surface.
