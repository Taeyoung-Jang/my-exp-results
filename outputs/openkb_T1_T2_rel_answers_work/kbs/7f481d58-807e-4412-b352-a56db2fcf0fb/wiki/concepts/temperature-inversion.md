# Temperature Inversion

A **temperature inversion** occurs when a layer of warmer air sits above cooler air near the ground, creating a stable lid that suppresses vertical mixing. This condition often traps moisture, aerosols, and pollutants, leading to persistent low‑visibility haze.

## How it forms
- **Surface cooling**: During night or early morning, the ground loses heat faster than the air above, cooling the near‑surface layer.
- **Warm overlay**: A higher‑altitude air mass remains warmer, forming an inversion cap.
- **Stability**: The temperature gradient inhibits convection, so particles and moisture cannot rise and disperse.

## Observable effects (see [[summaries/session-07_2024-07-15]])
- **Uniform veil**: Haze appears as a suspended layer, often visible partway up hillsides.
- **Dry feel**: The air may feel relatively dry even though visibility is low.
- **Resistance to wind**: Light breezes struggle to disperse the haze because the warm cap blocks vertical mixing.
- **Contrast**: Distant objects look washed‑out and bluish, while nearby features retain sharper edges.

## Distinguishing from moisture‑driven fog
| Feature | Temperature Inversion | Moisture-driven Fog |
|---|---|---|
| Layer position | Suspended lid, above ground | Ground‑hugging blanket, pools in valleys |
| Reaction to sun | Persists into midday, slowly clears as inversion breaks | Thins rapidly as surface warms |
| Reaction to wind | Little change with light breeze | Disperses quickly with gentle wind |
