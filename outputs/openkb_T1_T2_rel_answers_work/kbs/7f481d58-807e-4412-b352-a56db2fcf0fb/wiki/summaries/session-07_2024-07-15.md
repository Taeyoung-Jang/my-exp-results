## Summary of Session 7 (2024‑07‑15)

- **Key drivers of morning haze**: a mix of [[concepts/humidity]], [[concepts/temperature-inversion]], fine aerosol/droplet particles, and the low angle of sunlight that increase [[concepts/light-scattering]].
- **Moisture‑driven haze (fog)**: cool air near the ground cannot hold much moisture, so tiny droplets remain suspended. It tends to sit low, feel damp, and fades as the sun warms the surface and light breezes mix the air.
- **Inversion‑trapped haze**: a layer of warmer air above cooler surface air creates a stable lid that holds aerosols and moisture aloft. The haze appears as a uniform veil, often dry to the touch, and is resistant to light wind.

### Distinguishing cues (no equipment needed)
1. **Layer position** – Inversion: a suspended “lid” visible partway up hillsides; Moisture: a ground‑hugging blanket that pools in valleys.
2. **Contrast & shadows** – Inversion: distant objects look washed‑out and bluish, while nearby features retain sharp edges; Moisture: even nearby shadows appear faint and edges are softened.
3. **Response to sun & wind** – Inversion: persists into midday and remains unchanged with a gentle breeze; Moisture: thins unevenly as the sun heats and a light wind stirs the air.

### Role of wind
- Light breeze quickly disperses moisture‑driven fog by mixing dry and saturated air.
- The same breeze has little effect on an inversion‑held haze because the warm cap prevents vertical mixing.

### Practical “at‑a‑glance” checklist
- Does the haze feel like a **suspended layer** or a **moist blanket**?
- Are **nearby objects still crisp** while distant ones are washed out?
- Does a **light wind change anything**?

These observations help decide whether the persistent morning softness is due to a **[[concepts/temperature-inversion]]** trapping aerosols/pollution or simply **[[concepts/humidity]]**‑driven fog.
