**User:** I’ve been on a kick lately trying to level up my home cooking, especially when it comes to sauces and flavor-building. If you were stocking a beginner-friendly “sauce toolkit” for someone who wants to cook more from scratch, what would you include (ingredients, pantry staples, and a few foundational techniques)?

**Assistant:** For a beginner‑friendly sauce toolkit, it helps to have a small group of ingredients that you can mix and match to build lots of different flavors without feeling overwhelmed. A few versatile pantry items go a long way: good olive oil for everyday cooking, a neutral oil for high heat, and an acid or two like vinegar or lemon to brighten sauces. Aromatics such as garlic, onions, and shallots are the backbone of most sauces, and having tomato paste, soy sauce, mustard, and a couple of chiles or chili pastes gives you instant depth and complexity. Keeping some broth, butter, and a basic dairy option like cream or yogurt means you can move toward creamy, silky sauces whenever you want. Herbs and spices round out the kit; dried oregano, thyme, smoked paprika, and a spice blend you like will cover a lot of ground.

As for techniques, start with sweating aromatics gently in oil, because it’s the foundation of most stovetop sauces. Deglazing a pan with wine, vinegar, or broth after sautéing something builds instant flavor and teaches you how to turn pan drippings into something delicious. Learning how to reduce liquid until it thickens naturally is another key skill, since many sauces rely on concentration rather than added thickeners. Making a quick pan sauce after cooking meat or vegetables is a great way to practice all of this at once. And finally, emulsifying—whisking oil into something watery, like mustard or lemon juice—opens the door to vinaigrettes, mayo‑style sauces, and plenty of variations once you get the feel for it.

With just those ingredients and a grasp of those basics, you can riff on an enormous range of sauces without needing complicated recipes.

**User:** Could you give me a concrete “starter list” with quantities (like what to buy for the next month), and then 5–6 scratch sauce templates I can practice on repeat—like a basic pan sauce, a vinaigrette, a tomato sauce, a simple curry-ish sauce, and maybe one creamy option? I’d love them written as flexible ratios rather than rigid recipes.

**Assistant:** Here’s a compact “month‑long” starter bundle and the flexible sauce templates you can practice. I’ll keep everything in plain sentences instead of bullet lists.

For the pantry, a liter bottle of olive oil and a small bottle (about 500 ml) of a neutral oil will cover almost everything. A small jar of tomato paste, a 250 ml bottle of soy sauce, a standard 350 g jar of Dijon mustard, a 250 ml bottle of vinegar (red wine or apple cider), a 250 ml bottle of fish sauce if you enjoy savory depth, and a small tube or jar of chili paste are all you really need. Add a kilogram of onions, a couple of garlic bulbs, a few lemons, and about 200 g of butter. A liter of broth and about 250 ml of cream or a tub of plain yogurt rounds things out. For dried herbs and spices, a small jar each of oregano, thyme, smoked paprika, and a curry powder or masala blend is plenty.

For a basic pan sauce, think in terms of equal parts fat and deglazing liquid, meaning roughly one spoon of fat (butter or oil) to one spoon of shallots or garlic, cooked until soft, then one part wine or broth to two parts stock if you want it thinner or one to one if you want it syrupy. Let it reduce until it clings to a spoon, then swirl in a small knob of butter at the end.

For a vinaigrette, use one acidic part to three parts oil, plus a small spoon of mustard to help it emulsify. Adjust by adding more acid if it tastes flat or a trickle more oil if it’s too sharp.

For a tomato sauce, start with about one spoon of olive oil to one small onion, then add one spoon of tomato paste and cook it briefly until darkened. Add one can of tomatoes (or about 400 g) and a splash of water, then simmer until it thickens. Season with salt, a pinch of sugar if needed, and herbs to taste.

For a simple curry‑style sauce, sauté aromatics in a spoon or two of oil, add one spoon of curry powder or masala, then stir in one part tomato (a half cup of crushed tomatoes works) to one part liquid (broth or coconut milk). Let it reduce until glossy and adjust with salt and a squeeze of lemon.

For a creamy sauce, start with one spoon of butter and one spoon of flour cooked briefly together, then whisk in three to four parts warm milk, broth, or cream until it reaches the thickness you want. You can enrich it with a spoon of mustard, a handful of cheese, or herbs.

These ratios stay stable even as you swap flavors, so practicing them a few times builds confidence fast.

**User:** One constraint: I make it a point to avoid pre-made sauces because I value the process of building the dish myself. With that in mind, can you tweak the starter list so it leans even more toward base ingredients (like whole spices vs blends, canned tomatoes vs jarred sauce), and suggest a simple weekly practice plan using those ratio templates?

**Assistant:** You can shift the starter list toward truer “building blocks” without making it complicated. Instead of blends, keep a few whole spices like cumin seeds, coriander seeds, and whole dried chilies; they give you far more flexibility. A small bag of each will easily last a month. Keep canned whole or crushed tomatoes rather than any jarred sauce. Add a small jar of whole peppercorns, a stick or two of cinnamon, and a thumb of fresh ginger if you use it often. Everything else from the earlier list stays the same: olive oil, neutral oil, vinegar, soy sauce, tomato paste, onions, garlic, lemons, butter, broth, and either cream or yogurt.

A simple weekly practice plan can fit neatly into regular meals so it doesn’t feel like homework. Early in the week, make a quick vinaigrette using the one‑to‑three acid‑to‑oil ratio and use it on a salad or roasted vegetables. The next night, cook something in a skillet and turn the browned bits into a pan sauce with the equal‑parts aromatics and deglazing liquid approach. Later in the week, simmer a batch of tomato sauce using the oil‑aromatic‑paste‑tomato ratio; freeze whatever you don’t use. Over the weekend, make a small curry‑style sauce by toasting a spoon of your whole spices, adding aromatics, and then using the equal parts tomato and liquid approach. On a quieter night, practice the creamy sauce by making a quick roux and whisking in milk or broth until silky.

Repeating each template once a week gives you muscle memory without much extra effort, and the whole‑ingredient pantry keeps everything adaptable as you learn your own preferences.

**User:** Could you be more specific on the “base ingredient” shopping list with rough quantities (like how many cans of tomatoes, how much butter, onions, garlic, lemons, and which whole spices), and also suggest a 4-week schedule where each week focuses on one template but still uses the others in smaller ways so I’m practicing everything without getting bored?

**Assistant:** For a month of cooking from true base ingredients, you can stock up once and be set. Two to three cans of whole or crushed tomatoes (about 400 g each) will cover repeated tomato‑ and curry‑style sauces. A half‑kilo block of butter is usually enough for pan sauces and the occasional creamy sauce. Around six to eight onions and two full garlic bulbs will give you aromatics for the month. Four to six lemons handle vinaigrettes and finishing acidity. For whole spices, a small jar (50–75 g) each of cumin seeds and coriander seeds, a small handful of whole dried chilies, and a small jar of whole peppercorns is plenty. Add one cinnamon stick and a thumb of fresh ginger if you like it in curries. Keep olive oil, a neutral oil, vinegar, tomato paste, and a liter of broth on hand, plus about 250 ml of cream or a tub of plain yogurt.

A four‑week practice plan can revolve around one “focus” sauce per week, while still touching the others lightly so everything stays familiar. In the first week, center your cooking on vinaigrettes by making a few variations of the one‑to‑three acid‑to‑oil ratio; use them on salads and on roasted vegetables. During that same week, try at least one quick pan sauce after a weeknight protein and fold a spoon of tomato paste into a simple skillet dish for practice.

In the second week, make the tomato sauce your main project by simmering one full can with aromatics and paste until thick and sweet. Use extra sauce for different meals. Keep your vinaigrette skills fresh by making a small jar to use throughout the week, and finish one dinner with a fast pan sauce.

In the third week, focus on a curry‑style sauce by toasting a spoon of cumin and coriander seeds, then building the equal‑parts tomato and liquid base. Use different vegetables or proteins so it feels varied. Keep a simple tomato sauce in rotation one night, and make at least one vinaigrette without measuring so you keep building intuition.

In the fourth week, concentrate on the creamy sauce by making a small roux and whisking in milk or broth until smooth, then flavoring it with herbs, mustard, or cheese. Fit in one curry‑style sauce earlier in the week and one quick pan sauce near the end so everything stays in your muscle memory.

By the end of the month, each technique will feel natural, and the base ingredients will still leave you room to improvise.
