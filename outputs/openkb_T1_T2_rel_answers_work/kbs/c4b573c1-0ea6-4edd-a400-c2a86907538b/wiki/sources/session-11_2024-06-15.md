**User:** I’m trying to map out my next few career steps in a STEM/engineering-type field, and I keep hearing about “technical” paths versus “leadership” paths. How do people usually decide between them, and what kinds of questions should I be asking myself early on to avoid getting boxed into the wrong track?

**Assistant:** People usually land on one path or the other by paying attention to what parts of the work actually energize them day to day. A technical path tends to fit people who like solving hard problems, diving deep, and being the person others come to for expertise. A leadership path fits people who enjoy shaping direction, coordinating others, and spending more time influencing than building.

The most useful questions to ask yourself early on are the ones about what kind of work you want most of your future days to look like. Do you enjoy long stretches of individual problem‑solving, or do you prefer guiding others and clearing obstacles for them? Do you want to get better and better at a specific domain, or are you more excited about moving across domains to manage projects and teams? How much time do you want to spend communicating, negotiating, and making decisions with incomplete information?

It also helps to pay attention to how you feel when you watch people a few levels ahead of you. If their jobs look appealing rather than draining, that’s a good sign. And if you’re unsure, you can usually experiment: take on a small leadership responsibility, or volunteer for a deep technical task, and notice which one feels more like the kind of stress you want in your life.

**User:** If I want to stay on the technical track, what are the best ways to signal that to my manager without sounding like I’m not a “team player”? Also, what kinds of roles/titles should I look for (or avoid) if I want to keep doing hands-on lab/engineering work long-term?

**Assistant:** You can usually signal a preference for the technical track simply by framing it around the impact you want to have, rather than what you want to avoid. For example, you might tell your manager that you feel most effective when you’re solving complex engineering problems, developing new techniques, or owning technical depth that the team can rely on. Emphasizing that you want to contribute at a higher level by becoming a technical anchor or subject‑matter expert makes it clear that you’re still very much a team player—just in a different way than people who gravitate toward coordination or people‑management work.

It also helps to show that you’re still willing to collaborate, mentor, and communicate. Managers get nervous only when “technical track” sounds like “I don’t want to work with people.” If you make it clear that you’re happy to partner with others while keeping your core focus on engineering, you’ll come across as balanced rather than siloed.

As for roles, aim for titles that include things like engineer, scientist, specialist, or architect. Senior, staff, and principal versions of those titles usually signal deeper technical responsibility rather than people management. Roles to be cautious about are ones centered on project or program management, operations management, or anything where the primary deliverables are schedules, budgets, or personnel decisions rather than technical output. Sometimes titles like “lead engineer” can go either way, so it’s worth asking how much hands‑on time versus coordination time the role actually involves.

If you keep making it clear through your interests, the projects you volunteer for, and the skills you develop that you want to grow as a technical expert, most managers will support that path rather than push you toward leadership by default.

**User:** I’m planning to stay on the technical track; honestly, I prefer focusing on the lab work rather than taking on management duties. Given that, what’s a good way to say that in a 1:1 in a way that still sounds ambitious, and what kinds of concrete growth goals (skills, project ownership, mentoring, publications/patents, etc.) should I propose so my manager sees a clear progression?

**Assistant:** You can frame it in a 1:1 as ambition toward depth rather than toward managing people. Something like: you’ve noticed that your strongest impact comes from solving difficult technical problems and driving experiments forward, and you want to grow into someone the team relies on for deep expertise and high‑stakes technical execution. That keeps the tone positive, forward‑looking, and clearly aligned with the team’s goals.

For growth goals, think in terms of leveling up your scope rather than changing your job type. You could aim to take ownership of a more complex subsystem or research area, drive a project from concept through validation, or become the go‑to person on a specific technical method. You can also add one or two “beyond yourself” goals, like mentoring junior engineers or documenting techniques so the team moves faster. If your field values publications or patents, choosing one substantial contribution to formalize each year can also signal strong technical ambition.

The main idea is to show that you’re not avoiding responsibility—you’re choosing a type of responsibility that’s about technical depth, reliability, and influence through expertise rather than through direct management. That usually lands very well with managers.

**User:** Can you give me a few ready-to-use scripts for that 1:1—like a short version (30 seconds), a longer version (2–3 minutes), and one if my manager pushes back with “but leadership is the next step”? Also, could you suggest 6–8 specific, measurable growth goals I could propose that keep me hands-on in the lab while still increasing my scope and impact?

**Assistant:** Here are the scripts in plain, conversational form.

For the short version, you could say something like: “I’ve realized my strongest impact comes from deep technical work, especially in the lab. I’d like to keep growing on the technical track and take on increasingly complex problems so I can become a go‑to expert for the team.”

For a slightly longer version, you might say: “I’ve been paying attention to the parts of my work where I’m both most effective and most energized, and it’s the hands‑on technical side—designing experiments, troubleshooting complex issues, and driving technical decisions. I’d like to keep developing along the technical track and grow toward being the person people rely on for depth and execution. I’m happy to take on more responsibility, but I want that responsibility to be in the form of technical ownership, higher‑impact projects, and mentoring others in the technical parts of the work rather than management duties.”

If your manager pushes back with “but leadership is the next step,” you can say: “I definitely want to grow, and I see leadership as part of that—but leadership through expertise. I’d rather lead by driving technical decisions, mentoring juniors on the technical side, and owning complex problem areas. That’s where I can contribute the most. I’m not ruling anything out long‑term, but right now the technical path is where I can make the biggest impact.”

For measurable growth goals that keep you hands‑on, you could propose things like taking full technical ownership of a more complex subsystem or research area; driving a project from early concept through lab validation; becoming the in‑house expert on a specific technique or tool and documenting best practices; designing and executing a challenging experimental campaign that unblocks a team goal; mentoring a junior engineer specifically on lab methods or analysis; submitting a patent or internal invention disclosure tied to your work; or presenting a technical deep dive to your group to solidify your expertise and help others move faster.
