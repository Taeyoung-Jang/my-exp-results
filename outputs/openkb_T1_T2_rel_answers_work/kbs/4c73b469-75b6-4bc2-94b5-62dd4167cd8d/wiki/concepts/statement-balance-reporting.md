# Statement Balance Reporting

**Definition**: The practice by credit‑card issuers of reporting the account balance that exists on the *statement closing date* (the date the monthly statement is generated) to credit bureaus and internal risk models.

---

## Why it matters
- The reported balance determines the **credit utilization** figure that appears on your credit report. A lower reported balance can improve your utilization ratio, which is a key factor in many credit‑scoring models.
- Issuers use the same balance to assess account risk, decide on tier changes, and consider limit‑increase requests.

---

## Key details from [[summaries/session-33_2025-02-13]]
- **Timing**: Most issuers snapshot the balance on the statement closing date, *not* the due‑date. Paying down the balance before this date keeps the reported utilization low while still allowing regular card usage.
- **Practical tip**: With a small limit, make a payment whenever the balance approaches ~10‑30 % of the limit. This keeps the balance that gets reported comfortably low.
- **Impact on requests**: A consistently low reported balance over a few months signals stability and can improve the odds of a tier or limit increase.

## Practical recommendations
1. **Identify your statement closing date** – it is listed on your monthly statement.
2. **Schedule a payment** a few days before that date if your balance is nearing a high utilization threshold.
3. **Track reported balances** via your credit‑reporting agency to confirm the snapshot reflects your intended low utilization.
4. **Avoid large, irregular spikes** close to the closing date, as they can inflate the reported balance.

---

## When to let a balance report vs. $0
- Reporting a small balance (e.g., under 10 % of the limit) shows activity without raising utilization.
- Reporting $0 is also acceptable and signals no revolving debt, but some issuers may prefer to see modest usage to confirm the card is being used.
