**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about how people choose what to study and how that choice shapes their career options later. If you were starting from scratch today, what factors would you weigh most—personal interest, job market, earning potential, or something else—and how would you test whether a path is a good fit before committing?

**Assistant:** I’d start with personal interest, because it’s hard to stick with something you don’t genuinely like, but I’d keep an eye on whether that interest has realistic ways to turn into work. I’d also look at the job market—not obsessively, just enough to see if the field is growing, stable, or shrinking—and I’d check whether the earning potential matches the lifestyle I want.

To test the fit, I’d try small, low‑commitment experiments before diving in. That could be talking to people who do the job, shadowing for a day, taking an intro course, or doing a small project that mimics real work. If the day‑to‑day tasks feel energizing rather than draining, that’s usually a good sign the path is worth pursuing.

**User:** Could you walk me through a practical way to do those “low‑commitment experiments” while I’m in school—like a 2–4 week plan with specific steps (people to talk to, questions to ask, small projects to try) if I’m deciding between something like computer science, business, and psychology?

**Assistant:** You can treat the next few weeks like a set of mini test‑drives, rotating your attention across the three fields without committing to any of them. During the first week, try setting up brief chats with one student and one working professional from each area. You can reach out through your school’s career center, professors, or LinkedIn. In each conversation, ask what a typical day looks like, which parts of the work feel rewarding or tedious, and what skills actually matter once you’re doing the job. The goal isn’t to get career advice; it’s to see whether their description sounds like something you’d enjoy living with.

In the second week, dip into each subject’s “intro level” experience. That could mean doing a short online coding tutorial for computer science, reading a beginner‑friendly business case study or trying a simple budgeting or market‑analysis exercise for business, and watching a quick lecture or trying a short personality‑research activity for psychology. Pay attention not to whether you’re instantly good at it, but to whether you’re curious enough to keep going for another hour.

In the third and fourth weeks, choose the two fields that felt most interesting and try a slightly deeper project in each. For computer science, that might be building a tiny program or automating something in your own life. For business, you could analyze a company you like and write a short “what I’d improve” memo. For psychology, you might run a very small, informal survey with friends and try interpreting the results. As you work, notice how you feel: energized, bored, frustrated in a good way, or drained.

By the end, you’ll have talked to real people, sampled each field’s basic tasks, and tried doing a bit of the work yourself. The one that feels the most naturally interesting during the day‑to‑day moments—not the one that looks best on paper—usually points you in the right direction.

**User:** That helps a lot. Could you give me a list of 5–7 specific questions to ask in those student/professional chats that work across all three fields, plus a few “field-specific” questions for CS vs business vs psychology? Also, how would you suggest I track my reactions during the projects so I’m not just going off gut feeling in the moment?

**Assistant:** Here are the general questions you can use. I’ll give them as a single flowing set so it’s still conversational. You can ask things like: What does a normal day actually look like for you? Which parts of the work feel energizing and which feel routine or frustrating? What skills do you use most often that you didn’t expect when you started? What would you tell your past self before choosing this major or job? How much of your time goes to independent work versus collaboration? How did you realize this field was (or wasn’t) the right fit?

For field‑specific questions, you can sprinkle in a few. In computer science, you might ask how much time they spend coding versus planning, debugging, or discussing design decisions. In business, you can ask how data‑driven the work is versus relationship‑driven and how much pressure there is around deadlines. In psychology, you can ask whether the work feels more research‑heavy, people‑focused, or paperwork‑heavy, and how emotionally draining or rewarding it tends to be.

To track your reactions, keep a quick daily or per‑session log with just a few sentences after each small project or conversation. Note what parts felt fun or absorbing, what parts dragged, and whether you found yourself wanting to keep going once the “assigned” time was up. After a couple of weeks, patterns usually appear on their own, and you can make the decision based on those patterns rather than just whatever mood you happened to be in that day.

**User:** Just to give you some context, I'm currently pursuing my bachelor's degree. Given that, could you tailor those questions and the tracking method to what’s realistic for a busy undergrad—like how many chats to aim for, how long to spend on each mini‑project, and what a simple scorecard template might look like?

**Assistant:** As a busy undergrad, you can keep this whole process light. Aim for one or two short chats per field over a couple of weeks. Fifteen minutes is enough if you come in with clear questions. In each conversation, you can ask what their normal week looks like, which parts of the work feel enjoyable or draining, which skills they actually use every day, and what surprised them once they got deeper into the field. Then add one field‑specific question, like how much time is spent coding versus planning in computer science, how much of business work actually involves data versus people, or how emotionally taxing the work feels in psychology.

For the mini‑projects, thirty to sixty minutes per field is enough to get a feel without derailing your schedule. You don’t need anything polished; you’re just checking whether the work feels natural to sit with.

A simple scorecard can be a short repeated prompt rather than anything structured. After each activity or conversation, jot down something like: “What parts felt good? What parts dragged? Would I willingly do this again tomorrow? How curious am I to go a little deeper?” If you want a quick numeric cue, you can add a one‑to‑five rating for enjoyment and another for “would I keep exploring this?” Over a couple of weeks, the field that consistently gets the better notes usually becomes obvious.
