# Repair vs. Replacement

## Definition
A decision framework that treats a repair as "buying time" and compares its cost and lifespan extension against the expense and benefits of purchasing a new device.

## Core Principles
1. **Cost Threshold** – A repair is generally worthwhile if its price is less than roughly one‑third of the cost of a comparable new device.
2. **Life Extension** – The repair should add at least one to two years of comfortable, frustration‑free use.
3. **Functionality Limits** – If the device is already hitting software‑update or security limits, a repair may not solve the underlying problem.

## Application Steps
1. Identify any **[[concepts/real_problem_triggers]]** that are prompting the upgrade discussion.
2. Estimate how much longer the repair would keep the device free from those triggers.
3. Compare the repair cost to **≈ 33 %** of a new device’s price.
4. If the repair meets both the cost and life‑extension criteria, choose repair; otherwise, consider replacement.

## Typical Scenarios
- **Phone battery swap** – Often cheap (< 33 % of a new phone) and adds > 1 year of use.
- **Laptop storage upgrade** – Cost‑effective when CPU/RAM are still adequate; can extend life by a couple of years.
- **High‑cost component replacement** – If a part costs approaching the price of a new laptop, replacement is usually smarter.

## Relationship to Other Concepts
- Aligns with [[concepts/upgrade_decision_criteria]]: upgrades should solve a real problem rather than add nice‑to‑have features.
- Informs the decision tree described in [[summaries/session-11_2024-06-15]].

## Exceptions
Even when the cost‑threshold rule suggests replacement, an early upgrade may be justified if:
- A specific new feature dramatically improves workflow (e.g., faster compile times).
- The device poses a security risk due to end‑of‑updates.
- Repairs are unavailable or would require multiple costly fixes.
