**User:** I’ve been thinking a lot about what life actually looks like after residency match—like the day-to-day rhythm, how people keep themselves functional, and whether there’s any realistic way to protect sleep and relationships. If you were advising someone about to start intern year, what habits or systems would you suggest setting up early?

**Assistant:** I’d tell them to think less in terms of “big life overhauls” and more in terms of a few small routines that make the chaos livable.

The first is having a predictable personal anchor at the start or end of the day—something tiny but consistent, like ten quiet minutes with coffee, a walk around the block, or a quick journal line. It sounds trivial, but that sense of “my day has a beginning and an end” keeps you from feeling like you’re dissolving into the work.

The second is to treat sleep as a protected appointment rather than a luxury. You won’t always succeed, but deciding ahead of time what your cutoff is, how you wind down quickly, and how you handle post‑call sleep makes a huge difference. Interns who do best aren’t the ones who sleep the most; they’re the ones who recover deliberately.

The third is to set expectations with the people you care about before things get hectic. If everyone understands that you may disappear for a week but you still care, it prevents a lot of quiet resentment. Even short check‑ins—texting a photo of your lunch or calling during your walk home—can keep relationships alive in a surprisingly real way.

The last thing is to automate anything you can. Meal prep, laundry schedules, rides, finances, even laying out clothes the night before. The fewer small decisions you have to make, the more your brain can tolerate the rest of what intern year throws at you.

None of this makes the year easy, but it makes it survivable without losing who you are in the process.

**User:** That makes sense. Can you get really concrete about the sleep piece—like what a post-call recovery plan should look like (nap length, caffeine cutoff, light exposure, when to try going to bed “normally” again)? Also, what are a few “automation” things you’ve seen actually stick for interns when the schedule is unpredictable?

**Assistant:** For post‑call days, the goal is just to land the plane gently rather than to “fix” your sleep in one shot. Most interns do best with a short nap when they get home—about an hour, maybe ninety minutes at most. Long naps tend to push your bedtime so far back that you end up in a weird jet‑lag loop. Caffeine is better avoided after the latter half of the shift so it can actually wear off by the time you’re trying to sleep. When you wake up from that short nap, get some light on your face and a real meal in your system, even if it’s small. Then stay awake through the afternoon and aim for a normal bedtime that night, even if you feel foggy. The “reset” usually happens by the next morning, not the same day.

For automation, the things that survive unpredictable rotations are the ones that don’t require motivation. Interns stick with having groceries delivered on the same day every week, using repeating calendar reminders for bills and simple chores, keeping a default set of meals or snacks stocked so they never have to think about what to eat, and setting up a consistent morning bag‑check routine so they’re never scrambling for badges, chargers, or pens at 4:45 a.m. Anything that removes a decision instead of creating one is the stuff that actually lasts.

**User:** Can you lay out a sample “post-call day” schedule hour-by-hour for, say, getting home at 11am after a 28-hour call—like when to nap, shower, eat, get sunlight, and what to do if you accidentally sleep 4 hours? And for automation: what’s your go-to “always packed” call bag checklist + a simple meal/snack system that works when you don’t know if you’ll get a break?

**Assistant:** Here’s one way to describe a post‑call day without turning it into a rigid list. Imagine you walk in the door around 11 a.m., drop your stuff, drink a little water, maybe shower if it helps you unwind, and lie down by about 11:30. You let yourself sleep for roughly an hour—long enough to take the edge off but not long enough to push your circadian clock. When you wake up around 12:30 or 1 p.m., you open the blinds or step outside for ten minutes so your brain knows it’s daytime, then eat something simple and grounding. After that, you stay awake through the afternoon doing light, low‑stakes things—laundry, an episode of a show, a walk—nothing mentally heavy. Around your usual bedtime, maybe 9 or 10 p.m., you wind down and go to bed again, even if you feel a bit “wired but tired.” The real reset happens the next morning.

If you accidentally crash for four hours and wake up in the late afternoon, don’t panic. Get light exposure right away, have a meal, stay awake until a later but still reasonable bedtime—something like midnight—and accept that your schedule will drift slightly for a day or two before settling again.

For the call‑bag question, think of it as a bag that never gets unpacked. It always has scrubs, a warm layer, deodorant, a charger, spare socks, a couple of pens, lip balm, a tiny toiletries kit, and a pair of foam earplugs for any chance at sleep. Snacks live there too—things that hold up forever and don’t require refrigeration, like nuts, jerky, granola bars, instant oatmeal packets, or a shelf‑stable smoothie. The idea is that you never have to assemble this bag; you just restock it when you notice something missing.

Food usually works best when you have a “default system.” Interns do well with one ready‑to‑eat meal in the fridge at all times—something like a big container of pasta, rice and veggies, or chili—and a small stash of grab‑and‑eat items in their work bag so they can eat in two‑minute bites between tasks. It’s less about planning perfect meals and more about never being fully empty or dependent on the cafeteria actually being open.

**User:** Since the residency match, my weeks have turned into a string of 28-hour calls and crash-sleep in the on-call room; I keep a spare toothbrush in my locker and my social life is mostly whoever’s on the same service grabbing a ten-minute coffee between pages. Given that reality, can you tailor that post-call schedule to what actually happens when you *don’t* reliably get home at 11am (sometimes it’s 1–2pm), and include what to do if you’re too keyed up to nap even though you’re exhausted? Also, what are 2–3 ways to protect at least one relationship or friendship when “free time” is basically those ten-minute coffee windows?

**Assistant:** If you get home closer to one or two in the afternoon, the idea stays the same but shrinks a bit. You walk in, drop your stuff, rinse off if it helps, drink some water, and lie down as soon as you reasonably can. Even if that means trying for a nap at 1:30 or 2, you still keep it short—something in the range of a brief doze rather than a true sleep cycle. If you’re too wired to fall asleep, lying down in a dark, quiet room for twenty or thirty minutes still gives your brain a partial reset. After that, you get some daylight on your face and eat something simple. Then you stay awake until a normal bedtime, even if “normal” needs to shift later by an hour for that day. The key is not trying to catch up in a single afternoon, just guiding yourself back toward your usual rhythm by the following morning.

If you accidentally sleep too long, you just treat the rest of the day like a late‑arrival afternoon. Get light, eat, stay awake, and aim for a bedtime that’s reasonable rather than perfect. It’s the overall pattern that matters, not the exact clock time.

As for relationships, the trick is to make connection friction‑free. One way is to have a standing micro‑check‑in with someone you care about—maybe a good‑morning or end‑of‑shift voice note you send no matter what. Another is to pick one person and give them “drop‑in access,” meaning they know they can text you anytime and you’ll answer when you get a quiet moment without feeling like it’s an obligation. And sometimes the most protective thing is scheduling a tiny ritual, like sharing one meme a day or a two‑minute phone call on your walk from the hospital to your car. When your life is compressed, the small consistent touchpoints end up feeling surprisingly real and sustaining.
