# Adrenaline De‑Escalation

**Type:** Concept

**Description:** A short, repeatable routine that lowers post‑call adrenaline, promotes physiological calm, and smooths the transition to rest.

---

## Overview
Adrenaline de‑escalation is a micro‑habit designed for professionals (e.g., medical staff) who experience sudden spikes of stress hormones after urgent, late‑night events. The goal is to **quickly down‑regulate the sympathetic nervous system** so the body can move from a high‑alert "problem‑solving" state to a relaxed, sleep‑ready state.

The protocol is mentioned in [[summaries/session-38_2025-01-09]] and builds on the broader ideas of [[concepts/anchor_habits]] and [[concepts/grounding_techniques]].

---

## Core Steps (2–5 minutes)
1. **Slow exhalation breathing** – 2 minutes
   - Inhale for 3 seconds, exhale for 6 seconds (the exhale is twice as long as the inhale). This type of breathing activates the parasympathetic nervous system.

2. **Progressive muscle relaxation** – 1 minute
   - Sequentially relax the jaw, shoulders, and hands, holding each relaxation for a few seconds. This reduces muscular tension that often accompanies adrenaline.

3. **Brain dump (mental off‑load)** – 30 seconds–1 minute
   - Write down any lingering tasks, thoughts, or cases on a small notepad. Off‑loading the mental checklist signals the brain that the information is stored, reducing the need to keep rehearsing it.

4. **Transition to pre‑sleep wind‑down** – immediately after step 3
   - Move straight into the [[concepts/pre_sleep_wind_down]] routine (dim lights, warm‑water splash, six slow breaths, phone out of reach) to lock in the relaxed state.

---

## Why It Works
- **Breathing control** lowers heart rate and cortisol levels.
- **Muscle relaxation** interrupts the fight‑or‑flight feedback loop.
- **Externalizing thoughts** prevents rumination, a common barrier to sleep.
- **Consistency**: Repeating the same sequence trains the brain to associate these cues with a safe, calm environment.

---

## Practical Tips
- Keep a **notepad** and pen at the bedside or in a bag next to your [[entities/pen_light]] and [[entities/stethoscope]].
- Pair the breathing with a *soft visual cue* (e.g., a dim night‑light) to reinforce the transition.
- If you’re on a rotating [[entities/hospital_on_call_shift]], practice the steps wherever you end the call – in a break room, at the nurses' station, or in a quiet hallway.
