**User:** I’ve got a couple networking events coming up and I’m trying to make a better first impression—what are some practical, non-cringey ways to introduce myself and keep conversations going without feeling like I’m “pitching” the whole time?

**Assistant:** A good rule of thumb is to treat networking more like meeting people at a friend’s gathering than like a business transaction. When you introduce yourself, keep it simple: say your name, what you do in a plain-English way, and one short detail that’s easy for someone to react to. Something like, “I’m Alex, I work in product design—lately I’ve been focused on accessibility projects.” That’s enough to open the door without sounding like a pitch.

To keep conversations going, ask small, easy questions rather than big formal ones. Things like what brought them to the event, what they’re working on lately, or what they’re curious about in the industry. People usually relax when they get to talk about themselves, and you can let the conversation flow from their answers.

It also helps to react to what they say instead of trying to steer things back to your own goals. If they mention something interesting, follow that thread a little. You come across as engaged and genuine without forcing anything.

And when you want to wrap up, you can just say something like, “I’ve really enjoyed talking with you—I’m going to mingle a bit, but I’d love to stay in touch,” and offer your info. Simple and human beats polished and pitchy almost every time.

**User:** That helps—can you give me a few specific intro scripts for different situations (walking up to a group, one-on-one at the bar/coffee line, and when someone asks “so what do you do?”), plus a couple graceful exit lines that don’t feel abrupt?

**Assistant:** Here are a few short scripts you can use that still feel natural.

For walking up to a group, you can step in with something simple like, “Hey, mind if I join you? I’m Alex.” After you get a couple names back, you can add, “I’m in product design and still pretty new to this event, so I’m meeting people.” It’s friendly without barging in.

For a one‑on‑one moment in a bar or coffee line, something light works best: “Lines are good for something, I guess. I’m Alex.” Then follow with a casual, “Are you here with a team or just flying solo tonight?” It eases you both into a conversation without feeling forced.

When someone asks, “So what do you do?” keep it short and human: “I work in product design—mostly focused on accessibility right now. It’s been fun but a challenge.” That little hint of detail gives them something to latch onto without you giving a full pitch.

For exits, aim for warm and brief. You can say, “This has been great—I’m going to wander a bit, but I’ve really enjoyed chatting.” Or, “I don’t want to monopolize you, but I’d love to continue this another time. Mind if I grab your info?” Both feel natural and leave things on a good note.

**User:** Could you also give me a few follow-up text/email templates for after the event—like one for someone I had a great chat with, one for someone more senior I don’t want to bother, and one for reconnecting a week later if they didn’t reply?

**Assistant:** Here are a few simple, natural follow‑ups you can copy and tweak.

For someone you had a great chat with, you can write something like:  
“Hey [Name], it was great talking with you at the event last night. I really enjoyed our conversation about [topic]. If you’re up for it, I’d love to continue it over coffee sometime, but no pressure at all.”

For someone more senior where you want to be respectful of their time, you can say:  
“Hi [Name], it was nice meeting you at the event. I appreciated your insight on [topic] and just wanted to say thanks. If there’s ever a quick chance to ask you one or two follow‑up questions, I’d be grateful, but please don’t feel obligated.”

For reconnecting a week later if they didn’t reply, keep it light and low‑pressure:  
“Hi [Name], just wanted to circle back in case my last message got buried. No worries at all if you’re busy. Just wanted to say I enjoyed meeting you and would be glad to stay in touch whenever it’s convenient.”

Each one sounds human, keeps things brief, and doesn’t corner the other person.

**User:** Also, small update: I finally printed a new batch of business cards for networking events, and I put my main line on them so people can reach me directly—any tips on how/when to hand them out without it feeling awkward or salesy?

**Assistant:** The easiest way to make business cards feel natural is to treat them like a convenience, not a pitch.

A good moment is right as a conversation is wrapping up and it actually makes sense to stay in touch. You can say something like, “I’ll give you my card—it has my direct number,” in the same tone you’d use when offering someone a pen. It feels like a simple hand‑off, not a transaction.

You can also offer one when the other person mentions something you might follow up on. If they say, “Send me that article,” or, “We should chat about that project,” you can just respond with, “Sure—here, this might make it easier.”

What usually feels awkward is giving a card too early or to people you haven’t really talked with yet. If you focus on the conversation first and treat the card as a practical next step, it comes across as normal and appreciated rather than salesy.
