## Tactile Ritual

**Type:** Concept
**Description:** Using physical cues to interrupt autopilot and create mindful, low‑effort habits.

A *tactile ritual* is a brief, analog habit that relies on a tangible object or simple physical action to bring attention to the present moment and guide intentional behavior.  The core idea is to replace an invisible, automatic process with a visible, touchable trigger that can be sensed and acted upon in under two minutes.

### Core Elements
1. **Physical Cue** – An object such as a pen, stone, coin, key, or a small notebook placed where it will be seen each morning.
2. **Prompt or Question** – A brief, verbally‑or‑written prompt (e.g., “What do I want to pay attention to today?”) that the cue reminds the user to answer.
3. **Micro‑Duration** – The ritual is designed to take seconds, not minutes, so it feels like a natural pause rather than a new task.

### Benefits
- **Reduces mental load** by externalizing the decision‑making trigger [[concepts/mental_load_reduction]].
- **Supports low‑maintenance design**; it needs no digital setup or ongoing upkeep [[concepts/low_maintenance_design]].
- **Creates an intentional morning routine** that nudges focus without adding complexity [[concepts/intentional_morning_routine]].
- **Links to other practices** such as morning touchpoints [[concepts/morning_touchpoint]] and paper‑based prompt cards [[concepts/paper_prompt_card]].

### Example from Session 32
In the conversation dated 2024‑12‑13, the assistant suggested several tactile rituals:
- Keep a stone, coin, or key by the bedside and ask, “What do I want to pay attention to today?”
- Place a pen and notecard on the kitchen counter to write the day’s top priority.
- Use a small notebook as a “settings panel” to rewrite three prompts each morning.
These examples illustrate how a simple object can serve as a cue for a quick reflective question, turning an automatic wake‑up into a deliberate choosing moment.

### Implementation Tips
1. Choose an object you already handle each morning.
2. Keep the associated prompt short and actionable.
3. Store prompts on a single card or notebook page to minimize friction.
4. Review the past few days’ entries in a glance to detect patterns, then adjust the cue or question as needed.
