# Microlearning

**Microlearning** is a pedagogical approach that delivers content in short, discrete units—often just a few minutes long—so that learners can engage with material during otherwise idle periods.  It emphasizes *brevity, focus, and immediate applicability*, reducing the mental load associated with traditional, longer study sessions.

## Core principles
- **Tiny learning bites** – examples include five‑minute articles, a single flashcard, or a one‑sentence chapter recap.  These bite‑sized pieces keep the effort low‑stakes and avoid the resistance people feel toward “homework.”
- **Contextual integration** – pair micro‑learning moments with existing routines (e.g., commuting, cooking).  This leverages [[concepts/dead-time-learning]] by turning dead time into productive learning slots.
- **Curiosity‑driven selection** – learners capture anything that sparks interest and return to it later, aligning with [[concepts/curiosity-driven-learning]].
- **Immediate recall** – after each micro‑learning unit, a quick mental summary (no note‑taking) reinforces retention through retrieval practice.

## Application in practice
In the conversation documented in [[summaries/session-40_2025-06-17]], microlearning was applied to a long‑commute audiobook habit:
- The user was encouraged to treat each chapter as a micro‑learning unit, pausing only mentally to restate the main idea in a single sentence.
- Additional micro‑learning tactics included listening to short podcasts or articles during walking or cooking, and reviewing a single flashcard during brief downtimes.
- By keeping each engagement brief, the learner avoided the feeling of extra work while still building a consistent habit.

## Benefits
- **Higher consistency** – Small, low‑effort tasks are easier to repeat daily.
- **Improved retention** – The brief mental recap after each unit acts as a spaced‑repetition cue.
- **Flexibility** – Learners can fit micro‑learning into any fragment of the day without needing additional tools or written notes.

## When to use
- When time is fragmented (commutes, waiting periods, routine chores).
- When aiming to build a habit without overwhelming cognitive load.
- When the goal is to stay curious and explore topics organically rather than following a rigid syllabus.
