## Main contribution
A practical, no‑tool routine for converting long‑commute audiobook listening into a consistent, high‑retention learning habit.

## Key ideas & concepts
- **Dead‑time learning** – Use already‑automated activities (walking, commuting, cooking) as learning anchors so the effort feels lightweight. [[concepts/dead-time-learning]]
- **Microlearning** – Keep learning bites tiny (5‑minute articles, one flashcard, a single chapter recap) to avoid “homework” resistance. [[concepts/microlearning]]
- **Curiosity‑driven selection** – Save anything that sparks interest and revisit it later, rather than following a rigid syllabus. [[concepts/curiosity-driven-learning]]
- **Audiobook retention** – Treat the audio stream as repeated exposure:
  1. Slightly slow playback or linger on a book longer than usual.
  2. After each chapter, give a quick mental “anchor”: restate the main idea in one sentence without writing it down.
  3. Cycle favorite titles every few months so familiar ideas appear in new contexts.
- **Weekly “audiobook retention” routine** –  
  1. **During the commute**: listen normally; when a chapter ends, use the silence to mentally summarize the key point.  
  2. **Immediately after the drive**: perform a ten‑second memory jog (e.g., “Today’s big idea was X”).  
  3. **End‑of‑week loop‑back** (e.g., Friday morning): replay one or two standout chapters without any note‑taking.
- **Rules of thumb for high‑value chapter selection** –  
  * Perk‑up moments: any segment that makes you think “I could actually use that.”  
  * Clear frameworks or step‑by‑step ideas that tie concepts together.  
  * The feeling of “I wish I could write that down.”  
  Trust these mental cues to decide which chapters to replay during the weekly loop‑back.
- **Mental recall over written notes** – The act of retrieval (even silently) strengthens memory more than note‑taking in this context.
- **Repetition for retention** – Re‑listening to familiar sections in new contexts consolidates learning without extra apps.
