# Conversation session 38

**User:** I’m trying to plan out my herb bed and winter care a bit better. I finally got around to planting rosemary and a little lavender right along the garden—the soil never really locks up anymore all year round, so I’m hoping they’ll make it through without me hauling pots into the garage. Any tips on what I should do now (mulch, pruning, spacing, watering, etc.) to give them the best shot, and are there any warning signs I should watch for if we do get a random cold snap?

**Assistant:** If your soil stays workable all winter, you’ve already got a big advantage. Rosemary and lavender both hate sitting in cold, soggy ground more than they hate the cold itself, so think mostly in terms of drainage and gentle protection.

A light layer of mulch is helpful, but keep it pulled back an inch or two from the base of each plant so moisture doesn’t stay trapped against the stems. Prune only lightly right now if you need to shape anything; the heavier haircut is better saved for spring once new growth starts. As for spacing, just make sure they have enough room for air to move around them. Rosemary especially likes good airflow in winter to keep the stems from getting damp and mildewy.

Water normally through fall but ease up once temperatures regularly drop; they prefer to be on the dry side when it’s cold. The only time to intervene is before a sudden freeze after a dry spell—giving them a normal watering the day before a cold snap can keep the roots from desiccating.

If you do get a real cold shot, the main red flags are drooping, darkened, or mushy stems on lavender, and brittle, brown tips on rosemary that keep spreading downward. If you see that sort of damage, brush off any wet mulch, let things dry a bit, and trim off dead tips once the weather settles.

Otherwise, if the ground isn’t locking up and drainage is decent, both plants usually settle in fine after their first year.

**User:** Got it. When you say “light mulch,” what material would you pick for these—straw, shredded leaves, pine needles, gravel? I’ve heard gravel is better for lavender/rosemary but I’m not sure if that makes cold snaps worse. Also, if we get a freak week in the teens with wind, is a frost cloth worth it or does that trap too much moisture?

**Assistant:** For these two, mulch is mostly about keeping the soil from swinging too wet or too warm, not about heavy insulation. I’d go with something that dries quickly and doesn’t mat down. Pine needles or a thin layer of shredded leaves works well. Gravel is fine too—lavender especially likes it—but I wouldn’t rely on gravel alone for winter because it doesn’t give much temperature buffering. It won’t make a cold snap worse; it just doesn’t help much if the temperature really plunges.

If you expect a week in the teens with wind, a frost cloth can absolutely help. The key is to keep it loose and breathable so it isn’t trapping damp air against the foliage. As long as it’s not airtight plastic and you remove it once the cold passes, it won’t create a moisture problem. The cloth’s main job is to cut the wind, which is often what really hurts rosemary and lavender in borderline climates.

**User:** If I go with gravel, do I put it right up to the stems or still leave that little gap like with organic mulch? And would you do gravel over the whole bed or just a ring around each plant?

**Assistant:** Still leave a small gap. Even with gravel, it’s better not to pile anything right against the woody base so moisture can evaporate and the stems don’t stay constantly damp.

You don’t need to cover the whole bed unless you like the look. A ring around each plant is enough to keep the immediate root zone drier and improve airflow. If the rest of the bed drains well, there’s no real benefit to blanketing everything.

**User:** Makes sense. How wide would you make that gravel ring for a first-year rosemary and a small lavender—like 6 inches out from the stem or more? And is pea gravel okay, or should it be something sharper/larger so it doesn’t pack down?

**Assistant:** For young plants, about six inches around the stem is plenty for now. As they get bigger you can widen it, but they don’t need a big gravel apron their first season.

Pea gravel is fine. It doesn’t usually pack down in a way that causes problems because water still moves through it easily. If you already have a sharper or slightly larger gravel, that works too, but there’s no need to hunt it down just for this.

**User:** If I end up using frost cloth during a cold snap, do I drape it directly over the rosemary/lavender or should I rig it so it’s not touching the foliage? And should I uncover them during the day if it warms up, or just leave it on for the whole cold spell?

**Assistant:** You don’t have to keep the cloth off the foliage the way you would with plastic. Frost cloth is breathable, so draping it right over the plants is usually fine. If you can give it a little bit of lift with a stake or two, that’s great, but it’s not required for rosemary or lavender.

If daytime temperatures climb back above freezing and the sun is out, it’s better to pull the cloth off so the plants can dry and get some light. If the whole week stays cold and cloudy, you can leave it on without fussing over it. The main thing is to avoid trapping them under a warm, wet cover once the weather turns mild again.

**User:** One more thing—if the rosemary does get some tip burn after a cold snap, do I trim it right away or wait until spring to see what actually recovers? And same question for the lavender if any stems look browned.

**Assistant:** Wait until spring. Both plants can look rough after a cold spell, and it’s hard to tell what’s truly dead until new growth starts. If you trim right away, you risk cutting back wood that would have recovered once the weather warms.

The only time to snip sooner is if you see mushy, collapsed tissue—go ahead and remove that so it doesn’t rot further. Otherwise, leave the browned tips alone through winter and do your cleanup once you see fresh buds breaking in spring.
