# Attention Friction

**Definition**: Intentional, low‑effort obstacles placed in a user's environment or device settings that disrupt automatic phone‑checking behavior, helping the user stay within a brief, purposeful usage window.

## Core Elements
1. **Physical Placement** – Keeping the phone out of arm's reach (e.g., on a dresser across the room or a hallway shelf) adds a tiny amount of effort that discourages mindless scrolling.
2. **Digital Barriers** – Configuring a dedicated iPhone [[concepts/focus-mode]] (named *Morning Quick Check*) that limits allowed contacts and apps, hides notification previews, disables lock‑screen widgets, and optionally dims the lock screen.
3. **Behavioral Rules** – Simple self‑imposed cues such as “if I reach for the phone, I must stand up to get it” or using a lock‑screen reminder like *"Morning first, phone later"*.
4. **Temporal Boundaries** – Treating the phone check like brushing teeth: a defined 1‑2 minute window for essential messages, followed by an immediate downstream activity (shower, coffee, etc.).

## How It Was Applied in Session 39
- The conversation recommended a **quick‑check routine** limited to texts, missed calls, and calendar reminders, explicitly avoiding social feeds.
- An iPhone [[entities/iphone]] **Focus** was described with step‑by‑step menu navigation to allow only selected contacts (via [[entities/messages]]) and the Calendar app, while silencing everything else.
- Physical friction suggestions included placing the phone on a kitchen counter or dresser a few steps from the bed, providing enough distance to break the impulse.
- Additional cues such as a lock‑screen reminder and the *Dim Lock Screen* option were suggested to reduce visual stimulation.
