# DIY and Low‑Cost Methods for Sensing Humidity, Temperature, and Salt Aerosols

This concept outlines practical, inexpensive techniques for estimating atmospheric humidity, temperature, and the presence of sea‑salt aerosols during everyday walks, especially in coastal settings. It draws on observations and suggestions from [[summaries/session-39_2024-07-15]].

## Core Ideas

- **Thermometer‑hygrometer**: A pocket‑size device that simultaneously displays temperature and relative humidity. It provides a quantitative baseline that can be correlated with personal sensory cues (e.g., skin stickiness, hair frizz). This aligns with the broader theme of [[concepts/low-tech-environment-monitoring]].
- **Sensory Cues for Humidity Perception**: People can infer humidity levels by noting how quickly sweat evaporates, whether skin feels damp, or how hair behaves. These subjective signals are central to [[concepts/humidity-perception]].
- **DIY Salt Aerosol Detection**: Simple, non‑instrumental methods include:
  1. Wiping a clean glass surface or phone screen before a walk and inspecting it afterward for a faint tacky film or tiny specks, indicating sea‑salt deposition.
  2. Observing metal objects (e.g., a keychain) for early dullness or rust, which can hint at ambient salt levels.
  These observations relate to the presence of [[concepts/coastal-aerosols]] and the broader impact of [[entities/sea-salt]] in coastal air.

## Practical Workflow
1. **Prepare**: Carry a small [[entities/thermometer-hygrometer]] and a clean microfiber cloth.
2. **Before the Walk**: Wipe a glass pane or phone screen with the cloth; note its pristine state.
3. **During the Walk**: Pay attention to:
   - Skin feeling (sticky vs. dry)
   - Hair frizz or waviness
   - Breath temperature (cool bite vs. warm heaviness)
   - Strength and distance of ocean scents (if near the [[entities/ocean]] or [[entities/coastline]])
4. **After the Walk**: Check the cloth‑wiped surface for residue; inspect metal items for any dulling.
5. **Record**: Log the temperature, humidity reading, and any observed salt residue. Over multiple days, patterns emerge that link quantitative readings with subjective experience.

## Benefits
- **Cost‑Effective**: Requires only a cheap hygrometer and everyday items (cloth, glass).
- **Immediate Feedback**: Sensory cues provide real‑time perception, while the hygrometer offers objective data.
- **Contextual Insight**: Differentiates between coastal humidity (rich in [[entities/sea-salt]]) and inland humidity, supporting a nuanced understanding of [[concepts/coastal-aerosols]].

## Limitations
- DIY salt detection is qualitative; it cannot replace laboratory‑grade aerosol measurement.
- Perception of humidity varies between individuals, so personal calibration is needed.

By combining low‑tech instruments with simple observational tricks, anyone can develop a reliable sense of atmospheric conditions without relying on weather apps or expensive equipment.
