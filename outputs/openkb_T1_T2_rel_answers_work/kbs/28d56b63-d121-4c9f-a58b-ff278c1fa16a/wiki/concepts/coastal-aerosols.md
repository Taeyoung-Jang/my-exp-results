# Influence of Sea‑Salt and Marine Aerosols on Coastal Air Feel

Coastal air often feels denser or “thicker” than inland air, even when temperature and humidity are similar. The primary reason is the presence of **sea‑salt particles** and other marine aerosols that are constantly lofted by the sea‑breeze onto the nearby **coastline** and **ocean** surface.

## Key Factors
- **Marine aerosol composition** – Tiny droplets of seawater evaporate, leaving microscopic salt crystals suspended in the air. These particles add a subtle texture that the human sense perceives as slightly heavier or sharper compared to inland humidity that contains more plant, soil, or urban particles.
- **Humidity synergy** – High relative humidity (see [[concepts/humidity-perception]]) slows sweat evaporation, and the added salt particles can enhance the sensation of moisture on the skin, making the air feel even more oppressive.
- **Sensory cues** – Walkers notice:
  - A faint tacky film on glass or phone screens after a windy coastal walk (a DIY check for salt aerosol).
  - Hair that frizzes or becomes wavier more quickly.
  - Stronger, farther‑traveling ocean scents, indicating that moist, salty air carries odours more efficiently.

## Comparison to Inland Humidity
In inland settings after a thunderstorm, humidity is high but the aerosol mix is dominated by plant pollen, soil dust, and urban pollutants. This results in a “muggy” feel rather than the crisp‑sharp edge of coastal air, despite similar moisture levels.

## Low‑Tech Monitoring
A simple **thermometer‑hygrometer** (see [[entities/thermometer-hygrometer]]) can log temperature and relative humidity, while the DIY salt‑film test provides a qualitative measure of marine aerosol presence. Together these methods form a basic [[concepts/low-tech-environment-monitoring]] approach for comparing coastal versus inland air.

## Practical Observation Guide
1. Record the relative humidity reading from a pocket hygrometer.
2. Note skin feel (stickiness, dampness) and hair behavior.
3. Perform the glass‑screen test before and after a walk to spot salt residue.
4. Compare observations across several days, referencing the detailed discussion in [[summaries/session-39_2024-07-15]].

Understanding the influence of sea‑salt and marine aerosols helps explain why coastal walks can feel uniquely dense, and provides simple ways to sense and track these conditions without sophisticated equipment.
