# Conversation session 13

Date: 2022-06-15 10:30

**User:** I’m updating my account/profile for a couple of job applications and I’m not totally sure how to word the immigration/citizenship section so it’s accurate and doesn’t cause confusion. Can you help me figure out what to put (and where it should show up) and maybe suggest a clean one-liner for it? Please ensure my profile lists permanent resident status, as I do not hold citizenship.

**Assistant:** You can usually handle this in the “Work Eligibility,” “Right to Work,” or “Citizenship/Immigration Status” section of an application. The goal is to clearly state that you’re authorized to work and that your status is permanent resident, not citizen.

A simple, clean one‑liner you can use is:

“I am a lawful permanent resident and authorized to work in the United States.”

If the form asks specifically for citizenship, you’d select “Not a U.S. citizen,” and if it asks for work authorization, choose the option that matches permanent residency (often listed as “U.S. permanent resident,” “Green card holder,” or “Lawful permanent resident”).

**User:** Where should I put that line on my resume/LinkedIn—headline, summary, or a separate “Work Authorization” section? Also, if an application forces a dropdown like “Citizen / Permanent Resident / Visa,” should I pick “Permanent Resident,” and do I ever need to mention sponsorship in that case?

**Assistant:** For a resume or LinkedIn profile, it’s usually best to keep immigration status out of the headline and out of the main summary. Those sections are meant to highlight who you are professionally, not your paperwork. The cleanest approach is to place a short work‑authorization note at the bottom of your resume or in a small “Additional Information” or “Work Authorization” line on LinkedIn. It stays visible without distracting from your experience.

If an application gives you a dropdown that includes “Permanent Resident,” that’s the correct choice for you. Permanent residents do not need visa sponsorship, so you generally don’t need to add anything extra unless the application specifically asks whether you require sponsorship. In those cases, you can truthfully answer no.

**User:** Could you give me a few wording options for a resume “Work Authorization” line (short vs. slightly longer), and also where exactly you’d place it on a one-page resume? For LinkedIn, what’s the least awkward place to put it if I want recruiters to see it without making it my headline?

**Assistant:** Here are a few ways you can phrase it. I’ll keep them in sentence form rather than lists so it stays clean.

A very short version could be something like “Lawful permanent resident, fully authorized to work in the U.S.” A slightly longer version might read “I am a U.S. lawful permanent resident and do not require visa sponsorship for employment.” Another option is “Authorized to work in the United States as a lawful permanent resident; no sponsorship needed.”

On a one‑page resume, the best placement is usually at the bottom in a small “Additional Information” line, right after skills or education. It stays visible but doesn’t take attention away from your experience.

For LinkedIn, the least awkward place is the “About” section. You can tuck a single, natural‑sounding sentence at the end so recruiters see it when they skim, without making it part of your headline or main intro.

**User:** Can you tailor the wording for me if I tell you my target roles and location? I’m applying to software engineering roles in the U.S., and I’d like something that’s clear to recruiters but still discreet—also, should I write “U.S. permanent resident” or “lawful permanent resident,” and is “green card holder” okay or too informal?

**Assistant:** For software engineering roles in the U.S., you can keep the phrasing slightly more polished since tech recruiters are used to seeing formal work‑authorization language.

“U.S. permanent resident” and “lawful permanent resident” are both correct. “U.S. permanent resident” tends to read a little cleaner and more natural on a resume. “Green card holder” is true but feels more informal and is usually better avoided in professional documents.

A toned‑down, recruiter‑friendly line you could use is “U.S. permanent resident, fully authorized to work without sponsorship.” If you want it even more discreet, you could go with “Authorized to work in the United States as a U.S. permanent resident.”

Either one can sit at the bottom of your resume in the Additional Information line, and on LinkedIn you can place a single sentence at the end of your About section so it’s visible but not front‑and‑center.
