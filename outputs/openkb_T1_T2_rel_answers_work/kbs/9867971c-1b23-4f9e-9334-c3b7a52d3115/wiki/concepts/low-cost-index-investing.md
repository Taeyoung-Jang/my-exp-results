# Low‑Cost Index Investing

Low‑cost index investing is the practice of allocating a portfolio’s core to broad market index funds that track large segments of the market at minimal expense ratios. The approach relies on capturing the overall market return, rather than trying to pick individual winning stocks or time the market.

## Why Low‑Cost Index Funds?
- **Cost Efficiency**: Management fees and transaction costs are dramatically lower than actively managed funds, which improves net returns over the long run.
- **Diversification**: A single fund can provide exposure to hundreds or thousands of securities, reducing idiosyncratic risk.
- **Simplicity**: Investors can build a balanced portfolio with a few funds, avoiding the complexity of selecting many individual holdings.
- **Consistency**: By staying fully invested in the market, the strategy benefits from compounding and avoids the performance drag of frequent trading.

## Typical Vehicles
- **[[entities/broad-market-index-fund]]** – funds that track the entire market (e.g., total‑stock‑market ETFs).
- **[[entities/index-fund]]** – any fund that tracks a specific index, often used for both stock and bond exposures.

- The **bond allocation role** in a portfolio determines how much of the risk budget is taken up by fixed‑income assets; low‑cost index investing forms the equity side that pairs with that decision. See [[concepts/bond-allocation-role]].
- When introducing a bond ballast, the choice of an **intermediate‑term Treasury strategy** can complement a low‑cost equity core. See [[concepts/intermediate-term-treasury-strategy]].

## Practical Steps
1. **Select a broad market index fund** with an expense ratio well below 0.10 % (e.g., a total‑stock‑market ETF).
2. **Allocate a target percentage** of the portfolio to this fund based on your risk tolerance.
3. **Set up automatic contributions** to the fund to benefit from dollar‑cost averaging.
4. **Periodically rebalance** to maintain the intended risk profile, but avoid frequent trading.
5. **Combine with a suitable bond component** (e.g., [[entities/us-treasury-securities]] or a [[entities/total-bond-fund]]) to achieve the desired overall risk/return balance.

By adhering to low‑cost index investing, investors can build a durable, “boring” portfolio that typically outperforms higher‑fee strategies over the long term.
