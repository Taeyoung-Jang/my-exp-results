# Conversation session 1

Date: 2023-12-26 19:00

**User:** I’ve been thinking about gift-giving etiquette lately—especially around birthdays and weddings. How do you decide what’s “appropriate” to spend, and do you think gifts should be purely heartfelt or is there always some social expectation attached?

**Assistant:** It usually ends up being a mix of what feels right to you and what the situation expects. Most people quietly use a few cues: how close they are to the person, what others in the same circle tend to spend, and what the event means culturally. There isn’t a universal number that’s “appropriate,” just ranges people feel comfortable within.

As for whether gifts should be purely heartfelt, in an ideal world yes—but in real life there’s almost always some layer of social expectation. You don’t want to look careless or out of step, and people naturally think about that. Still, the gifts that feel best to give and receive are usually the ones with some personal thought behind them, even if you also kept the occasion’s norms in mind.

**User:** That makes sense. For weddings specifically, do you think it’s reasonable to use “cover your plate” as a baseline, or is that kind of transactional thinking missing the point? Also, if you’re not super close to the couple but you’re invited, how do you set a budget without over- or under-shooting what’s expected?

**Assistant:** “Cover your plate” is one of those ideas that people toss around, but it isn’t really a universal rule. It can make the whole thing feel like you’re paying an admission fee, which isn’t the spirit of a gift. Most couples aren’t tracking whether your gift equals the cost of your meal—they’re just glad you showed up.

A more reasonable approach is to think about your relationship to the couple and what feels comfortable for you financially. If you’re not especially close, it’s perfectly normal to give something modest but thoughtful, or a cash gift that fits within your own budget. People understand that not every guest is a best friend or close family member.

As long as the gift comes from a place of goodwill and isn’t clearly disproportionate to the level of the relationship, you’re not likely to misstep. The couple invited you because they wanted you there, not because they’re expecting a specific dollar amount.

**User:** I get that, but I still worry about accidentally standing out in either direction. If you’re trying to be “normal” for a typical mid-range wedding (not super fancy, not super casual), what kind of range would you consider reasonable for a single guest vs a couple? And would you adjust if you had to travel for it?

**Assistant:** For a fairly typical wedding, most people land in the same general ballpark without thinking too hard about it. For a single guest, something in the low-to-mid hundreds is very normal, and for a couple attending together, a bit more than that—basically a gentle step up rather than doubling it. It’s more about staying in the same social rhythm as everyone else than hitting an exact figure.

Travel definitely changes the calculus. If you’ve already spent real money getting there—flights, hotels, time off—most couples don’t expect you to give at the same level as someone who lives nearby. It’s perfectly acceptable to scale the gift back to whatever still feels generous within the context of everything you’ve already invested to be present.

**User:** I’m realizing part of why I overthink it is I actually keep track of what people spend on my gifts so I can match it later; taking more than I give feels like accepting a loan I didn't ask for. Is that a weird mindset to bring to weddings too—like should I try to let go of “matching” and just pick a budget based on closeness + what I can afford?

**Assistant:** It’s not weird in the sense that a lot of people quietly do some version of that mental math—but it does end up putting pressure on you that the other person probably never intended. Gift‑giving isn’t usually meant to be a long-term balance sheet, especially around weddings. People give what they can when the moment comes, and the “value” of it tends to wash out over the course of a relationship anyway.

Letting go of matching can actually make things easier and feel more genuine. If you base your gift on how close you are to the couple and what fits comfortably within your budget, you’ll almost always land in the socially normal zone without feeling like you owe anyone anything. And most couples would rather know you showed up with real goodwill than worry about whether the gift aligns perfectly with some past exchange.
