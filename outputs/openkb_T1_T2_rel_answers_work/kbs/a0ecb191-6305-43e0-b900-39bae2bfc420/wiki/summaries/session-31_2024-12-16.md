## Summary

The conversation focuses on how to adjust to a new, quieter neighborhood soundscape—especially the presence of birdsong—and how to create a calmer home environment without completely isolating oneself.

### Key Recommendations
- **Treat new sounds neutrally**: View unfamiliar noises as cues to habituate to rather than obstacles.
- **Introduce steady background sound**: Use soft music, a fan, or low‑level noise (white, pink, or brown) as an acoustic anchor that blends into the room. This relates to [[concepts/background-sound-masking]].
- **Physical acoustic tweaks**: Add thick curtains, rugs, and arrange furniture against shared walls to soften echoes.
- **Daytime strategies**: Embrace the birdsong by opening windows, pausing to notice it, and letting it become part of your rhythm.
- **Nighttime strategies**: Add a gentle, featureless sound to avoid stark silence. Choose a noise type that is low‑frequency and mellow (brown noise is often preferred, pink is a middle ground, white is brighter). These choices support good [[concepts/sleep-hygiene]].
- **Volume guidance**: Aim for a “barely there” level—roughly the sound of a very soft bathroom fan—so details are still audible but the sound fades into the background.
- **Placement tips**: Position fans or speakers across the room, angled toward a wall, and keep them away from the head of the bed. This creates a diffuse sound field.
- **Partner considerations**: Use distance and direction to minimize impact on a noise‑sensitive partner; favor lower‑frequency sounds and consider a timed fade‑in to ease the transition.

### Practical Setup
1. Place a small desk fan or speaker on a shelf a few feet from the bed, angled away.
2. Set volume to the point where you can’t easily pick out individual details.
3. Start with brown noise or a steady fan hum; experiment with pink or white if needed.
4. If a partner is sensitive, keep the source on your side of the room and use a gradual fade‑in at night.
