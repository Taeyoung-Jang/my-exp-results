# Cutback Week

**Definition**: A *cutback week* is a scheduled period—typically every fourth week—in which overall training volume is deliberately reduced while keeping the weekly structure intact. The goal is to provide physiological and mental recovery, solidify adaptations, and prevent overtraining.

---

## Why use a cutback week?
- Allows muscles, joints, and connective tissue to repair without a complete break from running.
- Helps maintain the habit of regular training while lowering stress on the body.
- Reduces the risk of burnout and injury, especially during long‑term marathon preparation.

## Typical structure
- **Same anchor days**: keep the pattern of Tuesday, Thursday, Saturday (long run), and Sunday runs unchanged.
- **Reduced mileage**: lower the total weekly mileage by roughly 20‑30 % compared to the preceding build week.
- **Shorter long run**: the Saturday long run drops to a more manageable distance (e.g., from 12 mi to 8 mi).
- **Ease the weekday runs**: keep Tuesday and Thursday runs at the low end of the 45‑60 minute window (≈4‑4.5 mi).
- **Maintain minimal quality**: optional light strides or a brief tempo can still be included, but keep the intensity low to preserve recovery.

## Example from [[summaries/session-38_2025-03-11]]
| Week | Tue | Thu | Sat (Long) | Sun |
|------|-----|-----|------------|-----|
| Build 1 (~25 mi) | 5 mi | 5 mi | 10 mi | 2‑3 mi |
| Build 2 (~27 mi) | 5 mi | 5 mi | 11 mi | 2‑3 mi |
| Build 3 (~29 mi) | 5 mi | 5 mi | 12 mi | 2‑3 mi |
| **Cutback (~23 mi)** | 4‑4.5 mi | 4‑4.5 mi | 8 mi | 2 mi |

The cutback week mirrors the regular schedule but **compresses** each run, preserving the rhythm of the training cycle while giving the body a chance to adapt.

## How it connects to other concepts
- Relies on consistent **[[concepts/anchor-days]]** to keep the habit alive without overloading.
- May still include light **[[concepts/quality-sessions]]** (e.g., strides) on easy days, but the focus is recovery.

---

## Red‑flag signs to trigger a cutback (or additional reduction)
- Persistent leg heaviness that doesn’t fade after warm‑up
- Declining sleep quality
- Soreness that alters running form
- Mental dread about the upcoming run
- Elevated heart‑rate or perceived effort at easy pace

When any of these appear, consider shortening the long run or swapping an easy run for rest.
