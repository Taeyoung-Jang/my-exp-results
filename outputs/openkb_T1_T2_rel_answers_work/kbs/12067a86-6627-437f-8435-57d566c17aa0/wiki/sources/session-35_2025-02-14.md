**Assistant:** Given how intense your travel pace is, the key is to build a routine that’s flexible enough to survive airport chaos but structured enough that you don’t have to think about it.

For sleep, anchor yourself to a simple rule: protect a minimum window rather than a fixed bedtime. If you know you have a 5 a.m. flight, go to bed as early as you can and top up with a 20–30 minute nap once you land. On normal nights, aim for the same general range of hours, even if the clock changes. Consistency in total sleep matters more than perfect timing when you’re bouncing between cities.

For workouts, make it frictionless. Choose one short, no‑equipment routine you can do in a hotel room and treat it like brushing your teeth—just something you do every morning for 10 to 15 minutes before the day gets away from you. Then rely on whatever “bonus” movement the day gives you, like walking the airport or doing a gym session only if your schedule magically opens up.

For eating, think in terms of damage control rather than perfection. Start the day with something stable—Greek yogurt, oatmeal, eggs, anything you can usually find in a hotel—and use that to set the tone. During client dinners, pick one of the big levers to keep reasonable: either portion size or alcohol. It doesn’t need to be both. When you’re on your own, grab the simplest, least‑chaotic option you can find, like a protein-forward bowl or salad, just to keep your baseline steady.

If you stick to a simple sleep window, a tiny daily workout, and a couple of predictable food habits, the rest can be messy without throwing you completely off track.

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple way to make it concrete without turning your days into a military schedule. Think of these as loose scripts you can fall back on, not rigid rules.

A travel day template might look like this: Wake up roughly ninety minutes before you need to head to the airport so you’re not starting the day in panic mode. Drink some water, do a quick ten‑minute hotel‑room workout, shower, and go. At the airport, grab something protein‑heavy for breakfast, like a yogurt with fruit or an egg sandwich that isn’t drenched in sauce. On the plane, avoid working until you’ve slept or at least closed your eyes for twenty minutes. When you land, walk a bit to shake off the flight, keep lunch light and simple, then treat the rest of the day as whatever your schedule demands. If there’s a client dinner, keep either alcohol or portion size modest. Before bed, aim for at least seven hours from whatever time you can realistically get horizontal.

A normal hotel day template is calmer. Wake up around the same time each morning, even if your evenings shift. Drink water, do a twelve‑to‑fifteen‑minute workout in the room, then have a predictable breakfast like oatmeal, eggs, or a protein‑y coffee shop order. Block off a short “reset window” in the afternoon if you can—just ten minutes to breathe, hydrate, maybe stretch. Dinner can be more controlled on these days since you’re not rushing between cities, so stick to something balanced and avoid eating too late if possible. Wind down and give yourself a consistent sleep window.

A simple repeatable no‑equipment workout you can do in a hotel room looks like this: Start with one minute of easy movement like marching in place. Then do three rounds of forty seconds of bodyweight squats, forty seconds of push‑ups (or hands‑on‑desk push‑ups if you’re tired), and forty seconds of a plank, with about twenty seconds to transition between them. Finish with a minute of slow stretching. It’s quick, requires no thought, and covers the basics.

For airport or coffee shop food, favor the default options that are hard to mess up. At Starbucks or similar, a protein box, oatmeal with a side of egg bites, or a simple breakfast sandwich works well. At typical airport fast‑casual spots, a salad or grain bowl with chicken or tofu is usually the safest bet, or a turkey or grilled chicken sandwich without heavy sauces. If all else fails, grab Greek yogurt, a banana, and a bag of nuts—it’s not glamorous, but it keeps you steady until you reach real food.

If you run these two templates on autopilot, the chaos around them matters a lot less.
