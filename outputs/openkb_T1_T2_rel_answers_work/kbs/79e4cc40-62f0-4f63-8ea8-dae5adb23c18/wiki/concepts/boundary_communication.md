## Boundary Communication

**Definition**: The practice of expressing personal preferences and limits regarding social plans in a clear, courteous, and non‑judgmental way.

### Core Elements (as illustrated in [[summaries/session-04_2024-06-15]])
1. **Framing as personal preference** – Phrase declines as a statement about your own comfort rather than a critique of the event (e.g., “Big parties aren’t my thing”).
2. **Offering an alternative** – Pair a ‘no’ with a suggestion for a smaller or one‑on‑one hangout to show continued interest in the relationship.
3. **Consistency and firmness** – When an invitation is repeatedly pushed, restate the boundary calmly and reiterate the preference.
4. **Tone** – Keep the language appreciative and brief; the goal is to convey respect for both your own needs and the inviter’s feelings.

### Example Templates (from the source)
- **Short**: “Thanks for thinking of me! Big parties aren’t really my scene, but I hope you have a great time.”
- **Warm**: “I really appreciate the invite. I’ve learned I’m much more comfortable in smaller hangouts, so I’ll pass on the party, but I’d love to catch up another day.”
- **Firm (pushy friend)**: “I totally get why you want me there, but big events don’t work for me. I’m going to sit this one out, but I’m always up for seeing you in a smaller setting.”

### Relationships to Other Concepts
- Linked to [[concepts/social_setting_preferences]] because understanding one’s preferred social atmosphere informs how boundaries are set.
- Connected with [[concepts/energy_management_in_social_events]] since boundary communication helps protect personal energy and prevent burnout.

### Benefits
- Reduces social anxiety and small‑talk fatigue.
- Encourages friends to respect individual comfort zones.
- Leads to more authentic, enjoyable interactions.
