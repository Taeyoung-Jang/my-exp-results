# Summary of session‑14_2023‑06‑01

**Topic:** Career decision‑making for conservation/restoration professionals who want to stay hands‑on while advancing professionally and financially.

### Key Points
1. **Self‑assessment** – Identify whether you are energized by technical problem‑solving and direct work with objects or by strategic, mentorship, and influence activities. Use real‑project reactions to gauge preference. *(Relevant to [[concepts/technical_vs_managerial_career_paths]])*

2. **Typical trade‑offs**  
   * *Hands‑on roles*: deeper material connection, technical immersion, less admin, slower promotion, limited decision‑making power.  
   * *Leadership roles*: broader impact, strategic influence, higher pay, but reduced bench time and increased meetings, budgets, and personnel duties. *(Relevant to [[concepts/technical_vs_managerial_career_paths]])*

3. **Technical‑track growth strategies**  
   * Aim for senior specialist or principal conservator titles where authority stems from expertise, not supervision. *(Relevant to [[concepts/senior_technical_track]])*  
   * Become the go‑to expert in a material, technique, or analytical method; lead complex treatments; publish case studies; collaborate on research.  
   * Increase visibility through workshops, mentorship, and consulting side‑projects.  
   * Leverage specialization, grant‑funded research, and high‑impact projects for higher compensation.

4. **Workplace structures that protect bench time**  
   * Institutions with separate technical and managerial ladders (large museums, well‑funded labs, federal/state agencies, private studios).  
   * Clear progression criteria based on expertise, research contributions, and specialized skill sets.  
   * Access to specialized equipment, analytical support, and scientist collaborations. *(Relevant to [[concepts/bench_time_protection]])*

5. **Interview / job‑posting checklist**  
   • Existence of a senior or principal conservator track without supervisory duties.  
   • Performance evaluation tied to technical output rather than management.  
   • Language indicating protected bench time, research days, or limited admin load.  
   • Percentage of time senior staff spend on treatment work.  
   • Current number of direct reports and expected growth.  
   • Availability of specialized equipment and analytical collaborations.  
   • History of promoting based on mastery, not leadership.  
   • Support for advanced training, workshops, or conference attendance.  
   • Clear delineation of budgeting, procurement, and scheduling responsibilities.

6. **Compensation negotiation tactics for specialist conservators**  
   • Position yourself as a scarce specialist; showcase past high‑risk or high‑profile projects.  
   • Link salary requests to measurable outcomes (e.g., complex project capacity, rare‑material expertise).  
   • Negotiate for non‑salary benefits: dedicated research time, professional‑development funds, occasional consulting permissions.
