**User:** I’ve been getting more into buying snacks and pantry staples from international grocery stores lately, and I’m realizing packaging can be surprisingly confusing. When you’re choosing between brands you don’t know well, what do you look for on the label to figure out what’s actually in it (ingredients, allergens, additives), and how do you judge whether a product seems trustworthy?

**Assistant:** When you’re staring at unfamiliar brands, the most useful thing you can do is slow down and read the label the same way you would for a product at a regular grocery store, but with a little extra attention.

I usually start with the ingredient list. Shorter is generally easier to interpret, and ingredients you can recognize without a chemistry background tend to make things clearer. If the list is unusually long or filled with additives you don’t normally see for that type of food, that’s a sign to pause and figure out whether it matters for you.

Allergen info is often printed in bold or set apart in its own line, even on international packaging. Sometimes it’s phrased differently, like “contains” or “may contain traces of,” but most countries require brands to highlight common allergens somehow. If you have specific sensitivities, it’s worth double‑checking for alternative names of ingredients you avoid.

For trustworthiness, I look for things like a clearly printed manufacturer name, an address or website, and an expiration or best‑by date that looks professionally applied. Consistent design, good‑quality printing, and intact seals also say a lot. If the label includes a certification mark you don’t recognize, a quick search can tell you whether it’s a legitimate food‑safety or quality standard.

Over time you’ll start to notice which brands are reliable, but until then, clear labeling, normal‑looking ingredients, and transparent manufacturer info usually point you in the right direction.

**User:** I tend to grab the versions with Korean text so I can read the ingredients myself instead of relying on those English sticker translations—are there particular Korean terms I should watch for that signal common allergens (like wheat/gluten, soy, milk) or additives (MSG, artificial sweeteners, preservatives)?

**Assistant:** If you’re comfortable reading Korean, there are a handful of words that make it much easier to spot allergens and additives quickly.

For wheat or gluten, you’ll usually see 밀 for wheat and sometimes 글루텐 written phonetically. Barley and rye show up as 보리 and 호밀, and they often travel with gluten even if it isn’t spelled out.

Soy is almost always 콩 or 대두. In processed foods it might appear as 대두단백 for soy protein or 대두레시틴 for soy lecithin.

Milk ingredients show up as 우유, but you might also see related terms like 유청 for whey and 탈지분유 for skim milk powder. Butter, cheese, and cream are usually borrowed words like 버터, 치즈, 크림.

MSG is most often listed as L‑글루타민산나트륨 or simply MSG. Some packages shorten it to 글루탐산.

For artificial sweeteners, look for names like 아스파탐 for aspartame, 아세설팜칼륨 for acesulfame K, and 수크랄로스 for sucralose. They’re usually easy to spot because they don’t look like ordinary food words.

Common preservatives include 소르빈산칼륨 for potassium sorbate and 아질산나트륨 for sodium nitrite. You may also see 산도조절제, which is a general term for acidity regulators rather than a specific chemical.

Once you get familiar with these terms, scanning a label in Korean becomes much faster, and you can usually trust the Korean ingredient list more than the tiny English sticker since it’s the original regulatory version.

**User:** Could you give me a quick cheat sheet of the most common Korean allergen statements/sections to look for (like “알레르기 유발물질” or “함유”), and any tricky ones where the allergen is hidden inside another ingredient name? Also, are “~가공품” labels something I should treat as a red flag for cross-contamination?

**Assistant:** You’ll usually find allergen info grouped in one predictable spot on Korean packaging, and a few key phrases make it easy to spot even at a glance.

The heading you already mentioned, 알레르기 유발물질, is the most common one. Sometimes it’s shortened to 알레르기 또는 알레르기 정보. Right after that, brands often use the word 함유 to mean “contains,” or may contain will show up as 있을 수 있음 or 미량 포함. If a product is manufactured in a facility that handles allergens, you might see 같은 제조시설에서 사용 or 제조라인 공유, which is the Korean version of “made on shared equipment.”

A few allergens do hide inside longer ingredient names. Milk shows up not only as 우유 but also as 유청, which is whey, and 분유 or 탈지분유, which are types of milk powder. Wheat can hide in terms like 맥아 for malt or 보리/호밀 for barley/rye, even if the word gluten isn’t written. Soy hides in 대두단백 for soy protein and 대두레시틴 for soy lecithin. Fish and shellfish are often wrapped into things like 멸치액젓 or 새우젓, which are anchovy and shrimp sauces rather than plain seafood.

As for the “~가공품” label, it isn’t a red flag by itself. It just means a processed ingredient, like 감자전분가공품 for a potato‑starch blend. Some of these blends contain multiple sub‑ingredients, so they’re worth checking closely, but the term doesn’t automatically imply cross‑contamination. If there’s a risk of shared equipment, you’ll usually see one of the warning phrases mentioned earlier.

Once you’ve seen these a few times, they start to jump out, and it gets much easier to skim for allergen clues even on unfamiliar brands.

**User:** I usually pick the packages with Korean text since I can verify the ingredients directly without needing to trust the translated stickers. Could you help me build a short “scan order” for Korean labels (like: first look for 알레르기 유발물질, then 원재료명, then 제조시설 경고, etc.), and include the exact Korean phrases that typically indicate “may contain” vs “made on shared equipment/lines”?

**Assistant:** You can think of it as a quick mental path you follow every time, but keep it in sentence form so it reads naturally.

I’d start by glancing for the allergen section first, because Korean packages almost always group it clearly. The heading 알레르기 유발물질 or sometimes 알레르기 정보 will tell you where the mandatory declarations are. In that section, “contains” is usually written as 함유, and “may contain” appears as 포함될 수 있음 or 미량 포함될 수 있음.

After that, I’d move to the ingredient list itself, which is labeled 원재료명 or 원재료 및 함량. That’s where you’ll catch the hidden forms of allergens, since sub‑ingredients are listed in parentheses.

Once you’ve checked that, look for manufacturing‑line warnings. These show up as phrases like 동일 제조시설에서 사용, 같은 제조라인에서 생산, or 함께 취급하는 시설, all of which signal shared equipment or shared production lines.

If you want a final quick check, glance near the storage or manufacturer info for any extra advisory wording, since some brands repeat allergy notes there.

Following that simple flow—official allergen section, ingredient list, shared‑line warnings—usually gives you everything you need without missing the subtle phrasing.
