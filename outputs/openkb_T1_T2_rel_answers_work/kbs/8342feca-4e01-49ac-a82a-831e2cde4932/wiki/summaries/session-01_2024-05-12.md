# Summary of session-01_2024-05-12

**Date:** 2024-05-12

## Core ideas
- Use quick mental **checkpoints** to decide on after‑work invitations:
  1. **Immediate emotional reaction** – spark vs. dread. [[concepts/emotional-checkpoints]]
  2. **Recent workload** – have you been "on" nonstop? [[concepts/energy-management]]
  3. **Recharge value** – does the plan energize you or drain you? *(concept not linked)*
- Communicate refusals **honestly but gently** to avoid hurt feelings. Phrase the refusal as a personal need rather than a judgment of the other person. [[concepts/gentle-communication]]
- Offer **alternative meeting times** (daytime coffee, lunch, weekend hang) that fit your energy pattern. *(concept not linked)*
- Maintain a consistent expectation: *evenings are for quiet downtime*.

## Suggested text-message templates
### For a coworker
> "Hey! Just a heads‑up that I usually keep my evenings quiet because that’s how I recharge, so I end up saying no to most after‑work plans. I’d still love to catch up, though—lunch or a daytime coffee works way better for me if you ever want to do that."

### For a close friend
> "I adore you, but I’ve learned I really need my nights at home to feel sane. So if I skip evening plans, it’s never about you. Let’s plan a weekend hang or a coffee instead—I’m always up for that."

### Quick “in‑the‑moment” decline
> "Ahh I’m wiped tonight and sticking to my chill‑evening routine, but thank you for thinking of me! Let’s find a time that fits my energy a bit better."

## Practical takeaways
- **Establish a personal rule**: evenings = downtime. Communicate it once; reinforce as needed.
- **Use the three checkpoints** as a rapid self‑audit before responding.
- Keep a few **template messages** handy for fast, courteous replies.
- Propose **daytime or weekend alternatives** to preserve the relationship while respecting your energy.
