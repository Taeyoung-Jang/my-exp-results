# Emotional Checkpoints for Social Decision‑Making

**Type:** Concept

## Overview
Emotional Checkpoints are a trio of rapid self‑assessment questions used to gauge whether an after‑work social invitation fits your current energy state. The method was introduced in [[summaries/session-01_2024-05-12]] and is reinforced by the cue‑based habit and after‑work routine strategies outlined in [[summaries/session-37_2025-01-11]]. By turning vague feelings into actionable decisions, the checkpoints help you stay aligned with personal well‑being while maintaining social engagement.

## The Three Checkpoints
1. **Immediate emotional reaction** – Do you feel a spark of interest or an instant sense of dread? A positive spark suggests the invite may be beneficial, while dread signals a likely drain.
2. **Recent workload and fatigue** – Have you been "on" nonstop today or this week? If you’ve had a high‑energy stretch, opting for downtime is a form of self‑maintenance.
3. **Recharge potential** – Will the planned activity genuinely replenish your energy, or is it likely to leave you more exhausted?

## How to Apply
- **Run the three questions** the moment the invitation arrives, especially after work.
- **Tie the checkpoints to cue‑based habits**: set up physical cues (e.g., packed salsa bag, ready‑to‑grab snack) that remind you to pause and run the checklist before deciding.
- **Leverage night‑before preparation** from the after‑work routine guide: lay out class clothes, place the bag by the door, and have a snack ready. These cues streamline the moment you get home and prompt the checkpoint assessment.
- **If the majority of answers point toward low energy or negative emotion**, politely decline and suggest an alternative that fits your rhythm. When answers are mixed, consider the type of interaction (brief coffee vs. a long evening out) and choose the option that leans toward recharge.
- **Minimum‑viable fallback**: On genuinely exhausted days, you can still honor the habit by following the same cue‑based steps up to getting into the car and then either attend the class or do a brief five‑minute warm‑up at home. This ensures the cue remains active without over‑exertion.
- **Example for a salsa night**: after arriving home at 6:00 PM, change into class clothes, grab the pre‑packed snack, and run the three checkpoints. If the answers are positive, head out by 6:20 PM; if not, use the minimum‑viable fallback to either drive to the studio and decide there or do a short dance warm‑up at home.

## Benefits
- Provides a structured yet quick decision‑making framework.
- Reduces social anxiety by grounding choices in personal well‑being.
- Integrates seamlessly with [[concepts/after-work-routine]] and [[concepts/cue-based-habit]] to minimize friction.
- Supports consistent energy management and helps maintain boundaries without guilt.
- Offers a low‑effort fallback that keeps the habit loop intact on low‑energy days.
