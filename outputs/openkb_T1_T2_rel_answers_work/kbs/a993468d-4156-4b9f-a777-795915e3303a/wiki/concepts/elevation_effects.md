## Elevation Effects

**Elevation effects** refer to the range of physiological and day‑to‑day changes people notice when they move to higher altitudes.  The conversation in [[summaries/session-06_2022-06-15]] identifies several common impacts:

- **Breathing & cardiovascular response** – Air pressure drops, so each breath delivers less oxygen.  People experience faster breathing, a higher resting heart rate, and quicker fatigue during exercise, especially above **1500–2000 m**.
- **Sleep disturbances** – Light, frequent awakenings are typical during the first days of exposure.
- **Hydration needs** – The thinner, drier air increases fluid loss; steady water intake becomes more important.
- **Cooking adjustments** – Water boils at lower temperatures, requiring longer cooking times or altered recipes for foods such as pasta, rice, and baked goods.
- **Acclimatization timeline** – Initial symptom relief occurs within a few days as ventilation and heart output increase.  More substantial physiological adaptation, like increased red‑blood‑cell production, takes **2–3 weeks** (see [[concepts/acclimatization]]).
- **Risk of altitude sickness** – Persistent headaches, nausea, dizziness, or severe fatigue signal that the body is not adapting normally; these are hallmarks of **altitude sickness** (see [[concepts/altitude_sickness]]).

Understanding these effects helps guide practical strategies—hydration, gradual activity, and monitoring for red‑flag symptoms—when traveling from lower elevations (e.g., 600 m) to mid‑altitude regions (2000–3000 m).
