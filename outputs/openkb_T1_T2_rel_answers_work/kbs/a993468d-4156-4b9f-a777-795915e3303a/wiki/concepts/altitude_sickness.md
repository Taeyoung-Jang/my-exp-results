# Altitude Sickness

Altitude sickness refers to the range of uncomfortable and potentially dangerous symptoms that appear when a person ascends to elevations where the atmospheric pressure—and thus the available oxygen—is reduced. The condition typically manifests above **1500–2000 m** and can affect anyone, though susceptibility varies.

## Typical Early Signs
- Persistent, throbbing headache
- Nausea or vomiting
- Dizziness or light‑headedness
- Unusual fatigue or weakness, even at rest
- Shortness of breath that does not improve with light activity

These symptoms were highlighted in the discussion from [[summaries/session-06_2022-06-15]], where they were distinguished from normal short‑term adjustment.

## Distinguishing Normal Acclimation
Normal acclimatization involves mild shortness of breath during exertion, a light headache that eases with rest, and slight fatigue. When these mild effects linger or worsen, they cross into the territory of altitude sickness.

## Risk Factors
- Rapid ascent without an acclimatization period
- Exertion soon after arrival at altitude
- Dehydration, often exacerbated by alcohol consumption
- Pre‑existing health conditions affecting respiration or circulation

## Prevention Strategies
1. **Gradual ascent** – give the body time to adjust, especially when moving from low elevations (e.g., 600 m) to 2500–3000 m.
2. **Stay hydrated** – steady water intake helps mitigate symptoms.
3. **Limit alcohol** on the first night; it can worsen dehydration and headaches.
4. **Avoid heavy exertion** on the arrival day; begin with light activity.

These recommendations align with the advice given in the source conversation, which emphasized taking it easy on the first afternoon and ensuring good sleep before a strenuous hike.

## Relationship to Other Concepts
- **[[concepts/acclimatization]]** describes the physiological processes (e.g., increased ventilation, red‑blood‑cell production) that reduce the likelihood of altitude sickness over days to weeks.
- **[[concepts/elevation_effects]]** outlines the broader changes in breathing, exercise tolerance, sleep, cooking, and hydration that begin around 1500 m and set the stage for potential sickness.

Understanding altitude sickness and its interplay with acclimatization and elevation effects helps travelers plan safe ascents and recognize when to pause or descend.
