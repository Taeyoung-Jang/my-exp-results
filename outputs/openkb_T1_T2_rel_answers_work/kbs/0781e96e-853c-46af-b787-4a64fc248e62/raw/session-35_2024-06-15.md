# Conversation session 35

Date: 2024-06-15 08:30

**User:** I’ve been thinking about how much I rely on my phone for basic day-to-day stuff like keeping track of time. Do you think dedicated gadgets still make sense in 2026, or is it basically always better to just use a smartphone?

**Assistant:** I think dedicated gadgets can still make sense, but only when they genuinely do something better than a phone. For most everyday tasks—time, reminders, quick notes—a smartphone is just too convenient to beat.

That said, some devices still earn their place. Good watches, e‑readers, high‑end cameras, or fitness trackers can offer a nicer experience, better battery life, or fewer distractions than a phone ever will. It really comes down to whether the standalone device adds enough value that you’d actually enjoy using it instead of your phone.

So it’s not that phones replace everything; they just replace everything that isn’t meaningfully better on its own.

**User:** Ever since I switched to this simple analog wristwatch a few months ago, I’ve liked how it keeps me on time without any apps or charging. With that in mind, what other dedicated gadgets (if any) do you think are actually worth using mainly because they reduce distractions rather than add features?

**Assistant:** If you’re enjoying the analog‑watch vibe—something that quietly does its job without dragging you into a notification spiral—there are a few other gadgets that tend to work well for that same reason.

A Kindle‑style e‑reader is one of the big ones. It’s basically just for reading, the screen is calm, and there’s nothing buzzing at you. People who switch often say they read more simply because the temptations of a phone aren’t there.

A dedicated music player can also be nice if you like listening without your phone pulling you into messages or apps. Even something as simple as a tiny clip‑on player feels surprisingly freeing if you use it for workouts or walks.

Some folks also love having a basic alarm clock instead of using their phone at the bedside. It keeps the phone out of arm’s reach at night, which cuts down on late‑night scrolling and first‑thing‑in‑the‑morning doomchecking.

And for note‑taking, a paper notebook or a simple digital notepad can be great. You write, you’re done, and there’s nothing else competing for your attention.

None of these are “necessary,” but they can protect your attention in a way a phone rarely does. If a device makes your day feel quieter or more focused, it’s usually worth keeping around.

**User:** If I wanted to try just one of those next—either an e‑reader or a basic alarm clock—which do you think would make the bigger difference day-to-day for cutting down on phone time?

**Assistant:** Between the two, the alarm clock usually makes the bigger day‑to‑day impact. It forces your phone out of your bedroom routine, which is when most people accidentally rack up a lot of screen time. If your phone isn’t the thing you reach for right before sleep or right when you wake up, your overall usage naturally drops.

An e‑reader is great too, but it mostly changes your reading habits. A standalone alarm clock changes two big daily touchpoints with your phone, so the effect tends to be stronger and more immediate.

**User:** That makes sense. Any tips for setting up a basic alarm clock routine so I don’t just grab my phone anyway—like where to put the phone at night, what to use for a morning routine, and whether a “dumb” clock is better than a smart speaker?

**Assistant:** A simple approach works best. Put your phone somewhere you’d have to physically get out of bed to reach—on a dresser across the room or even in the hallway. The goal isn’t to lock it away forever, just to break the reflex of grabbing it the moment you wake up or the moment you’re bored at night.

A “dumb” alarm clock is usually better than a smart speaker because it can’t tempt you with voice commands, news briefings, or music. You set the time, it rings, and that’s it. That tiny bit of friction helps a lot.

For the morning routine, try giving yourself one small task before you touch your phone, like getting dressed, brushing your teeth, or making coffee. It creates a natural buffer so you start the day on your own terms instead of diving straight into notifications.

Once those pieces are in place, you barely have to think about it—the routine handles the discipline for you.
