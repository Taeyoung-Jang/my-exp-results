**Assistant:** It sounds like you’re already doing the smartest thing: shifting the work to the fixtures instead of your wrist. You can lean into that by shaping your whole corner around “light‑touch” tasks. Keep the tools you adjust often—measuring tools, pencils, small squares, setup blocks, wrenches for fence adjustments—right at the height where your forearm can stay neutral. Anything heavier or rarely used can live higher or lower so you’re not lifting awkwardly with that wrist.

Jigs that hold small pieces securely will probably give you the biggest payoff. A good general-purpose sled, a few soft‑jaw hold‑downs, and a small parts carrier you can slide past a cutter without pinching anything should all become workhorses. Even a simple toggle‑clamp board sized for your machines can save your wrist over and over. Build them so they’re easy to grab with your non‑injured hand and don’t require much squeezing or twisting to lock in place.

For those final whisper‑thin passes, the trick is to shift precision away from your grip and into the machine setup. Dial the fence or stop blocks until you’re confident the machine will do the exact cut, then focus on guiding the work smoothly rather than holding it tightly. On tiny parts, a carrier sled with a handle you can push with your palm instead of your fingers keeps things controlled without a death grip. If the surface is slick, a bit of low‑tack grip tape on your jigs often gives you enough traction to stay relaxed.

If it all feels like the work is “held” and you’re mostly just nudging it through, you’ve got the right idea.

**Assistant:** For the reach zone, think of a shallow arc in front of you where your elbow stays close to your side and your wrist stays straight. Anything you adjust constantly should live in that arc: your small square, depth gauge, a couple of pencils, your most‑used drill bits, hex keys for fence and bit changes, and a light mallet. These are easiest in a shallow drawer or a low‑friction tray right under the bench surface so you can slide it open with your hip or forearm instead of twisting caps or digging. On the wall, keep only things you lift rather than manipulate: your larger squares, clamps, and jigs. Let the wall be for grab‑and‑go objects that don’t require fine wrist movement.

The vise and drill press pair nicely if the drill press table just overlaps your bench edge; that way you can transition a part from vise to drill with minimal lifting. The router table works best slightly lower than the bench so your arms relax while guiding stock. Keep all three arranged so you pivot your torso rather than making reachy arm movements.

For small‑parts carriers, the gentlest style is a flat sled with a tall rear fence and a rounded push handle you can press with the heel of your palm. A mushroom‑style knob works too if it’s large enough to let you push with the whole palm instead of pinching. I also like a side handle that’s angled forward about thirty degrees so your wrist stays straight as you feed the piece.

For clamping on these sleds, toggle clamps are usually the least effort because they lock with a short, low‑force motion, and you can adjust their pressure once and then leave them. Cams feel good when they’re tuned well, but they take more wrist rotation. Wedges hold beautifully and need almost no strength, but they’re slower and can creep unless the surfaces are just right. For your situation, a toggle clamp set to light pressure is probably the sweet spot: quick to flip, very little wrist strain, and predictable every time.

**Assistant:** Here’s a compact, concrete plan you can follow without turning it into a hunt‑and‑peck exercise.

First, build the general clamping board. Make it about 12×18 inches from 3/4‑inch ply with a stiff backer strip on the long edge so it won’t flex when you clamp it in the bench vise. Drop two light‑pressure toggle clamps along the front edge and a strip of 150‑grit grip tape on the surface. This becomes your universal “hold the thing so I don’t have to” platform. It’s fast, flat, and doesn’t ask your wrist for anything.

Second, make the small‑parts carrier for the router table. Something around 8×10 inches works. Use 1/2‑inch ply so it’s light. Add a two‑inch‑tall rear fence and a palm‑heel push handle that’s shaped more like a dome than a knob—about two inches in diameter—so you can lean your palm on it instead of gripping. One toggle clamp on top is enough; keep the clamping pressure mild so it locks with almost no force. A thin UHMW strip on the bottom keeps the sled sliding smoothly so you don’t need to steer it with your wrist.

Third, build the drill‑press carrier. It can be even simpler: a 6×8‑inch ply base with a short back fence and a forward‑angled handle made from a scrap of dowel set around thirty degrees. The dowel lets you push or steady the part with the heel of your hand. A wedge pair works well on the drill press because you don’t need much force, and tapping the wedges together with your non‑injured hand avoids twisty motions.

Fourth, add a shallow top drawer or pull‑out tray for all your small set‑and‑adjust tools—setup blocks, depth gauge, small square, hex keys, pencil. Keep it just below the bench surface so you can open it with a hip bump or forearm. No tiny lids, no twist caps, nothing that needs pinching.

Fifth, mount a simple wall rail for larger items that don’t require delicate wrist movements: long squares, clamps, and your jigs. Hanging these means no rummaging and no twisting offsets.

For hardware, look for palm‑style knobs instead of star knobs. The palm versions let you press rather than twist. Quick‑release vises or retrofitted QR add‑ons are worth it because they convert long crank motions into one simple lever action. For your router table, a micro‑adjust bit‑height wheel with a large diameter is easier on your wrist than a small twist knob, and above‑table adjustments help you stay in a neutral, upright position. On the drill press, a keyed chuck with a T‑handle you can push with your palm is often easier than the small key styles that require a tight pinch.

If you aim for everything to be either palm‑pressed, hip‑nudged, or flipped with a single motion, you’ll feel the change right away.

**User:** One more thing: for those whisper-thin final passes, how do you actually “dial it in” without a bunch of tiny knob tweaks? I can do test cuts, but I’m trying to avoid repetitive micro-adjusting that ends up irritating my wrist.

Are there a couple reliable methods—like using shims, feeler gauges, setup blocks, stop collars—so I can sneak up on a fit with fewer adjustments? And if you have a favorite approach for the router table specifically vs. the drill press, I’d love to hear it.

**Assistant:** For clean final passes without a bunch of fussy knob‑twiddling, the trick is to shift the precision into fixed references so the machine can’t wander and you don’t have to “sneak up” on anything with your wrist.

One simple method is using thin, consistent shims. Set the cut slightly shallow on purpose, make a test pass, then slip a known‑thickness shim between your fence and its stop to bump it forward the exact amount you want. Because you’re not turning anything—just adding or removing a shim—you avoid the micro‑adjusting completely. Brass shim stock, playing cards, or even veneer offcuts work. Once you learn their thickness by feel, the process becomes almost automatic.

Feeler gauges or setup blocks work the same way for depth. On the router table, you can drop the bit until it kisses the top of a block or gauge rather than twisting the adjuster in tiny increments. If your router has above‑table adjustment, you can rest your palm on the wrench or crank and let your shoulder do the motion instead of your wrist. A stop collar on the bit’s shaft helps too: set the collar once, then you can return to a known depth without hunting for it.

On the router table specifically, the shim‑behind‑the‑fence trick is the hero because fences tend to move predictably in straight lines. Set the fence a hair shallow, slide the shim behind the outfeed side, tighten once, and you’re done. For tiny side‑to‑side refinement, a thin feeler gauge between the fence and its mounting point gives repeatable increments without touching a knob.

On the drill press, it’s easier to work vertically, so I’d lean on fixed‑height spacers. Stack blocks under your workpiece or under the drill press table so the quill movement stays the same each time. If you need to “creep up” on a depth, swap a spacer for a thinner one rather than tweaking the depth stop wheel. A depth stop with a large, palm‑friendly collar also lets you lock it with a push instead of a pinch.

If everything is based on swapping solid references—shims, spacers, collared stops—you only do one real adjustment at the start, and every refinement after that happens without asking your wrist to do precision work.
