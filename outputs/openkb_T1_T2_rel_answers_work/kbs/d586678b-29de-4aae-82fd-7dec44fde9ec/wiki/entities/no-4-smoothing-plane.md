# No. 4 Smoothing Plane

**Type:** product

The No. 4 smoothing plane is a classic, relatively heavy, bevel‑down hand plane favored for achieving a glass‑like surface on wood. In the conversation **[[summaries/session-14_2022-06-15]]**, it is highlighted as the go‑to tool for fine finishing work.

## Key Characteristics
- **Size & Weight:** Larger than a bench plane but smaller than a jack plane, providing enough mass for steady cuts without being unwieldy.
- **Blade (Iron):** Works best with a **[[concepts/sharp-iron-preparation]]** routine – dead‑sharp, lightly cambered, and polished back.
- **Chipbreaker:** Optimal performance relies on a well‑tuned **[[concepts/chipbreaker-setup]]**; the recommendation is to start the breaker ~0.1 mm from the iron edge.
- **Mouth & Frog:** The frog should give solid support; the mouth is kept moderate and only tightened when challenging grain demands it.
- **Use Cases:** Ideal for ultra‑fine smoothing, especially on figured grain where tear‑out is a concern. It can be paired with a **[[entities/york-pitch-frog]]** for higher cutting angles if needed.

## Relationship to Other Tools & Concepts
- Often compared with the **[[entities/no-4-5-smoothing-plane]]**, which is a slightly larger version offering extra mass.
- Serves as an alternative to a **[[entities/bevel-up-smoother]]** when a chipbreaker is desired; bevel‑up designs rely more on edge geometry than a chipbreaker.
- Works in concert with the **[[entities/chipbreaker]]** to mitigate **[[concepts/tear-out-diagnosis]]** issues.

## Practical Advice from the Session
1. **Prioritize a sharp iron** before any other adjustments.
2. **Set the chipbreaker close** (≈0.1 mm) for tight control over shavings.
3. **Adjust the frog** only to tighten the mouth for difficult grain; otherwise keep a middle‑of‑the‑road setting.
4. **Test cuts** on the workpiece; if tear‑out persists, consider a higher angle via a York‑pitch frog.
