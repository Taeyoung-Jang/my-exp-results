# Conversation session 14

Date: 2022-06-15 14:30

**User:** I’ve been getting more into hand-tool woodworking lately, especially tuning and using hand planes, but I’m still fuzzy on what really matters for getting a glassy surface. If you were setting up a plane from scratch, what steps would you prioritize (iron prep, chipbreaker, mouth opening, frog alignment, etc.), and how would you decide between different plane types for smoothing?

**Assistant:** I’d focus on the few things that make the biggest difference and not worry about every possible tweak.

First, I’d get the iron dead sharp. That usually matters more than anything else. A small camber on a smoother helps avoid track marks, and a polished back plus a clean, even bevel are enough. I wouldn’t obsess over mirror finishes beyond what’s needed to get a keen edge.

After that, I’d make sure the chipbreaker fits tightly against the iron. No light should show at the edge, because any gap can cause clogging or tear‑out. For smoothing, I’d set the breaker very close to the edge—just a hair—but not so close that it causes choking.

Then I’d check that the frog or bed gives solid support to the iron. You’re basically looking for a stable platform with no chatter. I only adjust the frog forward to tighten the mouth if I’m working difficult grain. Otherwise, a middle‑of‑the‑road setting usually works fine.

I worry about the mouth opening only when dealing with tear‑out‑prone woods. A tight mouth helps, but a sharp iron and good chipbreaker setup usually matter more.

As for choosing the plane, a dedicated smoothing plane like a No. 4 or 4½ is the easiest to set up for an ultra-fine cut, especially if it’s on the heavier side. A bevel‑up smoother can work beautifully too, especially if you like controlling the attack angle with different bevels, but they rely on a perfect edge since there’s no chipbreaker to help. Traditional bevel‑down planes with a tuned chipbreaker tend to give excellent results on tricky grain.

If I had to pick just one setup for the nicest surface, I’d grab a well‑tuned No. 4 with a very sharp, lightly cambered iron and a chipbreaker set close. That combination gives a reliable, glassy finish on almost anything.

**User:** That helps a lot. When you say “set the chipbreaker very close,” what are you using as a starting distance in practice (like 0.1 mm / 0.004" vs farther), and does that change depending on the wood or shaving thickness? Also, how do you tell the difference between tear-out from bad grain direction vs problems in the setup (breaker gap, mouth too tight, iron not sharp enough)?

**Assistant:** For a true smoothing cut, I usually start with the breaker about the thickness of a fine shaving away from the edge—something in the neighborhood of 0.1 mm or about four thousandths of an inch. That’s close enough to control the chip but not so close that it constantly clogs. If the wood is mild and you’re taking gossamer shavings, you can ease it back a bit. If the grain is really squirrely, you creep it closer, but only as much as your plane will tolerate without choking.

Shaving thickness matters too. The thinner the shaving, the closer you can run the breaker. If you’re taking slightly heavier cuts, the breaker may need a touch more distance so the shavings flow.

Tear‑out from grain direction usually shows up as small patches or streaks in places where the grain reverses. You can often feel the resistance change or see the fiber direction shift. If you flip the board or skew the plane and the problem disappears, that’s a grain‑direction issue.

Setup problems tend to have a different feel. A too‑wide breaker gap gives long, continuous tear‑out even in spots where the grain should behave. A dull iron leaves a slightly fuzzy surface regardless of grain and feels like you’re pushing harder than you should. A mouth that’s too tight makes the plane feel starved, with shavings bunching or jamming even when the iron is sharp.

Once you’ve used a sharp iron and a known‑good breaker setting a few times, the differences become pretty easy to sense. The plane either feels smooth and predictable, or it starts sending you clear signals that something in the setup needs a tweak.

**User:** Got it. One more thing: when I’m chasing that last bit of tear-out on really figured grain, do you reach for a higher cutting angle (like a York pitch / back bevel / bevel-up with a higher microbevel), or do you stay with a standard angle and rely on the chipbreaker? I’m trying to figure out which path is more reliable before I start modifying anything.

**Assistant:** For me, a well‑set chipbreaker with a standard cutting angle is usually the first line of defense, because it keeps the plane easy to push and doesn’t require altering the tool permanently. A close breaker on a razor‑sharp iron will handle a surprising amount of gnarly figure.

When the grain is so wild that even a tight breaker and whisper‑thin shavings still leave little patches of tear‑out, that’s when I jump to a higher angle. A York pitch frog or a small back bevel on a bevel‑down plane gives you a noticeable bump in control. A bevel‑up smoother with a higher microbevel does the same thing but with less fuss. The tradeoff is that higher angles take more effort and don’t leave quite the same silky feel as a lower‑angle cut.

In practice, I only reach for the higher angle when the normal setup stops working. If a standard pitch plus a tight breaker is still giving trouble in a couple spots, a higher angle almost always cleans it up.

**User:** One constraint on my end: I rely on this hand plane for all my fine finishing work because it's the only one I own that holds an edge this well. Given that, if you had to choose just one “non-destructive” tweak to maximize tear-out resistance on figured wood (breaker set, back bevel, tighter mouth, skewing, wax/lube, etc.), what would you recommend I try first and what’s a simple way to tell if it helped?

**Assistant:** I’d start with the chipbreaker, because it’s completely reversible and almost always gives the most leverage against tear‑out without changing how the plane feels. Just creep it a little closer than you normally would and take a couple of test passes on the actual board. If it helped, the plane will start producing clean, controlled shavings even right over the problem spots, and the surface will suddenly shift from patchy to uniformly smooth. If it’s too close, you’ll know right away because the shavings get crinkly or start to clog.

It’s the quickest experiment, and you can back it off in seconds if it doesn’t improve the surface. Once you feel the “snap” of the fibers breaking cleanly in tricky grain, you’ll know you’re in the sweet spot.
