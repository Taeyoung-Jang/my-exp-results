# Climate Change Activism

**Type:** other

Climate Change Activism refers to the collective efforts of individuals, NGOs, and advocacy groups pushing for policies and societal changes that mitigate global warming and its impacts.  It focuses on reducing greenhouse‑gas emissions, transitioning to renewable energy, and fostering climate‑resilient communities.

## Key Points from [[summaries/session-14_2023-06-15]]
- In the discussed conversation, stability arguments are often raised against climate policy reforms.  Proponents of the status quo cite concerns such as **economic disruption**, **energy reliability**, and the risk of adopting **insufficiently mature technologies**.  These concerns can be legitimate risk considerations when dealing with long‑lived energy infrastructures.
- The same language of stability is also used to **delay necessary climate action**, especially when the harms of inaction (e.g., escalating temperature rise, extreme weather events) outweigh the speculative risks of transition.  In such cases, stability arguments function as a form of **power‑preservation**, protecting the financial and political interests of entrenched fossil‑fuel stakeholders.
- The conversation highlights a practical way to assess such arguments by applying [[concepts/risk-assessment]]: look for concrete, evidence‑based risks versus vague, self‑serving claims.

## Relationship to Core Concepts
- **[[concepts/stability-in-politics]]** – Climate Change Activism encounters stability narratives that argue for incremental change to avoid destabilizing energy markets.
- **[[concepts/power-preservation]]** – Some stability rhetoric serves to preserve the economic and political power of industries that benefit from the existing carbon‑intensive system.
- **[[concepts/risk-assessment]]** – Activists often frame their proposals in terms of long‑term risk mitigation, contrasting with short‑term stability concerns.

## Notable Contexts
- Climate policy debates where activists push for urgent emissions caps, renewable‑energy subsidies, and climate‑justice legislation.
- Situations where opposition frames rapid transition as a threat to jobs, energy security, or economic stability, thereby using stability arguments to **obstruct** reform.
