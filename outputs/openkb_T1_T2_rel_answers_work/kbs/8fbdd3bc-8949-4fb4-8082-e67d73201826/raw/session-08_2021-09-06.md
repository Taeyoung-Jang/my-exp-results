**User:** I’m planning a little get-together this weekend and trying to put together a snack menu plus a couple simple make-ahead dishes—could you suggest some ideas and maybe a quick checklist of ingredients to watch for? I need to avoid any foods containing peanuts because I have a severe allergy. Also, if you have tips on how to ask guests about allergens without making it awkward, that’d be awesome.

**Assistant:** For a casual get‑together, you can keep things simple with crowd‑friendly snacks like veggie sticks with hummus, a cheese plate with crackers, fruit, popcorn, or tortilla chips with salsa or guacamole. For make‑ahead dishes, something like a baked pasta, a big salad you can toss right before serving, or a slow‑cooker chili (using beans or turkey instead of anything peanut‑based) usually works well and doesn’t require last‑minute fussing.

When you shop, it helps to mentally check labels for anything that might hide peanut ingredients, especially packaged snacks, sauces, desserts, and anything labeled “may contain peanuts.” Sticking to whole foods or brands you already trust can make it easier.

If you want to ask guests about allergens without it feeling awkward, you can keep it light and casual. Something like, “I’m planning the menu and want to make sure everyone can enjoy everything—any allergies or no‑go foods I should know about?” Most people appreciate being asked, and phrasing it as part of ordinary planning keeps it from feeling heavy.

**User:** Could you give me a more specific menu (maybe 2–3 snack options and 2 make-ahead dishes) and include a “watch list” of common label terms/foods where peanuts can sneak in? Also, any ideas for a simple dessert that’s safe and easy to serve to a group?

**Assistant:** You could go with a small spread built around three easy snacks, like a fresh veggie board with hummus, a mild cheese assortment with safe crackers, and warm tortilla chips with salsa or guacamole. For make‑ahead dishes, a baked pasta with tomato sauce and mozzarella holds well and reheats beautifully, and a simple roasted‑chicken and rice dish (seasoned with herbs, lemon, and olive oil) can be cooked earlier in the day and served warm or at room temp.

A dessert that keeps things simple and peanut‑safe is a fruit crisp made with oats, butter, sugar, and whatever fruit you like; it bakes in one pan, can be made ahead, and tastes good warm or cold. A build‑your‑own ice‑cream sundae bar can also work as long as you choose clearly labeled, peanut‑free ice creams and toppings.

For your peanut “watch list,” keep an eye out for anything mentioning peanut oil, peanut flour, ground nuts, mixed‑nut products, and generic terms like “nut paste” or “nut meal.” Some packaged cookies, candies, granola bars, and Asian‑style sauces can hide peanut ingredients, and labels that say “may contain peanuts” or “processed on shared equipment” are worth avoiding if your allergy is severe.

If you keep the menu simple and stick to brands you already trust, it should be easy to pull off a comfortable, safe spread.

**User:** Could you swap the roasted-chicken and rice idea for a vegetarian make-ahead dish, and also suggest a couple peanut-free store-bought brands/items (like crackers, hummus, ice cream, or salsa) that are usually good options? I know labels vary, so general suggestions are fine, but I’d love a starting point.

**Assistant:** Sure, you can swap in something vegetarian without changing the feel of the menu. A make‑ahead option that works well is a hearty vegetable and bean bake: roasted zucchini, bell peppers, onions, tomatoes, and white beans layered with a little olive oil, herbs, and parmesan, then baked until everything melds together. It refrigerates and reheats nicely. Another easy one is a big roasted‑vegetable couscous or quinoa bowl with lemony dressing; you can serve it warm or at room temperature.

For store‑bought items, you’re right that labels vary, but a few brands are often good starting points. Many people with peanut allergies do well with Simple Mills or Triscuit crackers, Sabra or Ithaca hummus, Häagen‑Dazs or Tillamook ice cream, and salsas from brands like Frontera or Herdez. That said, it’s still important to check each package—especially for “may contain peanuts” or “processed on shared equipment” notes—because facilities and recipes can change.

If you stick with brands you’ve used safely before and double‑check anything new, the menu should come together smoothly and stay allergy‑safe.

**User:** Those brand suggestions help—could you also recommend a few peanut-free ice cream flavors/toppings that are lower risk for cross-contamination, and how you’d set up the dessert table to avoid mix-ups (like separate scoops, signage, etc.)?

**Assistant:** You can usually play it safest by choosing single‑flavor ice creams from brands you already trust, since simple flavors tend to be made on dedicated lines more often than mix‑ins. Vanilla, chocolate, strawberry, or mint‑chip (as long as the chips aren’t made in a shared nut facility) are common lower‑risk choices. For toppings, things like fresh berries, sliced bananas, chocolate syrup, caramel sauce, or sprinkles from a nut‑free line are usually easier to keep safe than baked add‑ins.

To avoid mix‑ups at the table, keep each flavor in its own spot with its own scoop and set the toppings in their own space with separate spoons. A small note card next to each bowl that says something simple like “nut‑free, please use this scoop only” helps people stay mindful without making a big deal out of it. If you set everything out cleanly with clear separation, guests can serve themselves comfortably while you keep the allergy risk low.
