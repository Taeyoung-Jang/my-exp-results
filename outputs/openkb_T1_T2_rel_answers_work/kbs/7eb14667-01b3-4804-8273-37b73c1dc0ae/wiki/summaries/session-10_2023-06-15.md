# Summary of session-10_2023-06-15

**Main focus**: Practical methods for individuals to evaluate political claims online, resist tribal thinking, and maintain an independent, well‑informed perspective.

## Core Ideas
- Shift attention from *who* is saying something to *how* the claim is supported and whether the reasoning is sound. critical thinking
- Look for **clear, traceable evidence**; treat emotional appeals, outrage, or vague vibes as red flags. information framing
- Read **multiple well‑sourced perspectives** to see overlap and divergence, not to create a forced “balance.”
- Allow opinions to stay **tentative** initially, updating them as evidence accumulates.

## General Credibility Checklist
1. **Identify the source** – Who is making the claim? Do they have a track record of accuracy? If unknown, treat as unverified. source verification
2. **Check the evidence** – Are there links to data, documents, or direct quotes you can verify? Absence → red flag.
3. **Spot framing tricks** – Is the language engineered to provoke anger, fear, or tribal “us‑vs‑them” thinking?
4. **Cross‑compare** – Find at least one other reputable source covering the same fact. Confirm basic consistency.
5. **Note knowledge gaps** – Write down 1‑2 key pieces (statistic, date, quote) to look up later. If they hold, the claim gains credibility; if not, discard.

## Tailored Checklists
### 1. Viral chart or infographic
- Verify the creator and any cited data source.
- Examine visual design: truncated axes, odd scales, misleading categories.
- Quick verification sites: official statistics portals (e.g., government agency), neutral fact‑checking sites.

### 2. Short clipped video of a politician
- Check for selective editing or sensational captions.
- Locate the full recording or transcript for context.
- Trusted archives: official channel uploads, C‑SPAN‑style repositories, major news outlets with unedited clips.

### 3. “Study says …” headline
- Compare headline to the study’s actual scope and findings.
- Identify authors, sample size, peer‑review status.
- Quick verification: journal abstract, public repository (e.g., PubMed, arXiv), science‑focused fact‑checkers.

## Default Weekly Routine for Independent Readers
1. **Select a small mix of outlets** – One straight‑news source, one analytical/explanatory outlet, one data‑focused site. The goal is methodological diversity, not ideological balance. media literacy
2. **Weekly pattern**:
   - Skim headlines from the core set.
   - Pick one topic of personal interest.
   - Read a deeper piece on that topic from a source you don’t usually follow.
3. **Guardrail** – When a story triggers strong certainty or irritation, pause and cross‑check with a neutral outlet or primary material (transcript, dataset, official statement). This single step mitigates echo‑chamber drift.

## Reliable Quick‑Check Resources
- Fact‑checking organizations (e.g., Snopes, PolitiFact, FactCheck.org).
- Government statistical agencies (e.g., U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics, Eurostat).
- Academic repositories (e.g., Google Scholar, PubMed, arXiv).
- Media archives (C‑SPAN, official press‑release platforms).
