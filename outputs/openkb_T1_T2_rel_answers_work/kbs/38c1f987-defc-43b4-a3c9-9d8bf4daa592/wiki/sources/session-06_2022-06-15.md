**User:** I’ve been thinking about how different people process feedback—especially criticism—really differently. Some seem to shrug it off instantly, while others replay it for days. What do you think shapes that difference most: personality, upbringing, stress levels, or something else?

**Assistant:** It’s usually a mix, but different factors tend to matter more for different people. Personality sets a baseline—some folks are naturally more sensitive or self‑questioning—while upbringing can teach you either to see criticism as dangerous or as something normal and useful. Stress plays a huge role too; even the most resilient person can take things harder when they’re overwhelmed. And past experiences matter a lot. If someone has been judged harshly or unfairly before, even gentle feedback can hit a nerve.

Most people aren’t fixed in one style, though. When they feel safe, supported, and rested, they handle criticism more easily. When they feel threatened or stretched thin, it lands much harder.

**User:** I’m trying to figure out how much of this is “me” versus circumstances, because I know that as an INFP, I tend to dwell on criticism longer than others might. If that’s my baseline, what are a couple practical ways to take in feedback without spiraling—especially when I’m already stressed?

**Assistant:** Two things tend to help without fighting your natural INFP wiring. First, slow the moment down. Instead of reacting to the emotional sting, give yourself a little buffer—something as simple as saying, “Let me sit with that and come back to you.” That pause keeps the stress reaction from taking over and gives your mind space to sort the feedback from the feelings.

Second, translate the criticism into your own language once you’re calmer. INFPs often take things personally because they process through emotions first, so reframing the feedback as information rather than judgment—what the other person actually wants or needs, separate from your worth—can keep it from turning into a spiral. Over time it becomes a habit, not a fight with your nature, just a way to give yourself a softer landing.

**User:** That makes sense. Could you give me a simple “script” I can use in the moment when someone gives me criticism (like at work), plus a quick checklist for how to process it later so I don’t replay it all night?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple script you can use in the moment:  
“Thanks for telling me. I want to make sure I understand, so I’m going to take a little time to think it over. I’ll follow up once I’ve had a chance to look at it more clearly.”

And for later, you can walk yourself through a quick mental sequence without turning it into a long replay: first ask what the person was actually trying to communicate, then separate the useful part from your emotional reaction, then decide on one small action you can take, and finally let the rest go by reminding yourself that the feedback is about a behavior, not your worth.

**User:** Could you make that checklist more concrete—like 5–7 specific questions I can write down and answer after I get feedback? Also, what should I do if the criticism was vague or kind of unfair, but I still can’t stop thinking about it?

**Assistant:** Here are some concrete questions you can jot down. I won’t number them, but each line is one question you can answer:

What was the exact behavior or outcome the person seemed to be talking about?  
What part of their message is clearly useful or actionable?  
What emotions did it stir up in me, and why those in particular?  
If I strip the emotion away, what’s the simplest next step I can take?  
Does this feedback match what I know about myself, or is it coming from their preferences or stress?  
Is there anything I need to clarify with them so I’m not guessing?

If the criticism was vague or felt unfair, and it keeps looping in your head, it helps to ground it in reality instead of the imagined version. You can do that by restating it in the plainest, least emotional terms possible, even if that makes it sound almost dull. Often the sting lives in the tone or the ambiguity, not the content. If it’s still bothering you, plan a short follow‑up conversation to ask for specific examples or expectations. Just knowing you have a concrete step to take usually quiets the mental replay.
