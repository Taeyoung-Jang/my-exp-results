# Summary of session-33_2024-06-15

**Main contribution**: Practical advice and a quick‑check mental checklist for answering forced‑choice “preference” items on personality batteries, especially in a [[entities/hospital]]/clinical work context.

## Key Points
- [[concepts/forced-choice-items]] aim to capture *typical* behavior, not ideal or capability. Answer based on what you **actually do most of the time**.  
- Mis‑interpretation often occurs when test‑takers substitute their own definitions for test wording. When the [[entities/psychologist]] clarified the wording, the user's scores “flipped,” indicating a shift from personal to test‑defined meaning rather than pathology.  
- A **10‑second mental checklist** can improve accuracy (see [[concepts/checklist-for-forced-choice]]):
  1. **Timeframe** – “What do I usually do on an average day?”
  2. **Frequency** – Look for words like *usually*, *often*, *more often*.
  3. **Context** – Separate **work** vs **home**; default to the *non‑role* self.
  4. **Ideal vs Actual** – Focus on *actual* behavior, not how you *should* act.
  5. **Literal wording** – Treat *prefer*, *tend to*, *more often* at face value.  
- When items present **two positive traits** (e.g., safety vs speed, empathy vs boundaries), choose the side that **comes first instinctively** before any professional training or self‑monitoring.  
- The guide recommends answering as **“average day”** rather than “at my best.” Average behavior reflects temperament; stress‑related shifts are part of real‑world patterns and should be incorporated if they are consistent.

## Practical Examples (illustrated)
1. **“I usually start tasks right away” vs “I usually wait until I’m ready.”**  
   - Focus on the *usual* pattern on a typical weekday.  
2. **“I prefer working with people” vs “I prefer working independently.”**  
   - Choose the natural pull, not the skill you possess.  
3. **Safety vs Speed**, **Empathy vs Boundaries**, **Autonomy vs Teamwork** – decide by the *first impulse* in everyday, non‑clinical situations.  

By consistently applying the checklist and the “first‑impulse” rule, test‑takers can provide honest, reliable responses without overthinking or test‑gaming.
