**User:** Do you think smartphone apps have gotten too bloated lately, or am I just imagining it? I’m curious what you’d recommend for keeping a phone feeling fast and low-maintenance without losing useful features.

**Assistant:** You’re definitely not imagining it. A lot of apps have become heavier over time, partly because companies keep adding features to keep people engaged, and partly because modern frameworks make development easier but sometimes less efficient.

If you want your phone to feel fast without giving up useful features, a few habits make a big difference. Using lighter alternatives for common apps helps a lot; for example, many social platforms run surprisingly well in a browser instead of their official apps. Keeping background activity in check also matters—turning off autoplay, trimming notification permissions, and stopping apps from running unnecessary processes can free up both battery and storage. And every once in a while, clearing cached data or uninstalling apps you rarely use gives your phone some breathing room.

You shouldn’t have to baby your device, but a little intentional pruning can keep it feeling snappy without sacrificing the essentials.

**User:** Yeah, that matches what I’ve been feeling. I’ve been trying to keep my phone boring lately—just one workspace, no extra downloads. If I need something, I open the browser and do it there so I don’t have to babysit updates. Given that, what are the best things to keep installed as actual apps versus “browser-only,” and are there any settings you’d tweak to make that setup smoother?

**Assistant:** If you’re going for a “boring phone” approach, the things worth keeping as actual apps are usually the ones where the native version is noticeably smoother or more reliable than the web version. Messaging, maps, camera, and anything tied to system‑level features usually fall into that category. Most other things—shopping, social media, reference tools, note dumping, banking, and even email for some people—work perfectly fine in a browser and don’t need to live on your phone full‑time.

To make a browser‑heavy setup feel easier, giving your most-used sites their own home‑screen shortcuts helps a lot, since it makes them feel app-like without taking up space or constantly updating. Turning off autoplay and notification permissions in your browser keeps it from becoming just as noisy as the apps you’re trying to avoid. And if your phone lets you restrict background activity for installed apps, it’s worth tightening that up so only your essential apps can run when they need to.

With that mix—native apps for core tasks, the browser for almost everything else—you usually end up with a fast, quiet phone that doesn’t demand much attention.

**User:** I’m on iPhone. Which core apps would you keep installed in my situation (like maps, messaging, 2FA, etc.) and which categories would you push to Safari home-screen shortcuts instead? Also, what specific iOS settings would you change to cut background stuff and notifications without breaking essentials?

**Assistant:** On an iPhone, the apps worth keeping installed are mostly the ones that tie directly into the system. Messages, Phone, FaceTime, Maps, Camera, Photos, Wallet, and whatever you use for 2FA are the ones that genuinely work better as native apps. I’d also keep whatever you use for email if you check it often, since the share sheet and notifications tend to be more reliable than in a browser. Everything else—social media, shopping, streaming, banking, note dumping, task lists—usually works perfectly well as Safari shortcuts without slowing anything down.

To keep background activity low, the biggest win is going into Settings and turning off Background App Refresh for anything that doesn’t need to stay alive in the background. iOS handles this gracefully, so essentials like Maps and Messages will still behave normally. Notifications are similar: leave them on only for communication, calendar, and anything that must reach you, and turn off banners and badges for the rest. You can also go into each app’s location permission and set almost everything to “While Using the App.” Finally, disabling unnecessary Siri Suggestions and turning off personalized recommendations reduces passive background work without affecting anything important.

With just those tweaks, the phone stays fast and quiet, and you still keep all the features that matter.

**User:** Could you give me a concrete “minimal iPhone” list—like 10–15 apps max—that you’d keep installed, and then a short list of categories you’d always do via Safari shortcuts? Also, where exactly in iOS do I turn off Background App Refresh and trim Siri suggestions (menu path), and any gotchas with 2FA/push notifications if I go browser-first?

**Assistant:** Here’s a simple, realistic “minimal iPhone” setup, kept conversational but still clear.

For the core apps, imagine a phone that only has the things the system truly handles better than the web. Messages, Phone, FaceTime, Photos, Camera, Maps, Wallet, Settings, and the App Store are basically unavoidable. Add your 2FA app, your preferred email app if you actually use it, your music or podcast player if you listen often, and maybe Files or Notes if you rely on iCloud syncing. That usually lands in the ten‑to‑fifteen‑app zone without feeling stripped down.

Almost everything else is cleaner through Safari shortcuts. Social media, shopping, news, banking, and video streaming are the big ones that behave fine in the browser and don’t need to sit on your phone running updates. Anything you do occasionally rather than daily is a good candidate for the browser.

For the settings: Background App Refresh lives in Settings, then General, then Background App Refresh. Turning it off entirely is usually safe, but if you prefer to keep it on for a few things, you can disable it app by app just below that toggle. Siri suggestions are split across a few menus: Settings, then Siri & Search, where you can turn off the suggestion switches at the top, and you can scroll down to each app and disable “Learn from this App” or the suggestions toggles if you want to go deeper.

For 2FA and notifications, the main thing to know is that two‑factor apps and authenticator push notifications won’t work through Safari; you’ll still need the actual 2FA app installed for any login approval that uses push‑based verification. Regular push notifications in general don’t work from web apps on iOS, so anything you truly need to alert you—messaging, banking alerts, delivery updates, calendar reminders—needs to be an installed app. Everything else can stay browser‑only without breaking anything important.

**User:** If I want to be strict about “no apps unless they’re truly essential,” which 2FA route is least annoying on iPhone—Authenticator app, SMS, hardware key, or passkeys—and what would you personally pick for a browser-first setup?

**Assistant:** If you want to stay strict about keeping almost nothing installed, passkeys are the least annoying on an iPhone. They use the system’s built‑in iCloud Keychain, so there’s no extra app to maintain, and they work smoothly in Safari without any extra steps. When a site supports them, you just unlock with Face ID and you’re in.

For the rest, a hardware key is the next cleanest option because it doesn’t require an app and doesn’t generate ongoing notifications. It’s just a physical tap when you need it. The downside is having to keep the key with you, but it fits the “minimal apps” philosophy.

Authenticator apps are fine but break your strict rule, since you’d need to keep one installed. SMS works everywhere and doesn’t need an app, but it’s the weakest security option and can be annoying if you get lots of codes.

For a browser‑first setup, I’d go passkeys wherever possible and fall back to a hardware key for anything that doesn’t support them yet. That combo keeps your phone clean, secure, and low‑maintenance.
