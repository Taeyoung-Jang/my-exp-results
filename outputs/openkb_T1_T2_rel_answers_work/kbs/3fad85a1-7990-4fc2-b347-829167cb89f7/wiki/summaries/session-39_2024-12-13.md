## Summary

This document offers a practical approach for evaluating whether shifting to an earlier work start time improves productivity without causing fatigue. It outlines a short‑term **self‑experiment** and provides a quick **daily log template** to capture relevant signals.

### Key Concepts
- **Self‑experiment** – run a low‑stakes two‑week trial of an earlier start while keeping other variables constant. [[concepts/self-experiment]]
- **Productivity metrics** – track sleep quality, wake‑up feeling, early‑day focus, midday energy dips, afternoon stamina, meeting effectiveness, overall stress, and the perception of the routine as natural or forced. [[concepts/productivity-metrics]]
- **Daily log template** – a one‑line, fill‑in‑the‑blank format that can be pasted into any notes app:
  
  `SleepQ _ /5 | Wake _ /5 | AM Focus _ /5 | Midday Dip Y/N | PM Stamina _ /5 | Early Mtgs _ /5 | Stress _ /5 | Natural/Forced: _`

- **Rating scale** – use a 1–5 Likert scale for quantitative items; simple yes/no for midday dip.
- **Trend interpretation rule** – after two weeks, if AM focus, early‑meeting effectiveness, and “natural vs forced” entries trend upward or stay consistently positive while stress and energy dips do not increase, keep the earlier start. Conversely, declining focus, more dips, rising stress, or frequent “forced” entries suggest reverting to the previous schedule. [[concepts/timing-experiment]]

### Practical Steps
1. **Set up the experiment**: shift start time earlier for two weeks, maintain normal work tasks.
2. **Log each day** using the one‑line template, filling in the placeholders in under a minute.
3. **Review** the collected data at the end of the period, looking for upward trends in the positive indicators and stability in stress/dip metrics.
4. **Decide** based on the trend rule whether to adopt the new schedule permanently.

### Benefits
- Minimal time investment for data collection.
- Objective, quantifiable signals to inform the decision.
- Flexible enough to adapt to individual preferences and job demands.
