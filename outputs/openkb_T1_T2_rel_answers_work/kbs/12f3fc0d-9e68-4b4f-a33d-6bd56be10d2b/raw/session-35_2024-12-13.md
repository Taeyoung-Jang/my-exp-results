**User:** I’m trying to figure out what to read next, and I’ve been on a big nonfiction kick lately—mostly biographies and history—so that’s what I’ve been checking out from the library. Can you recommend a few really absorbing picks in that lane (preferably stuff that reads like a story), and maybe tell me which ones are good on audiobook too?

**Assistant:** If you like nonfiction that moves with the energy of a novel, a few titles consistently hook people.

One great pick is *The Splendid and the Vile* by Erik Larson. It follows Churchill during the Blitz but reads like a tightly plotted drama. The audiobook is excellent—narrator John Lee has the perfect tone for it.

Another absorbing one is *Steve Jobs* by Walter Isaacson. Even if you already know the broad strokes, the personal detail and momentum make it feel like a character‑driven story. The audiobook works well because it’s cleanly narrated and easy to follow.

If you want something with a more adventurous bend, *Endurance* by Alfred Lansing—about Shackleton’s Antarctic expedition—almost feels made up because it’s so wild. The audiobook is strong and adds to the sense of isolation and danger.

And for a U.S. history angle, *Say Nothing* by Patrick Radden Keefe is incredibly gripping. It unpacks the Troubles in Northern Ireland with the pacing of a mystery. The audio version is very good and keeps the tension intact.

If you want recs tied to a certain time period or type of person (political figures, artists, science, etc.), I can narrow it further.

**Assistant:** Since you loved *The Splendid and the Vile*, I’d go to *Say Nothing* next. It has that same feeling of being dropped into a specific moment in history and watching it unfold almost like a thriller. The storytelling is tight, the stakes are high, and it has that “just one more chapter” pull. The audiobook keeps the tension really well, so it’s a strong pick if you like listening.

For microhistory with a similar immersive feel, you might like *The Ghost Map* by Steven Johnson. It follows the 1854 cholera outbreak in London and the detective‑work that finally revealed how the disease spread. It sounds like it would be dry, but it moves with a surprising amount of momentum and has the same sense of being right there in the streets.

Another good one is *Midnight in Chernobyl* by Adam Higginbotham. It zeroes in on the lead‑up to the explosion and the immediate aftermath, but it unfolds so cinematically that it almost feels like watching the event in real time. The audio edition is excellent if you prefer that format.

If you want something lighter or set in a different era, I can steer you in another direction too.

**User:** Could you give me a few more microhistory-style picks like those—ideally ones that focus on a single disaster, trial, expedition, or political moment—and flag which are especially good on audiobook? I’d also love one or two that lean more biography but still stay tightly centered on a specific period/event.

**Assistant:** Sure—here are a few more that hit that same “one moment in time” intensity, all in a more story‑driven, immersive style.

If you like big historical turning points, *Five Days at Memorial* by Sheri Fink is outstanding. It follows what happened inside a New Orleans hospital during Hurricane Katrina, and it reads like a tightly wound medical thriller. The audiobook is very strong because the narration keeps the tension without melodrama.

For something more investigative, *The Looming Tower* by Lawrence Wright zooms in on the run‑up to 9/11 and the personalities, rivalries, and missed signals that shaped the moment. It moves like a political and intelligence drama. The audio version is excellent—steady, clear, and easy to stay immersed in.

If you want an expedition story, *The Lost City of Z* by David Grann is perfect. It tracks Percy Fawcett’s final Amazon expedition and Grann’s attempt to retrace it. It has that mix of adventure, mystery, and atmospheric detail that makes it feel novelistic. The audiobook works well because the narrator captures both timelines smoothly.

For a disaster with strong narrative drive, *A Night to Remember* by Walter Lord is still one of the most gripping Titanic accounts ever written. It moves fast, almost minute‑by‑minute. The audiobook is short, cleanly narrated, and very easy to binge.

For something that leans more toward biography but still stays tightly focused on one concentrated window of time, *Clementine: The Life of Mrs. Winston Churchill* by Sonia Purnell works beautifully if you’re coming off a Churchill mood. It doesn’t cover her whole life evenly; it really locks in on the pressure‑cooker war years. The audiobook is good, especially if you like British narration.

Another great option is *The River of Doubt* by Candice Millard, which zeroes in on Theodore Roosevelt’s near‑fatal Amazon expedition. It’s basically a survival narrative wrapped around a political figure. The audio is particularly gripping because the narrator leans into the suspense without overacting.

If you want more in a certain flavor—heavier history, true-crime edge, political intrigue, or pure adventure—I can tune the recommendations further.
